# Chapter 2: Literature Review and Hypotheses

## Introduction

Government procurement occupies a distinctive position at the intersection of public administration, economics, and law. Each year, federal, state, and local governments in the United States spend trillions of dollars acquiring goods and services from the private sector, making public procurement one of the most consequential—and most contested—domains of governmental activity (Thai, 2001; Tkachenko et al., 2023). The method by which governments select contractors has profound implications for public value: it shapes the quality of services citizens receive, the cost borne by taxpayers, the fairness of market access for firms, and the integrity and legitimacy of government itself (Moore, 1995; Kelman, 2005). Despite these stakes, the empirical literature comparing source selection strategies—particularly the choice between lowest-price technically acceptable (LPTA) and best-value tradeoff approaches—remains remarkably thin, fragmented across disciplines, and disproportionately concentrated in defense acquisition contexts (Osman et al., 2017; Watson, 2015).

This chapter provides a comprehensive, integrative review of the theoretical and empirical literatures relevant to the central research question of this dissertation: *Does adopting best-value source selection strategies, as opposed to lowest-price technically acceptable methods, improve measurable public value outcomes in government procurement?* The review is organized into ten sections that move from foundational concepts to specific empirical evidence and ultimately to the conceptual model and formal hypotheses that guide the empirical investigation. Section 2.1 traces the historical and regulatory foundations of lowest-price norms and the emergence of the best-value continuum in federal acquisition. Section 2.2 introduces public value theory and value-for-money frameworks as the outcome lenses through which procurement results should be evaluated. Section 2.3 examines the economic theory underlying mechanism choice in procurement—auction theory, transaction cost economics, and contract incompleteness—establishing the theoretical rationale for why price-only mechanisms are suboptimal under conditions of complexity and uncertainty. Section 2.4 surveys the limited but growing empirical literature on the relationship between award strategies and procurement outcomes. Sections 2.5 through 2.9 address five critical mediating and moderating domains: bid protests and accountability (2.5), solicitation quality and requirements clarity (2.6), oversight layers and the fraud-protection tradeoff (2.7), renegotiation and strategic underbidding (2.8), and incumbency advantage and competition suppression (2.9). Section 2.10 synthesizes the preceding material into a conceptual model and presents ten formal hypotheses, distinguishing those testable with archival data from those requiring future survey or institutional-comparison designs.

Throughout, the review adopts an integrative and critical stance. Rather than merely cataloging individual studies, each section identifies points of theoretical convergence and divergence, assesses the strength and generalizability of the evidence, and highlights the specific gaps that this dissertation seeks to address. The goal is to demonstrate that the question of source selection strategy is not merely a procedural or regulatory matter but a fundamental question of institutional design with measurable consequences for public welfare.

## 2.1 Lowest-Price Norms and the Best-Value Continuum

### 2.1.1 Historical Roots of Lowest-Price Procurement

The preference for awarding government contracts to the lowest bidder has deep historical roots in Anglo-American public administration. In the United States, the requirement to award contracts based on sealed competitive bids can be traced to the early nineteenth century, when Congress enacted statutes mandating competitive bidding for military supplies and public works to combat patronage, corruption, and fiscal profligacy (Nagle, 1999; Schooner, 2001). The sealed-bid system rested on a compelling logic: by requiring agencies to specify their needs precisely and to accept the lowest responsive, responsible bid, Congress could constrain bureaucratic discretion, reduce opportunities for favoritism, and assure the public that taxpayer funds were being spent efficiently (Kelman, 1990; Schooner, 2001). This logic was deeply embedded in Progressive Era governance ideals that equated good government with rule-bound administration, minimal discretion, and visible accountability mechanisms (Kelman, 2005).

The Competition in Contracting Act (CICA) of 1984 codified full and open competition as the foundational principle of federal procurement, establishing a strong presumption in favor of competitive procedures and requiring agencies to justify any departure from competitive acquisition (Cibinic et al., 2015; Manuel, 2011). CICA reinforced the normative architecture in which competition—and by extension, price competition—served as the primary safeguard of the public interest. The Act reflected a bipartisan consensus that competitive markets, when properly structured, would drive costs down and quality up (Yukins & Schooner, 2007).

Yet even as CICA enshrined competition as the cornerstone of federal procurement, practitioners and scholars recognized that price competition alone was insufficient for complex acquisitions. The Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act (FASA) of 1994 and the Federal Acquisition Reform Act (FARA, also known as the Clinger-Cohen Act) of 1996 introduced important reforms that expanded the use of negotiated procurements and best-value source selection methods (Kelman, 2005; Manuel, 2011). These reforms reflected growing frustration with the rigidity of sealed-bid processes and their inability to accommodate the government's increasing reliance on complex services, information technology, and professional expertise—domains where specifying requirements precisely in advance was often impossible (Kelman, 1990, 2005).

### 2.1.2 The FAR Part 15 Framework: LPTA and Tradeoff

The current regulatory architecture governing source selection in federal procurement is primarily found in Part 15 of the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), which establishes two principal best-value approaches. Under FAR 15.101-2, the lowest-price technically acceptable (LPTA) method requires agencies to establish minimum technical standards; proposals that meet those standards are then evaluated solely on price, with the award going to the lowest-priced technically acceptable offer. Under FAR 15.101-1, the tradeoff process permits agencies to evaluate proposals on multiple factors—including technical approach, past performance, management capability, and other non-price dimensions—and to make award decisions that trade off higher price for superior quality when doing so is in the government's interest (FAR, 2024; Defense Acquisition University [DAU], 2023).

The distinction between LPTA and tradeoff is not merely procedural; it reflects fundamentally different assumptions about the nature of the goods or services being acquired and the role of the procurement process in creating value. LPTA assumes that requirements can be fully specified in advance, that quality above the minimum threshold does not add meaningful value, and that price is the dominant differentiator among qualified offerors (Congressional Research Service [CRS], 2024; Ban, 2015). Tradeoff, by contrast, assumes that quality varies meaningfully among offerors, that higher quality can generate value that exceeds the incremental cost, and that the government benefits from the exercise of informed judgment in weighing price against non-price factors (Baker, 2016; DAU, 2023).

The FAR does not express a blanket preference for one method over the other but provides guidance on the circumstances favoring each approach. LPTA is considered appropriate when requirements are well-defined, when the risk of unsuccessful performance is minimal, and when additional technical superiority offers no meaningful incremental benefit to the government (FAR 15.101-2). Tradeoff is favored when requirements are complex or difficult to define precisely, when the consequences of poor performance are significant, and when there is meaningful variation in the quality that different offerors can provide (FAR 15.101-1; CRS, 2024).

### 2.1.3 When LPTA Creates Adverse Selection

Despite the regulatory guidance, the choice between LPTA and tradeoff has been contested terrain. Critics of LPTA have argued that its use has expanded well beyond commodity-like acquisitions into domains where quality matters significantly—including professional services, information technology, and complex systems—creating a mismatch between the procurement mechanism and the nature of the acquisition (Ban, 2015; Baker, 2016; National Defense Industrial Association [NDIA], 2014). This mismatch can trigger adverse selection: when only price is evaluated above a minimum threshold, firms that invest in quality, experienced personnel, innovative approaches, and robust management systems are disadvantaged relative to firms that strip costs to the bone, potentially compromising quality, underbidding strategically, or employing less experienced staff (Akerlof, 1970; Ban, 2015).

The adverse selection problem in LPTA procurements is a specific manifestation of the market-for-lemons dynamic identified by Akerlof (1970). When buyers cannot observe quality directly and rely on price as the selection criterion, high-quality sellers are driven from the market because they cannot recover the costs of quality production. In the procurement context, this dynamic manifests as experienced contractors declining to compete on LPTA solicitations, leaving the government with a smaller pool of lower-quality offerors (NDIA, 2014; Professional Services Council [PSC], 2014). Industry surveys have documented widespread contractor reluctance to pursue LPTA opportunities for complex services, with firms reporting that the cost of preparing proposals cannot be justified when the sole selection criterion is price (PSC, 2014; NDIA, 2014).

Congressional concern over LPTA overuse led to several legislative interventions. Section 813 of the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) for Fiscal Year 2017 required the Department of Defense (DoD) to establish criteria and guidance for the appropriate use of LPTA, including a presumption against its use for certain categories of acquisitions such as knowledge-based professional services, cybersecurity, and systems with significant life-cycle costs (CRS, 2024; Public Law 114-328). The NDAA for FY 2018 further restricted LPTA use, requiring DoD contracting officers to avoid LPTA when it would deny the government the ability to consider the past performance of offerors or when the technical approach or quality of work could significantly affect outcomes (Public Law 115-91). These legislative actions reflected a growing bipartisan consensus that LPTA, while appropriate for commodity purchases, was being misapplied in contexts where quality variation among offerors was both significant and consequential.

### 2.1.4 The Best-Value Spectrum

It is important to recognize that LPTA and full tradeoff are not a binary dichotomy but rather endpoints on a continuum of source selection approaches. Between these poles lie a variety of intermediate mechanisms, including price-technical tradeoffs with specified weighting schemes, oral presentations combined with written technical proposals, tiered evaluation approaches that first screen for technical acceptability and then conduct tradeoff analysis among qualified offerors, and advisory multi-step processes (DAU, 2023; Cibinic et al., 2015). Some agencies have experimented with lowest-price technically rated approaches that create bands of technical ratings rather than simple pass/fail determinations (Gordon, 2006).

At the state and local level, the continuum is even more varied. While many states have adopted procurement frameworks modeled on the American Bar Association's Model Procurement Code or the National Association of State Procurement Officials (NASPO) guidelines, there is substantial variation in the extent to which state procurement laws permit or encourage best-value considerations (NASPO, 2019; National Institute of Governmental Purchasing [NIGP], 2016). Some states continue to mandate lowest-bid awards for broad categories of procurement, while others have enacted legislation permitting or requiring best-value evaluations for complex acquisitions (NIGP, 2016; Prier & McCue, 2009). This cross-jurisdictional variation creates a natural laboratory for studying the effects of source selection strategy on procurement outcomes—an opportunity that the empirical literature has not yet fully exploited and that this dissertation seeks to leverage.

### 2.1.5 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

The historical and regulatory analysis reveals a fundamental tension in public procurement: the desire to constrain discretion and prevent corruption through price-based competition versus the need to exercise informed judgment in selecting contractors for complex acquisitions. This tension is not merely a matter of regulatory design; it reflects a deeper question about the nature of value in public procurement. If value is defined solely as lowest cost, then LPTA is the appropriate mechanism. If value encompasses quality, performance, timeliness, innovation, and lifecycle cost, then the procurement mechanism must be capable of evaluating and weighting these dimensions. The next section introduces the theoretical frameworks—public value theory and value-for-money—that provide the conceptual foundation for a richer understanding of procurement outcomes.

## 2.2 Public Value and Value for Money as Outcome Frameworks

### 2.2.1 Moore's Public Value Theory

Mark Moore's (1995) *Creating Public Value: Strategic Management in Government* introduced a conceptual framework that has become one of the most influential paradigms in public administration. Moore argued that the purpose of public management is to create "public value"—value that is authorized through democratic processes, generated through operationally effective public organizations, and legitimated through accountability to the citizenry. Unlike private-sector value creation, which is measured primarily through financial returns to shareholders, public value is inherently multidimensional, encompassing the quality and effectiveness of public services, the fairness and equity of their distribution, the trustworthiness and legitimacy of governmental institutions, and the degree to which governmental action enables broader societal welfare (Moore, 1995, 2013).

Moore's strategic triangle identifies three conditions necessary for public value creation: (a) the proposed activity must be substantively valuable—it must produce outcomes that citizens and their representatives regard as worthwhile; (b) it must be politically legitimate and sustainable—it must have the support of key authorizers in the political environment; and (c) it must be operationally and administratively feasible—the public organization must have the capacity to deliver the desired outcomes (Moore, 1995). Applied to procurement, this framework suggests that the value of a procurement strategy should be evaluated not merely on the price achieved but on the quality and effectiveness of the goods and services obtained (substantive value), the fairness and transparency of the acquisition process (legitimacy), and the capacity of the government to manage the resulting contract effectively (operational feasibility).

Benington (2011) extended Moore's framework, arguing that public value should be understood as encompassing ecological, political, economic, social, and cultural dimensions. In the procurement context, this broader conception suggests that the impact of acquisition strategies extends beyond immediate contract outcomes to include effects on market competition, small business participation, workforce development, innovation, and community economic development—dimensions that lowest-price mechanisms systematically undervalue (Benington & Moore, 2011).

### 2.2.2 Value for Money: Lifecycle and Risk-Adjusted Assessment

The value-for-money (VfM) concept provides a more operationally specific framework for evaluating procurement outcomes. Originating primarily in the United Kingdom's public financial management tradition and adopted by international development institutions, VfM is defined as the optimum combination of whole-life cost and quality (or fitness for purpose) to meet the user's requirement (HM Treasury, 2006; OECD, 2019). Critically, VfM is not synonymous with lowest price; rather, it requires consideration of the total cost of ownership—including acquisition cost, operating and maintenance costs, disposal costs, and the costs of poor quality or contract failure—relative to the benefits delivered over the entire lifecycle of the acquisition (Asian Development Bank [ADB], 2018; European Bank for Reconstruction and Development [EBRD], 2024).

The OECD's Recommendation on Public Procurement (2015) identifies VfM as one of the core principles guiding public procurement systems, alongside transparency, integrity, and access. The OECD framework emphasizes that VfM assessment should consider not only direct monetary costs but also "value associated with quality, skills, experience, knowledge, and innovation" (OECD, 2019, p. 12). This articulation aligns with the best-value tradeoff concept in the FAR, suggesting a convergence between the international VfM framework and the U.S. regulatory approach to source selection.

The ADB (2018) and EBRD (2024) have developed more detailed VfM assessment methodologies that incorporate risk adjustment, recognizing that the expected cost of a procurement decision depends not only on the contract price but also on the probability and magnitude of adverse outcomes such as cost overruns, schedule delays, performance failures, and contract terminations. Under these frameworks, a low initial price that carries a high probability of cost growth or performance failure may represent poor value for money compared to a higher initial price with more predictable lifecycle costs and outcomes (ADB, 2018; Burger & Hawkesworth, 2011).

### 2.2.3 The Measurement Challenge

A significant obstacle to the empirical study of public value in procurement is the multidimensional nature of the concept and the difficulty of operationalizing it through measurable indicators. Unlike price, which is unidimensional and readily observed, public value encompasses dimensions that are qualitatively distinct, that may be partially incommensurable, and that often manifest over extended time horizons (Bryson et al., 2014; Hartley et al., 2017). Quality of service delivery, equity of access, innovation, transparency, accountability, and public trust are all dimensions of public value that resist simple quantification (Alford & O'Flynn, 2009).

Wylie (2021) provided one of the more systematic attempts to operationalize public value in the procurement context, developing a multi-criteria framework that includes dimensions such as cost efficiency, quality outcomes, social value, environmental sustainability, and innovation. Malacina et al. (2022) conducted a systematic literature review on public procurement and value creation, identifying a fragmented landscape in which different studies emphasize different dimensions of value without converging on a shared measurement framework. Their review found that while the theoretical case for multidimensional value assessment in procurement is well-established, empirical studies overwhelmingly rely on cost-based outcome measures, with non-price dimensions receiving comparatively little systematic attention.

### 2.2.4 Operationalizing Public Value Through Measurable Indicators

For the purposes of this dissertation, public value is operationalized through a set of measurable outcome indicators that can be constructed from archival procurement data. Drawing on the theoretical frameworks of Moore (1995) and VfM (OECD, 2019; ADB, 2018), and guided by the measurement approaches used in prior procurement research (Osman et al., 2017; Watson, 2015; Rendon, 2009), five primary outcome dimensions are identified:

1. **Cost growth**: The percentage difference between the initial award value and the total realized contract value (including modifications), measured over the contract lifecycle. High cost growth indicates poor value for money and suggests inadequate initial pricing, scope instability, or strategic underbidding (Bajari et al., 2014; Ganuza, 2007).

2. **Schedule performance (timeliness)**: The degree to which contract deliverables are completed within the originally specified timeframe. Schedule delays impose costs on the government in the form of deferred capabilities, extended oversight, and potential need for interim solutions (Rendon, 2009; DAU, 2023).

3. **Performance quality**: Where available, assessed through Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS) ratings, which evaluate contractors on dimensions including quality, schedule, cost control, management, and regulatory compliance (Watson, 2015; Federal Acquisition Regulation 42.1502).

4. **Protest incidence**: The rate at which procurement actions result in bid protests, measured as a proportion of total contract actions. While protests serve an important accountability function (discussed in Section 2.5), high protest rates may also indicate process quality problems, ambiguous evaluation criteria, or loss of stakeholder confidence in the procurement process (GAO, 2025; Schwartz & Manuel, 2018).

5. **Service continuity**: The degree to which contracts proceed to completion without termination for default or termination for convenience due to contractor nonperformance. Contract termination represents a significant failure in value delivery and imposes substantial costs on the government in the form of recompetition, transition, and capability gaps (Cibinic et al., 2015).

These five dimensions, while not exhaustive of the public value concept, provide a tractable and empirically grounded set of outcome measures that capture the core dimensions of VfM—cost efficiency, timeliness, quality, process integrity, and reliability of service delivery—and that can be constructed from available federal procurement datasets. The construction of a composite public value index from these dimensions is described in Chapter 3 (Methodology).

### 2.2.5 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

Public value theory and VfM frameworks provide the outcome lens through which the effects of source selection strategy should be evaluated. By moving beyond simple price comparisons to consider lifecycle cost, quality, timeliness, process integrity, and service reliability, these frameworks align the evaluation of procurement strategy with the broader purposes of government. The next section turns to the economic theory underlying the choice of procurement mechanism, establishing why the theoretical expectation is that price-only mechanisms will produce inferior outcomes when the goods or services being acquired are complex, difficult to specify, or subject to significant performance variation.

## 2.3 Procurement Economics, Mechanism Choice, and Contract Incompleteness

### 2.3.1 Auction Theory Fundamentals

The economic analysis of procurement begins with auction theory, the branch of microeconomics and mechanism design that studies how competitive bidding processes allocate goods and services (Krishna, 2010; Milgrom, 2004). In the simplest procurement setting—the first-price sealed-bid auction—the buyer specifies a good or service, invited sellers submit sealed bids, and the contract is awarded to the lowest bidder. Under the assumptions of the independent private values (IPV) model, in which each bidder knows its own cost of performance but is uncertain about competitors' costs, the first-price sealed-bid auction produces efficient outcomes: the contract is awarded to the lowest-cost supplier, and the winning bid reflects the competitive pressure of the market (Vickrey, 1961; Riley & Samuelson, 1981).

However, the efficiency properties of the simple auction model depend on assumptions that are often violated in government procurement. First, the model assumes that the good or service being procured can be fully and precisely specified, so that all bids are for an identical product. In practice, many government acquisitions involve complex services, customized systems, or professional expertise for which the precise scope and quality of deliverables cannot be fully specified in advance (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001). Second, the model assumes that bidders can accurately estimate their costs of performance. For complex acquisitions, cost estimation is inherently uncertain, and bidders may strategically shade their bids below expected costs in hopes of recovering through post-award modifications (Jung et al., 2013). Third, the model assumes that the buyer can perfectly observe and verify the quality of the delivered goods or services, enabling enforcement of the contract terms. When quality is difficult to observe or verify, price-only auctions create incentives for winners to cut quality to improve margins—the classic quality-shading problem (Che, 1993; Asker & Cantillon, 2008).

These limitations have motivated the development of scoring auctions, in which the buyer evaluates bids on both price and quality dimensions, using a scoring rule that assigns weights to each dimension (Che, 1993; Asker & Cantillon, 2010). Scoring auctions are the economic analog of the FAR Part 15 tradeoff process. Theoretical analysis has shown that scoring auctions can improve buyer welfare relative to price-only auctions when quality varies meaningfully among suppliers and when the buyer can specify and evaluate quality dimensions with reasonable accuracy (Che, 1993; Branco, 1997; Hanazono & Nakabayashi, 2017). The scoring auction literature thus provides a theoretical foundation for the prediction that best-value tradeoff methods will outperform LPTA in contexts where quality variation is significant and quality can be evaluated.

### 2.3.2 Transaction Cost Economics

Oliver Williamson's (1975, 1985, 1996) transaction cost economics (TCE) provides a complementary theoretical framework for understanding the choice of procurement mechanism. TCE posits that economic transactions are characterized by three dimensions—asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency—and that the optimal governance structure (market, hybrid, or hierarchy) depends on the configuration of these dimensions (Williamson, 1985). In the procurement context, asset specificity refers to the degree to which the goods, services, or investments required for contract performance are specialized to the particular transaction and cannot be redeployed to alternative uses without significant loss of value. Uncertainty refers to the difficulty of specifying, monitoring, and verifying contract performance. Frequency refers to the regularity with which the transaction recurs.

Williamson's framework predicts that as asset specificity and uncertainty increase, the costs of market-based governance—of which competitive price-based procurement is a form—rise relative to the costs of more relational or hierarchical governance mechanisms (Williamson, 1985, 1996). In highly specific and uncertain transactions, the winner of a price-only auction may find that the contractual terms are inadequate to govern the relationship, leading to costly renegotiation, disputes, and opportunistic behavior. This prediction is directly relevant to the LPTA vs. tradeoff debate: LPTA, as a form of market governance that relies heavily on price competition, is likely to perform well for transactions with low asset specificity and uncertainty (commodity-like purchases) but poorly for transactions with high specificity and uncertainty (complex services, IT systems, professional expertise) (Tadelis, 2012; Brown & Potoski, 2003).

Brown and Potoski (2003, 2005) applied TCE to the study of government contracting decisions, finding that asset specificity and measurement difficulty were significant predictors of the mode of service delivery (in-house production vs. contracting out) and the type of contract used. Their analysis supported Williamson's prediction that transactions characterized by high asset specificity and measurement difficulty were more likely to involve relational contracting mechanisms—analogous to best-value tradeoff processes—rather than pure price competition. Levin and Tadelis (2010) provided further evidence that local governments' decisions to contract out services were influenced by the difficulty of specifying and monitoring quality, with services that were harder to measure being less likely to be contracted out through simple price-based mechanisms.

### 2.3.3 Contract Incompleteness

The theory of incomplete contracts, developed by Hart and Moore (1988), Grossman and Hart (1986), and Hart (1995), provides additional theoretical depth to the analysis of procurement mechanism choice. Incomplete contract theory begins from the observation that many economically important transactions cannot be fully specified in a contract because the relevant contingencies are too numerous, complex, or unforeseeable to enumerate and price ex ante (Hart & Moore, 1988). When contracts are incomplete, the allocation of residual control rights—the right to make decisions in circumstances not covered by the contract—becomes a central determinant of the efficiency of the transaction (Hart, 1995).

In the procurement context, contract incompleteness has direct implications for the choice between LPTA and tradeoff. When the scope of work can be fully specified and all relevant contingencies can be anticipated, a complete contract can be written and enforced, and price competition among qualified bidders is an efficient allocation mechanism. But when the scope is complex, requirements are evolving, and contingencies are difficult to foresee—conditions that characterize many government service and IT acquisitions—the contract is necessarily incomplete, and the initial award price is a poor predictor of the total cost and value of the transaction (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Tadelis, 2012).

Bajari and Tadelis (2001) developed a formal model that explicitly addresses the tradeoff between competitive bidding (auctions) and negotiation in procurement, incorporating contract incompleteness as a central feature. Their model shows that when projects are relatively straightforward and can be well-specified, competitive bidding is the preferred mechanism because it exploits competitive pressure to drive down costs. However, as project complexity increases and contracts become more incomplete, competitive bidding becomes less efficient because the winning bidder has weak incentives to adapt to unforeseen circumstances—the rigid contract terms that make price competition work also make post-award adaptation costly and contentious. In these circumstances, negotiation—which allows the buyer to select a contractor based on quality and capability rather than price alone and to build relational governance mechanisms that facilitate adaptation—produces superior outcomes.

### 2.3.4 Bajari, McMillan, and Tadelis (2009): Empirical Analysis

Bajari, McMillan, and Tadelis (2009) provided important empirical evidence testing the predictions of the Bajari and Tadelis (2001) theoretical model. Using data on private-sector building construction contracts in Northern California, they analyzed the determinants of the choice between competitive bidding and negotiation and the consequences of this choice for project outcomes. Consistent with the theoretical predictions, they found that projects with greater complexity, uncertainty, and the potential for design changes were more likely to be procured through negotiation rather than competitive bidding. Moreover, they found that the use of competitive bidding for complex projects was associated with higher rates of cost overruns and ex post renegotiation—evidence that price-only selection mechanisms perform poorly when contract incompleteness is significant.

While the Bajari et al. (2009) study examined private-sector construction rather than government procurement, its findings are highly relevant to the public procurement context. Government agencies face even more acute contract incompleteness problems than private-sector buyers because of the additional constraints imposed by regulation, the difficulty of exercising the kind of informal relationship management that private buyers use to manage incomplete contracts, and the political and legal consequences of contract failures (Kelman, 2002; Brown & Potoski, 2003). The implication is that the negative consequences of price-only mechanisms documented by Bajari et al. (2009) may be even more severe in the government context.

### 2.3.5 Principal-Agent Problems in Government Procurement

The principal-agent framework adds a further layer of complexity to the analysis of procurement mechanism choice (Laffont & Tirole, 1993; Ross, 1973). In government procurement, the principal-agent relationship is multi-layered: taxpayers are the ultimate principals, but their interests are mediated through elected officials, agency heads, procurement officials, contracting officers, and contract administrators, each of whom may have partially divergent objectives, information sets, and incentive structures (Miller, 2005; Kelman, 2005). The contractor, as the agent, has private information about its costs, capabilities, and intended effort level that the government, as principal, cannot fully observe (Laffont & Tirole, 1993).

Laffont and Tirole (1993) developed the canonical model of procurement under asymmetric information, showing that the optimal procurement mechanism involves a tradeoff between two goals: extracting cost efficiency through competitive pressure (rent extraction) and providing incentives for the contractor to invest in quality and effort (incentive provision). In their framework, the optimal contract generally involves elements of both fixed-price and cost-reimbursement, with the specific mix depending on the degree of uncertainty, the observability of effort, and the risk preferences of the parties. The model implies that pure price competition (as in LPTA) is optimal only when cost uncertainty is low and quality is perfectly observable—conditions that are often not met for complex procurements.

Tirole (1986) and Laffont and Tirole (1991) further analyzed the problem of contractor cost-padding and moral hazard, showing that when the government cannot perfectly monitor contractor effort, fixed-price contracts—which are the dominant contract type under LPTA—create incentives for quality shading. The contractor, having won the contract by bidding the lowest price, has a strong incentive to minimize costs of performance, potentially at the expense of quality, unless the government can effectively monitor and enforce quality standards. This theoretical prediction aligns with the empirical observation that LPTA procurements are associated with higher rates of quality problems and performance shortfalls (Watson, 2015; Baker, 2016).

### 2.3.6 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

The economic theory of procurement mechanism choice provides a strong theoretical foundation for the prediction that LPTA will produce inferior outcomes relative to tradeoff for complex, uncertain, and difficult-to-specify acquisitions. Auction theory, TCE, incomplete contract theory, and principal-agent models converge on the conclusion that price-only mechanisms work well for simple, well-defined transactions but create adverse selection, moral hazard, and costly renegotiation when applied to complex procurements. This theoretical convergence motivates the core hypothesis of this dissertation—that best-value tradeoff methods produce higher public value than LPTA—while also specifying the moderating conditions under which the difference should be most pronounced. The next section reviews the empirical evidence directly addressing the relationship between source selection strategy and procurement outcomes.

## 2.4 Empirical Evidence on Award Strategies, Lead Time, and Outcomes

### 2.4.1 Overview of the Empirical Literature

Despite the theoretical richness of the procurement mechanism choice literature, empirical studies directly comparing the outcomes of LPTA and tradeoff source selection methods in government procurement remain relatively scarce. The existing literature is concentrated primarily in the defense acquisition domain, conducted largely at the Naval Postgraduate School and other defense-affiliated institutions, and often relies on small samples, case studies, or descriptive comparisons rather than rigorous quasi-experimental designs (Osman et al., 2017; Watson, 2015; Baker, 2016). Civilian agency procurement has received far less empirical attention, creating a significant gap that this dissertation addresses.

Several factors contribute to the thinness of the empirical literature. First, procurement data are dispersed across multiple systems (FPDS-NG, USAspending.gov, CPARS, individual agency databases) that are difficult to integrate and that often lack the granular variables needed to distinguish LPTA from tradeoff awards or to measure post-award outcomes (Tkachenko et al., 2023). Second, the choice of source selection method is endogenous—agencies select LPTA or tradeoff based on the characteristics of the acquisition—creating identification challenges that require careful econometric treatment (Bajari et al., 2009). Third, many relevant outcomes (e.g., performance quality, user satisfaction) are not systematically recorded in available databases or are recorded with significant measurement error (Rendon, 2009).

Despite these challenges, the existing literature provides valuable evidence that informs the hypotheses of this dissertation. The following subsections review the key empirical studies.

### 2.4.2 Osman, Gomez, and Gunderson (2017)

Osman, Gomez, and Gunderson (2017) conducted one of the more systematic analyses of source selection method and procurement outcomes, using data on DoD contracts awarded between 2008 and 2015. Their study examined the relationship between the source selection method (LPTA vs. tradeoff vs. lowest price) and several outcome variables, including contract cost growth, schedule performance, and the incidence of post-award modifications. Using a sample of over 1,000 contracts across multiple defense agencies, they found that LPTA contracts exhibited higher rates of cost growth and schedule delays compared to tradeoff contracts, even after controlling for contract type, dollar value, and product/service category.

Notably, Osman et al. (2017) found that the difference in outcomes was most pronounced for service contracts and for contracts in product/service categories characterized by high technical complexity. For simpler, commodity-like acquisitions, the differences between LPTA and tradeoff outcomes were smaller and often not statistically significant. This pattern is consistent with the theoretical predictions of Bajari and Tadelis (2001) and Williamson (1985): the superiority of tradeoff methods is contingent on the complexity of the acquisition and the degree of contract incompleteness.

However, Osman et al. (2017) also documented that LPTA contracts had shorter procurement administrative lead times (PALT)—the time from solicitation to award—than tradeoff contracts. This finding reflects the simpler evaluation process under LPTA, which requires only a pass/fail technical assessment rather than a comparative evaluation of multiple factors. The PALT advantage of LPTA creates a genuine tradeoff for agencies: faster awards but potentially worse outcomes. The efficiency of the procurement process itself has value—delayed acquisitions impose costs on the government in the form of capability gaps, extended reliance on interim solutions, and bureaucratic resources devoted to the ongoing procurement—and this speed advantage must be weighed against the lifecycle outcome disadvantage.

### 2.4.3 Watson (2015)

Watson (2015), in a Naval Postgraduate School thesis, examined the relationship between source selection strategy and contractor performance, using CPARS ratings as the primary outcome measure. Drawing on a sample of DoD contracts, Watson compared performance ratings across contracts awarded using LPTA, tradeoff, and other source selection methods. The study found that contracts awarded through tradeoff source selection methods received higher average CPARS ratings on the quality, schedule, and management dimensions compared to LPTA-awarded contracts. The differences were statistically significant and robust to controls for contract value, duration, and service category.

Watson's (2015) findings are particularly noteworthy because CPARS ratings represent the government's own assessment of contractor performance—a direct measure of value delivered rather than an indirect proxy. If LPTA procurements systematically produce lower performance quality, as Watson's evidence suggests, then the cost savings achieved through lower initial prices may be illusory, as the government receives commensurately less value. This interpretation aligns with the VfM framework discussed in Section 2.2: value for money is the ratio of quality to cost, and a mechanism that reduces both cost and quality may not improve VfM.

However, Watson's (2015) study has important limitations. The sample was drawn exclusively from DoD contracts, limiting generalizability to civilian agencies. The study relied on CPARS ratings, which are subjective assessments by government evaluators and may be subject to rater bias, grade inflation, or systematic differences in rating practices across agencies and evaluators (Rendon, 2009). The study also did not fully address the endogeneity of source selection method choice—the possibility that agencies selecting tradeoff methods may differ systematically from those selecting LPTA in ways that also affect performance outcomes.

### 2.4.4 Baker (2016)

Baker (2016) examined the relationship between source selection strategy and contract performance outcomes in a study that attempted to address some of the methodological limitations of prior research. Using a sample of DoD service contracts, Baker compared outcomes across LPTA and tradeoff awards while controlling for a richer set of covariates, including contract complexity, competition level, and agency characteristics. The study found that tradeoff awards were associated with lower cost growth and fewer performance deficiencies, particularly for complex service acquisitions.

Baker's (2016) contribution was particularly valuable in its attention to the interaction between source selection method and contract complexity. The finding that the performance disadvantage of LPTA was concentrated in complex acquisitions provides empirical support for the theoretical prediction that the choice of procurement mechanism matters most when the acquisition is difficult to specify and quality variation among offerors is high. For simpler acquisitions, where requirements can be fully specified and quality above the minimum threshold adds little value, LPTA and tradeoff produced similar outcomes—suggesting that LPTA is indeed appropriate in its intended domain.

### 2.4.5 Additional Empirical Evidence

Beyond these focal studies, several other empirical analyses contribute to the evidence base. Rendon (2009) developed and validated a procurement process maturity model that identified source selection capability as a critical dimension of procurement organizational maturity, finding that agencies with more sophisticated source selection processes—including the ability to effectively implement tradeoff evaluations—achieved better procurement outcomes overall. Snider and Rendon (2008) examined the relationship between contract management practices and post-award outcomes, finding that agencies with stronger contract management capabilities were better able to realize the potential benefits of tradeoff source selection.

The Government Accountability Office (GAO) has produced numerous reports examining specific procurement failures and identifying source selection strategy as a contributing factor. GAO's 2024 annual report on defense acquisition highlighted cases in which the use of LPTA for complex procurements contributed to performance failures, cost overruns, and schedule delays, recommending that DoD strengthen its guidance on the appropriate use of LPTA and increase oversight of source selection method decisions (GAO, 2024). While GAO reports are not peer-reviewed empirical studies, they provide valuable case evidence and institutional analysis that complement the quantitative literature.

At the international level, Dimitri et al. (2006) provided a comprehensive analysis of procurement mechanism choice across European public procurement systems, finding significant variation in the use of price-only versus multi-criteria evaluation methods and in the outcomes achieved under different approaches. Their analysis is consistent with the U.S.-focused literature in finding that multi-criteria evaluation methods (the European analog of best-value tradeoff) are associated with better outcomes for complex acquisitions. The European Commission's (2011, 2020) evaluations of the EU procurement directives have similarly found that the use of the "most economically advantageous tender" (MEAT) criterion—which requires evaluation on both price and quality—is associated with better value for money compared to lowest-price-only awards.

### 2.4.6 Gaps in the Literature

The empirical literature on source selection strategy and outcomes, while providing valuable initial evidence, exhibits several significant gaps that this dissertation seeks to address:

1. **Civilian agency evidence**: The vast majority of empirical studies have examined DoD procurement, leaving a major gap in evidence on civilian federal agencies and state/local governments. Civilian procurement differs from defense acquisition in important ways—including the types of goods and services procured, the regulatory framework, the market structure, and the institutional capacity of procurement organizations—and findings from defense contexts may not generalize (Tkachenko et al., 2023).

2. **Cross-sector comparison**: No existing study has systematically compared LPTA and tradeoff outcomes across multiple federal agencies and product/service categories using a common methodological framework and outcome metrics.

3. **Endogeneity**: Prior studies have not adequately addressed the endogeneity of source selection method choice. Because agencies choose LPTA or tradeoff based on acquisition characteristics that also affect outcomes, naive comparisons may be biased. This dissertation employs propensity score matching and instrumental variable techniques to address this identification challenge.

4. **Composite outcomes**: Prior studies have typically examined individual outcome dimensions (cost growth, schedule, performance ratings) in isolation rather than constructing composite public value indices that capture the multidimensional nature of procurement value.

5. **Mediating mechanisms**: The causal pathways through which source selection strategy affects outcomes—including solicitation quality, competition, protest dynamics, and post-award management—have not been systematically modeled.

### 2.4.7 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

The empirical evidence, while limited, consistently points in the direction predicted by theory: best-value tradeoff methods are associated with better procurement outcomes than LPTA, particularly for complex acquisitions. However, the evidence base is narrow, concentrated in defense contexts, and methodologically limited. This dissertation contributes to the literature by providing the first large-scale, cross-agency, quasi-experimental analysis of source selection strategy and public value outcomes in U.S. federal procurement. The following sections review the literatures on five critical mediating and moderating factors—bid protests, solicitation quality, oversight, renegotiation, and incumbency—that shape the pathway from source selection strategy to procurement outcomes.

## 2.5 Bid Protests, Accountability, and Procurement Delay

### 2.5.1 The Dual Nature of Bid Protests

Bid protests occupy a paradoxical position in the procurement system: they are simultaneously a critical accountability mechanism that safeguards the integrity of the procurement process and a source of delay, cost, and disruption that can undermine timely acquisition (Gordon, 2006; Schwartz & Manuel, 2018). Understanding this dual nature is essential to assessing the effects of source selection strategy on procurement outcomes, because the choice between LPTA and tradeoff affects both the likelihood and the consequences of protests.

A bid protest is a formal challenge by an interested party—typically an unsuccessful offeror—alleging that a government agency violated applicable procurement laws, regulations, or the terms of the solicitation in conducting a procurement (Gordon, 2006; Cibinic et al., 2015). Protests may challenge any aspect of the procurement process, including the solicitation terms, the evaluation criteria, the evaluation of proposals, the source selection decision, and the agency's conduct of discussions or negotiations. The protest system serves several important functions: it provides a check on arbitrary or unlawful agency action, it creates incentives for agencies to conduct procurements carefully and in compliance with applicable rules, and it provides a forum for resolving disputes without resort to more costly and disruptive litigation (Yukins, 2012; Schooner, 2001).

### 2.5.2 GAO Protest Process and Outcomes

The Government Accountability Office (GAO) is the primary forum for federal procurement protests, handling approximately 2,000-2,500 protest cases per year (GAO, 2025). The GAO protest process is designed to be expeditious: GAO must issue a decision within 100 calendar days of the protest filing, and agencies must provide their administrative reports within 30 days (GAO, 2025; 4 C.F.R. Part 21). When a protest is filed, the agency is generally required to suspend contract performance (if the contract has already been awarded) or delay award (if it has not), pending resolution of the protest—the so-called automatic stay provision, which is a distinctive feature of the U.S. protest system (Cibinic et al., 2015; Schwartz & Manuel, 2018).

GAO's annual report on bid protests provides a detailed accounting of protest outcomes. In recent fiscal years, GAO has reported that approximately 45-50% of protests are resolved without a formal GAO decision—typically through agency corrective action in which the agency voluntarily re-evaluates proposals, amends the solicitation, or takes other steps to address the protester's concerns (GAO, 2025). Of the protests that proceed to a merits decision, GAO sustains approximately 15-18%—finding that the agency violated applicable law or regulation in a manner prejudicial to the protester (GAO, 2025). The "effectiveness rate"—the percentage of protests in which the protester obtains some form of relief, whether through GAO sustain or agency corrective action—has typically been in the range of 40-50% in recent years (GAO, 2025).

These statistics have important implications for the assessment of procurement quality. The fact that nearly half of all protests result in some form of relief suggests that agencies make significant process errors with appreciable frequency—errors that, in the absence of the protest mechanism, would go uncorrected and could result in suboptimal or unlawful contract awards. At the same time, the protest process imposes real costs: agencies must prepare administrative reports, respond to legal arguments, and potentially re-do evaluations; contractors must bear the costs of protest preparation and litigation; and the government may experience capability gaps during the period of delay (Schwartz & Manuel, 2018; Lusby, 2013).

### 2.5.3 Other Protest Forums

In addition to GAO, procurement protests can be filed at the agency level (with the contracting officer or agency protest forum) or at the United States Court of Federal Claims (COFC) (Gordon, 2006; Cibinic et al., 2015). Agency-level protests are generally faster but may be perceived as less independent, since the agency is essentially reviewing its own decision. COFC protests involve formal litigation with discovery, briefing, and judicial decision-making, and can be more thorough but also more time-consuming and expensive (Gordon, 2006). The existence of multiple protest forums creates a layered accountability system in which different forums serve different functions: agency-level protests enable quick self-correction, GAO protests provide independent review with binding timeline requirements, and COFC protests provide a judicial backstop for the most consequential disputes (Yukins, 2012).

At the state level, protest mechanisms vary considerably. Some states provide robust protest forums with clear timelines and independent review, while others provide limited or no protest rights, relying instead on administrative complaint processes or general contract litigation (NASPO, 2019; NIGP, 2016). This variation in protest availability and process creates cross-jurisdictional differences in the accountability environment for procurement—differences that may affect both the behavior of procurement officials and the quality of procurement outcomes. Yukins (2012) argued that the availability of protest mechanisms, while imposing costs, ultimately improves procurement quality by creating incentives for careful compliance and by correcting errors that would otherwise persist.

### 2.5.4 Protests and Source Selection Strategy

The choice between LPTA and tradeoff affects protest dynamics in several ways. First, tradeoff procurements involve more complex evaluation processes with greater exercises of subjective judgment, creating more potential grounds for protest (Schwartz & Manuel, 2018). An LPTA evaluation is relatively simple—proposals either meet the technical requirements or they do not, and the lowest-priced acceptable proposal wins—leaving limited scope for evaluation challenges. A tradeoff evaluation, by contrast, involves subjective assessments of technical merit, qualitative comparisons among proposals, and judgment calls about the relative weight of price and non-price factors—all of which can be challenged as arbitrary, unreasonable, or inconsistent with the evaluation criteria (Gordon, 2006; Cibinic et al., 2015).

Second, however, the greater procedural simplicity of LPTA does not necessarily translate into fewer protests. Protests challenging LPTA procurements often focus on the appropriateness of using LPTA in the first instance—arguing that the acquisition is too complex or quality-sensitive for a price-only evaluation—or on the adequacy of the technical acceptability standards (CRS, 2024). Furthermore, when LPTA leads to awards to low-quality contractors who subsequently fail to perform, the resulting recompetition or termination creates additional opportunities for protest and delay.

Congressional Research Service (2018) analyzed the relationship between source selection method and protest activity, finding that while tradeoff procurements had a somewhat higher raw protest rate, the difference was largely explained by the greater complexity and dollar value of tradeoff acquisitions—factors independently associated with higher protest rates. After controlling for acquisition complexity and value, the difference in protest rates between LPTA and tradeoff was modest. Schwartz and Manuel (2018) provided a comprehensive analysis of the bid protest system, concluding that protests, while imposing costs and delays, serve an important quality-control function and that efforts to limit protest rights should be approached cautiously.

### 2.5.5 Balancing Integrity with Timely Acquisition

The tension between accountability and timeliness is a recurring theme in the procurement literature and in policy debates about protest reform (Gordon, 2006; Schooner, 2001; Lusby, 2013). Proposals to limit protest rights—by shortening filing deadlines, raising filing fees, limiting the scope of reviewable issues, or eliminating the automatic stay—are periodically advanced by agencies and contractors who view protests as a source of undue delay (Lusby, 2013). Opponents of such proposals argue that the protest system is the primary external check on agency procurement decisions and that weakening it would increase the risk of waste, fraud, and abuse (Yukins, 2012; Schooner, 2001).

The empirical evidence on the net effect of protests on procurement quality is mixed but suggests that the accountability benefits generally outweigh the delay costs, at least at the current level of protest activity. GAO's data show that the vast majority of protests are filed in connection with a small proportion of total contract actions—protest rates have consistently been below 2% of contract actions eligible for protest—and that most protests are resolved within a few months (GAO, 2025). The costs of delay, while real, are typically modest relative to the total value of the procurement, and the corrective actions taken in response to sustained protests often result in improved outcomes (Schwartz & Manuel, 2018).

However, the balance between accountability and timeliness may differ across source selection methods and acquisition types. For complex, high-value tradeoff procurements, the accountability function of protests is particularly valuable because the evaluation process involves substantial discretion and the consequences of error are significant. For simpler LPTA procurements, where the evaluation is largely mechanical and the consequences of error are more limited, the delay costs of protests may outweigh the accountability benefits. This observation suggests a more nuanced approach to protest management that tailors the intensity and speed of review to the complexity and significance of the procurement.

### 2.5.6 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

Bid protests are an integral part of the procurement ecosystem, serving as both an accountability mechanism and a source of delay. The relationship between source selection strategy and protest dynamics is complex and bidirectional: tradeoff methods create more potential grounds for protest but may also produce more defensible decisions, while LPTA methods simplify the evaluation but may generate protests challenging the appropriateness of the method itself. Understanding these dynamics is essential to modeling the full pathway from source selection strategy to public value outcomes. ## 2.6 Solicitation Quality, Requirements Clarity, and Need Misalignment

### 2.6.1 Requirements as Determinant of Procurement Outcomes

The quality of the solicitation—particularly the clarity, completeness, and accuracy of the requirements—is widely recognized as one of the most important determinants of procurement outcomes, yet it has received remarkably little systematic empirical attention (Johansson & Lahtinen, 2012; OECD, 2009). A solicitation that clearly communicates the government's needs, specifies evaluation criteria unambiguously, and provides sufficient information for offerors to prepare accurate and competitive proposals creates the foundation for a successful procurement. Conversely, a solicitation with vague, incomplete, or internally inconsistent requirements undermines the entire acquisition process, regardless of the source selection method employed (NIGP, 2014; Office of Procurement Ombudsman Canada, 2025).

The importance of solicitation quality is a recurring theme in GAO protest decisions. A substantial proportion of sustained protests involve findings that the agency's evaluation was inconsistent with the stated evaluation criteria, that the solicitation was ambiguous, or that the agency failed to follow its own solicitation terms (GAO, 2025). These findings suggest that solicitation quality is not merely a theoretical concern but a practical problem with measurable consequences for procurement integrity and outcomes.

### 2.6.2 Ambiguity, Uncertainty, and Strategic Behavior

When requirements are ambiguous or incomplete, several adverse consequences follow. First, offerors face increased uncertainty about the government's actual needs and the likely scope of contract performance, which increases the cost and risk of proposal preparation and may deter participation—particularly by smaller firms with fewer resources to absorb the risk (Johansson & Lahtinen, 2012). Second, ambiguous requirements create opportunities for strategic behavior: sophisticated offerors may bid based on a narrow interpretation of the requirements, planning to recover through post-award modifications when the full scope of the government's needs becomes apparent (Jung et al., 2013). Third, ambiguity in evaluation criteria creates discretion in the evaluation process that can be—or can be perceived to be—exercised arbitrarily, increasing the likelihood of protests and undermining stakeholder confidence in the procurement process (OECD, 2009).

The interaction between solicitation quality and source selection method is particularly important. Under LPTA, the consequences of ambiguous requirements are amplified because the evaluation mechanism provides no means of distinguishing between offerors who have genuinely understood and can meet the government's needs and those who have adopted a narrow interpretation of the requirements to minimize price. When the only selection criterion above the technical floor is price, offerors have a strong incentive to interpret requirements narrowly and price aggressively—knowing that the ambiguity creates room for post-award scope expansion (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001). Under tradeoff, the evaluation of technical approach and past performance provides some safeguard against this problem, as offerors who demonstrate a deeper understanding of the government's needs and a more robust approach to addressing them can be rewarded with higher technical scores—even if their prices are higher (DAU, 2023).

### 2.6.3 IT Procurement and Requirements Challenges

Information technology procurement presents particularly acute requirements challenges. IT systems are complex, evolving rapidly, and often require customization to the specific operational needs of the acquiring agency (GAO, 2024; Kelman, 2005). The process of translating operational needs into technical specifications is inherently difficult and error-prone: requirements may be stated in terms that are too general to guide development, too specific to accommodate evolving technology, or too focused on technical specifications rather than operational outcomes (Johansson & Lahtinen, 2012).

The challenges of IT procurement requirements have been extensively documented in the literature on government IT project failures. Standish Group reports have consistently found that unclear or changing requirements are among the top causes of IT project failure, with public sector projects particularly susceptible (Standish Group, 2020). The GAO has identified requirements management as a critical risk area for federal IT acquisitions, recommending that agencies invest in requirements definition, stakeholder engagement, and iterative development approaches that can accommodate evolving needs (GAO, 2024).

The interaction between IT procurement challenges and source selection method is significant. When IT requirements are complex and evolving, LPTA is particularly problematic because it forces the government to specify requirements precisely enough for a pass/fail technical evaluation—a level of specificity that may be unattainable for complex IT systems—while selecting the contractor based solely on price, without regard to the contractor's capability to adapt to evolving needs or to bring innovation and expertise to the project (Ban, 2015; CRS, 2024). Tradeoff methods, by contrast, allow the government to evaluate the contractor's understanding of the problem, its proposed technical approach, the experience and qualifications of its personnel, and its capacity for adaptation—factors that are critical determinants of success in complex IT projects.

### 2.6.4 OECD Anti-Bias Tender Design Guidance

The OECD (2009) has provided comprehensive guidance on tender design that addresses the risk of bias, ambiguity, and manipulation in procurement solicitations. The OECD framework emphasizes several principles for effective tender design: requirements should be stated in terms of outcomes or functional capabilities rather than prescriptive technical specifications where possible; evaluation criteria should be clearly stated, objectively measurable where feasible, and weighted in accordance with their relative importance to the government's needs; and solicitations should provide sufficient time and information for offerors to prepare thoughtful proposals (OECD, 2009).

The OECD guidance also addresses the risk that tender design features may inadvertently or deliberately favor particular offerors. Short response timelines, overly narrow technical specifications, excessive experience requirements, and evaluation criteria that align closely with the capabilities of a specific firm can all operate to suppress competition and undermine the integrity of the procurement process (OECD, 2009; Fazekas & Blum, 2021). These tender design features interact with source selection strategy: under LPTA, where only price matters above the technical threshold, narrow specifications that limit the pool of technically acceptable offerors have a direct and deterministic effect on competition and outcomes. Under tradeoff, the broader evaluation framework provides some buffer against specification narrowing, as a wider range of approaches can compete on quality dimensions.

### 2.6.5 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

Solicitation quality—the clarity, completeness, and fairness of the requirements—is a critical mediator in the pathway from source selection strategy to procurement outcomes. Poor solicitation quality amplifies the disadvantages of LPTA by creating conditions that reward narrow interpretation and strategic pricing, while tradeoff methods provide some buffering against requirements deficiencies by evaluating the depth of offerors' understanding and the quality of their proposed approaches. The operationalization of solicitation quality indicators and their incorporation into the empirical analysis is described in Chapter 3. ## 2.7 Oversight Layers, Review Boards, and the "Fraud Protection" Tradeoff

### 2.7.1 The Rationale for Oversight in Public Procurement

Public procurement operates under a regime of oversight that has no parallel in private-sector purchasing. Multiple layers of review, approval, and audit are imposed on procurement decisions by statute, regulation, and agency policy, reflecting the fundamental premise that public expenditure must be subject to external checks to prevent waste, fraud, and abuse (McCue, 2003; Kelman, 2005). These oversight mechanisms include pre-award reviews by higher-level contracting authorities, legal sufficiency reviews, source selection boards, peer reviews, competition advocates, inspectors general, GAO audits, congressional oversight, and the bid protest system discussed in Section 2.5 (Cibinic et al., 2015; Manuel, 2011).

The theoretical justification for procurement oversight rests on principal-agent theory and the observation that public officials, like all agents, may deviate from their principals' interests in the absence of monitoring and accountability mechanisms (Miller, 2005). In the procurement context, the potential for deviation takes several forms: corruption (accepting bribes or personal benefits in exchange for favorable contract actions), favoritism (directing awards to preferred contractors for non-meritorious reasons), waste (failing to pursue cost-effective solutions due to negligence or indifference), and policy subversion (using procurement authority to pursue personal or bureaucratic objectives that conflict with legislative intent) (McCue, 2003; Thai, 2001). Oversight mechanisms are designed to deter these behaviors by increasing the probability of detection and the consequences of wrongdoing.

### 2.7.2 Oversight as a Source of Delay and Cost

While the fraud-prevention rationale for oversight is well-established, a growing body of literature has documented the costs that excessive oversight imposes on procurement efficiency. Kelman (1990, 2005) was one of the most influential critics of what he termed the "procurement pathology"—the tendency of the procurement system to impose layer upon layer of rules, reviews, and approvals in response to individual instances of failure or scandal, creating a regulatory burden that far exceeds what is necessary to prevent wrongdoing and that significantly impedes the government's ability to acquire goods and services efficiently.

Kelman (1990) argued that the procurement system's preoccupation with preventing corruption and process violations came at the expense of achieving good outcomes. By constraining the discretion of procurement professionals, the system made it difficult for competent professionals to exercise the judgment necessary to achieve the best results, while doing relatively little to deter the small number of individuals who were actually inclined toward corruption. The result, Kelman argued, was a system that punished the many to constrain the few—imposing costs on every procurement action to prevent misconduct that affected only a tiny fraction of actions.

Subsequent research has documented the empirical consequences of this dynamic. Procurement administrative lead time (PALT)—the time from solicitation to award—has increased substantially over recent decades, with complex federal procurements often taking 12-18 months or longer from requirement identification to contract award (DAU, 2023; RAND Corporation, 2022). While multiple factors contribute to PALT, oversight and review requirements are consistently identified as significant contributors by procurement professionals and organizational studies (DAU, 2023; Rendon, 2009).

### 2.7.3 Calvo, Cui, and Serpa (2019): Oversight and Efficiency

Calvo, Cui, and Serpa (2019) provided rigorous causal evidence on the effects of oversight on public project efficiency using a regression discontinuity design. Their study exploited a threshold rule in a Latin American country that required projects above a certain budget threshold to undergo additional oversight and approval processes. By comparing projects just above and just below this threshold—which were similar in all respects except for the additional oversight requirement—they were able to estimate the causal effect of the additional oversight on project outcomes.

The results were striking: projects subject to additional oversight experienced significantly longer delays and higher cost overruns compared to otherwise similar projects below the threshold. The additional oversight did not reduce the incidence of quality deficiencies or other performance problems—suggesting that the oversight was imposing costs without producing offsetting benefits. Calvo et al. (2019) interpreted their findings as evidence that the marginal oversight layer, at least in their context, was operating in the region of diminishing returns where the costs of additional review exceeded the benefits of additional scrutiny.

While the Calvo et al. (2019) study was conducted in a non-U.S. context, its findings resonate with the U.S. procurement experience. The study provides causal evidence supporting the theoretical arguments of Kelman (1990, 2005) and others who have contended that the U.S. procurement system suffers from excessive oversight that impedes efficiency without materially improving integrity. In the context of this dissertation, the findings suggest that post-award administrative oversight layers—such as additional review and approval requirements for contract modifications, change orders, and performance assessments—may increase schedule delays and lifecycle costs, contributing to the erosion of public value.

### 2.7.4 McCue (2003): The Fraud-Protection Dilemma

McCue (2003) examined the tension between fraud prevention and procurement efficiency from a public administration perspective, analyzing how government agencies balance the competing demands of accountability and performance in their procurement systems. McCue's analysis identified a "fraud protection dilemma" in which the fear of waste, fraud, and abuse drives the adoption of increasingly burdensome procedural requirements that ultimately undermine the procurement system's ability to deliver value—the very outcome the requirements were designed to prevent.

McCue (2003) argued that the dilemma is exacerbated by the asymmetry of incentives facing procurement officials: the costs of procedural non-compliance (audit findings, protests, career consequences) are immediate, visible, and personal, while the costs of suboptimal outcomes (higher lifecycle costs, lower quality, delayed capabilities) are diffuse, delayed, and attributable to many factors beyond the procurement official's control. This incentive structure creates a bias toward procedural compliance at the expense of outcome achievement—a phenomenon that Kelman (2005) characterized as "process-over-results."

The interaction between oversight and source selection strategy is significant. LPTA procurements, by virtue of their simpler evaluation structure, generally require less oversight and fewer reviews than tradeoff procurements, contributing to their shorter PALT (Osman et al., 2017). Tradeoff procurements, which involve subjective judgments and comparative evaluations, attract more scrutiny from legal reviewers, source selection authorities, and potential protesters—increasing the oversight burden but also providing more opportunities for quality assurance. Understanding this interaction is essential to interpreting the observed differences in procurement cycle times between LPTA and tradeoff and to assessing the net public value implications of each approach.

### 2.7.5 Conditions Under Which Oversight Is Beneficial vs. Obstructive

The literature suggests that the relationship between oversight and procurement outcomes is not linear but curvilinear: some oversight is necessary and beneficial, but beyond a certain point, additional oversight produces diminishing returns and eventually net costs (Kelman, 2005; Calvo et al., 2019). The optimal level of oversight depends on several factors, including the risk of the procurement (higher-risk procurements warrant more scrutiny), the competence and integrity of the procurement workforce (more capable workforces require less external oversight), the quality of internal controls and management systems, and the availability of ex post accountability mechanisms such as performance evaluation and audit (McCue, 2003; Rendon, 2009).

Several scholars have proposed frameworks for calibrating oversight intensity to procurement risk. The DoD's Adaptive Acquisition Framework, introduced in 2020, represents one such effort, establishing different acquisition pathways with varying levels of oversight based on the complexity, urgency, and risk of the acquisition (DAU, 2023). Risk-based oversight frameworks recognize that applying the same level of scrutiny to a $50,000 office supply purchase and a $500 million IT system modernization is neither efficient nor effective and that the procurement system should concentrate its oversight resources where the risks are greatest and the potential benefits of scrutiny are largest.

### 2.7.6 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

Oversight layers in public procurement serve the essential function of preventing waste, fraud, and abuse, but excessive oversight can impede efficiency, increase costs, and delay capability delivery without materially improving integrity. The empirical evidence suggests that the current procurement system may err on the side of excessive oversight, particularly for lower-risk acquisitions. This finding has implications for the interpretation of PALT differences between LPTA and tradeoff procurements and for the assessment of the net public value of each approach. ## 2.8 Renegotiation, Change Orders, and Strategic Underbidding Under Low-Price Norms

### 2.8.1 The Phenomenon of Strategic Underbidding

One of the most consequential—and least visible—effects of lowest-price procurement norms is the incentive they create for strategic underbidding: the practice of submitting a bid below the expected cost of performance, with the intention of recovering the shortfall through post-award modifications, change orders, claims, and renegotiation (Jung et al., 2013; Bajari et al., 2014). Strategic underbidding, also known as "buying in" in government procurement parlance, is prohibited by FAR 3.501 but is widely recognized as difficult to detect and enforce (Cibinic et al., 2015; Gordon, 2006).

The economic logic of strategic underbidding is straightforward. When the selection criterion is lowest price and the contract is likely to be incomplete—because the requirements are complex, the scope is uncertain, or the performance period is long—a rational bidder may find it profitable to bid below expected costs to win the contract and then exploit the inevitable need for changes and modifications to recover the difference (Jung et al., 2013). This strategy is particularly attractive when the costs of switching contractors after award are high (creating lock-in), when the government's monitoring of post-award cost growth is imperfect, and when the process for approving modifications is routine rather than rigorous (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001).

### 2.8.2 Jung, Kim, and Suh (2013): Formal Analysis

Jung, Kim, and Suh (2013) provided a formal game-theoretic analysis of strategic bidding and contract renegotiation in procurement auctions. Their model demonstrates that when bidders anticipate the possibility of renegotiation after the initial contract is awarded—because the contract is incomplete and the scope is likely to change—they have an incentive to bid below their true expected costs. The magnitude of the underbid is increasing in the probability and expected magnitude of post-award changes, and decreasing in the competitiveness of the bidding environment (because more intense competition already drives bids down).

The implications of the Jung et al. (2013) model for the LPTA vs. tradeoff comparison are significant. Under LPTA, where the sole criterion above technical acceptability is price, the incentive for underbidding is maximized: the bidder who submits the lowest price wins, regardless of the realism or sustainability of that price. Under tradeoff, the evaluation of technical approach and past performance provides some check on unrealistic pricing, because evaluators can assess whether the proposed price is consistent with the proposed technical approach, the staffing plan, and the offeror's past performance—and can assign lower scores to proposals that appear to involve unrealistic pricing or inadequate resources (DAU, 2023).

### 2.8.3 The Winner's Curse in Low-Price Awards

The winner's curse—a well-known phenomenon in auction theory—occurs when the winning bidder discovers, after winning, that it bid too aggressively and that the contract will be unprofitable at the bid price (Thaler, 1988; Kagel & Levin, 1986). In the procurement context, the winner's curse is particularly problematic because the consequences extend beyond the bidder's financial loss to affect the government's receipt of services. A contractor that has underbid may respond by cutting quality, reducing staffing, skipping process steps, or seeking to renegotiate the contract terms—all of which reduce the value delivered to the government (Ganuza, 2007).

The winner's curse is more likely under LPTA than under tradeoff for two reasons. First, LPTA's price-only selection criterion intensifies price competition and increases the probability that the winning bid reflects aggressive pricing rather than genuine cost efficiency. Second, under tradeoff, the evaluation of technical approach and past performance allows the government to screen for offerors that have a realistic understanding of the scope of work and a credible plan for performing at the proposed price—reducing the likelihood of selecting a bidder that has fallen victim to the winner's curse or is intentionally underbidding.

### 2.8.4 Cost Shifting from Pre-Award to Post-Award

The phenomenon of strategic underbidding creates a systematic pattern of cost shifting from the pre-award phase (where the initial contract price is determined) to the post-award phase (where modifications, change orders, and cost adjustments accumulate). When this cost shifting occurs, the apparent price advantage of LPTA relative to tradeoff is illusory: the lower initial award price is offset by higher post-award cost growth, such that the total lifecycle cost may be equal to or greater than what would have been paid under a tradeoff approach that selected a more capable contractor at a higher initial price (Bajari et al., 2014; Osman et al., 2017).

The empirical evidence on post-award cost growth is consistent with this pattern. Osman et al. (2017) found that LPTA contracts exhibited higher rates of cost growth through modifications than tradeoff contracts. Bajari et al. (2014), analyzing California highway construction contracts, found that contracts awarded through competitive bidding exhibited higher rates of cost overruns than contracts awarded through negotiation, with the difference attributable largely to the higher incidence of strategic underbidding and the winner's curse in competitive bidding contexts. While the Bajari et al. (2014) study examined construction rather than government services, the underlying dynamics—competition on price, contract incompleteness, and post-award cost adjustment—are directly applicable to the government procurement context.

The cost-shifting phenomenon has important implications for the measurement of procurement value. If LPTA achieves lower initial prices but higher post-award cost growth, then studies that compare source selection methods solely on initial award price will overstate the cost advantage of LPTA and understate its lifecycle cost. This observation underscores the importance of using total realized contract cost (including modifications) rather than initial award price as the cost measure in empirical analyses—an approach adopted in this dissertation.

### 2.8.5 Mechanisms of Post-Award Cost Recovery

Post-award cost recovery takes several forms in government procurement. Contract modifications—both bilateral (agreed by both parties) and unilateral (directed by the government under the changes clause)—are the primary mechanism (Cibinic et al., 2015). Engineering change proposals, value engineering change proposals, claims under the Contract Disputes Act, and requests for equitable adjustment provide additional avenues for contractors to seek increased compensation after award (Gordon, 2006). While each of these mechanisms has a legitimate purpose—contracts must be adaptable to changing needs and unforeseen circumstances—the cumulative effect of strategic underbidding followed by aggressive post-award claims can significantly erode the value of the procurement.

Data from the Federal Procurement Data System (FPDS-NG) and USAspending.gov enable the measurement of post-award cost growth by comparing initial award values to total obligated amounts on completed contracts. This comparison provides a systematic, large-scale measure of the cost-shifting phenomenon that can be analyzed by source selection method, contract type, product/service category, and agency—enabling the empirical tests described in Chapter 3.

### 2.8.6 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

Strategic underbidding and post-award cost recovery are important mechanisms through which lowest-price procurement norms can undermine public value. By creating incentives for unrealistic pricing and facilitating post-award cost shifting, LPTA may achieve the appearance of cost efficiency at the expense of genuine lifecycle value. The measurement of cost growth and modification patterns in the empirical analysis provides a direct test of this mechanism. The next section examines a related phenomenon—incumbency advantage and competition suppression—that further mediates the relationship between source selection strategy and public value.

## 2.9 Incumbency Advantage, Tender Steering, and Competition Suppression

### 2.9.1 The Nature of Incumbency Advantage

Incumbency advantage in procurement refers to the informational, relational, and procedural advantages that the current contractor holds over potential competitors in a recompetition or follow-on procurement (Iossa, 2019; Klemperer, 2002). These advantages are a natural consequence of the incumbent's performance on the existing contract, which provides it with detailed knowledge of the government's operations, preferences, and working styles; established relationships with government personnel; proprietary knowledge of system architectures, data structures, and processes; and reduced transition costs relative to a new contractor that would need to invest in learning and startup activities (Lewis & Bajari, 2011; Iossa, 2019).

Incumbency advantage is not inherently problematic—it may reflect genuine efficiency gains from continuity and learning—but it becomes a concern when it is so substantial that it effectively precludes meaningful competition, allowing the incumbent to win recompetitions at higher prices or lower quality than would prevail in a genuinely competitive market (Klemperer, 2002; Iossa, 2019). The procurement system's interest in competition reflects not only the goal of achieving lower prices but also the goals of providing fair access to government business, preventing the concentration of government reliance on a small number of firms, and maintaining the health and competitiveness of the contractor base (CICA, 1984; Manuel, 2011).

### 2.9.2 Tender Steering and Specification Tailoring

Tender steering refers to the deliberate or inadvertent design of solicitation terms, specifications, or evaluation criteria in a manner that favors a particular offeror—typically the incumbent—over other potential competitors (Fazekas & Blum, 2021; OECD, 2009). Steering can take overt forms, such as specifying proprietary technology or brand names without permitting equivalents, or subtler forms, such as drafting performance work statements that closely mirror the incumbent's approach, setting experience requirements that only the incumbent can meet, or establishing unrealistically short response timelines that disadvantage offerors who lack pre-existing knowledge of the requirement (Fazekas & Blum, 2021).

Fazekas and Blum (2021) provided a comprehensive analysis of tender steering mechanisms and their effects on competition in public procurement. Their study, drawing on procurement data from multiple European countries, identified several quantifiable indicators of potential steering: extremely short submission deadlines, non-standardized product descriptions, evaluation criteria that weight factors correlated with incumbency (such as specific experience with the buying organization), and solicitation structures that create barriers to entry for new competitors. They found that these tender design features were associated with reduced competition (measured by the number of bids received), higher contract prices, and increased likelihood of contract amendments—all indicators of reduced public value.

### 2.9.3 Single-Bid Contracts and Competition Suppression

Single-bid contracts—procurements in which only one offer is received—are a particularly stark indicator of competition suppression. While a single bid may sometimes reflect a genuinely thin market or a legitimate sole-source situation, a high rate of single-bid contracts in categories where multiple qualified suppliers exist suggests that the procurement process is failing to generate effective competition (Fazekas & Blum, 2021; OECD, 2009). In the European context, studies have found that single-bid contracts are associated with higher prices (by 5-10% or more compared to contracts with multiple bids), lower quality, and higher rates of cost overruns (European Commission, 2020; Fazekas & Kocsis, 2020).

In the U.S. federal procurement context, FPDS data reveal that a significant proportion of contract actions receive limited competition or are awarded non-competitively, despite the statutory presumption in favor of full and open competition (Manuel, 2011; FPDS, 2024). The rate of effective competition varies substantially across agencies, product/service categories, and contract types—variation that can be analyzed to assess the relationship between competition intensity and procurement outcomes.

The interaction between source selection strategy and competition is important. Under LPTA, competition is channeled entirely through the price dimension, and the number of technically acceptable bidders is the primary determinant of the intensity of price competition. If incumbency advantage or tender steering reduces the pool of technically acceptable bidders, the effect on competition is direct and deterministic—fewer bidders means less competitive pressure on price. Under tradeoff, competition operates on multiple dimensions (price, quality, experience, approach), and even a smaller number of bidders may generate effective competition if they compete intensely on quality dimensions. This suggests that tradeoff methods may be more resilient to competition suppression than LPTA, maintaining the benefits of competition even in less-than-fully-competitive markets.

### 2.9.4 Iossa (2019): Tackling Incumbency Advantage

Iossa (2019) examined the problem of incumbency advantage from a mechanism design perspective, analyzing procurement mechanisms that can mitigate the incumbent's informational and procedural advantages and promote effective competition. Iossa's analysis identified several design features that can reduce incumbency advantage: requiring the government to make transition-relevant information available to all bidders, establishing neutral evaluation criteria that do not depend on specific knowledge of the government's operations, providing adequate response time for non-incumbent offerors to develop proposals, and using evaluation criteria that emphasize forward-looking capabilities rather than backward-looking experience with the specific contract.

Iossa's (2019) work has direct implications for the design of source selection strategies. Under tradeoff, the evaluation criteria can be structured to emphasize the quality and innovation of the proposed approach rather than specific past experience with the particular agency or contract, reducing the incumbent's advantage and promoting more effective competition. Under LPTA, where the evaluation is limited to technical acceptability and price, there are fewer levers available to mitigate incumbency advantage—the incumbent's superior knowledge of the requirement translates directly into more accurate cost estimation and more efficient proposal preparation, advantages that cannot be offset through the evaluation process.

### 2.9.5 Short Response Windows as Competition Barriers

The length of the solicitation response period is a potentially important but under-studied determinant of competition in procurement. Short response windows—the period between solicitation issuance and proposal due date—disadvantage non-incumbent offerors who need time to understand the requirement, develop a technical approach, assemble a team, and estimate costs (OECD, 2009; Fazekas & Blum, 2021). Incumbents, who already understand the requirement and have teams in place, can prepare proposals much more quickly, giving them a significant advantage when response windows are short.

The FAR requires agencies to provide "a reasonable time" for proposal preparation (FAR 5.203), but the interpretation of "reasonable" varies widely across agencies and acquisitions. Studies of European procurement data have found that unusually short submission deadlines are correlated with reduced competition and single-bid outcomes, suggesting that some agencies may use short deadlines—whether deliberately or through poor planning—as a mechanism for limiting competition (Fazekas & Blum, 2021; European Commission, 2020).

In the context of this dissertation, solicitation response time is included as a variable in the analysis of competition intensity and public value outcomes, enabling an assessment of whether short response windows are associated with reduced competition and lower public value, and whether this relationship differs across LPTA and tradeoff procurements.

### 2.9.6 Section Summary and Connection to the Dissertation

Incumbency advantage, tender steering, and competition suppression are important mediating factors in the pathway from source selection strategy to public value. These phenomena operate through the design of solicitations and the dynamics of competition, affecting the quality and diversity of the contractor pool and the intensity of competitive pressure. The empirical analysis in this dissertation examines competition intensity (measured by the number of offers received) as both a dependent variable (influenced by solicitation design and source selection method) and a mediating variable (transmitting the effects of competition to public value outcomes). The final section of this chapter synthesizes the theoretical and empirical literature reviewed in the preceding sections into a conceptual model and formal hypotheses.

## 2.10 Conceptual Model and Hypotheses

### 2.10.1 Synthesis and Conceptual Framework

The preceding nine sections have reviewed a diverse body of literature spanning public value theory, procurement economics, auction and mechanism design theory, transaction cost economics, contract theory, and empirical studies of procurement processes and outcomes. From this review, several convergent themes emerge that motivate the conceptual model guiding this dissertation's empirical analysis.

**Theme 1: Source selection strategy is a consequential institutional design choice, not merely a procedural detail.** The choice between LPTA and tradeoff reflects fundamentally different assumptions about the nature of value in procurement and creates different incentive structures for contractors and procurement officials. Theory and limited empirical evidence consistently suggest that LPTA is associated with inferior lifecycle outcomes for complex acquisitions, while tradeoff methods enable the government to capture quality-adjusted value that LPTA systematically underweights (Moore, 1995; Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Osman et al., 2017; Watson, 2015).

**Theme 2: The effects of source selection strategy on outcomes are mediated by several process and institutional factors.** The pathway from source selection method to procurement outcomes is not direct but operates through mediating mechanisms including solicitation quality (Section 2.6), competition intensity (Section 2.9), protest dynamics (Section 2.5), post-award cost adjustment (Section 2.8), and oversight burden (Section 2.7). Understanding these mediating mechanisms is essential to designing interventions that improve procurement outcomes.

**Theme 3: The moderating effect of acquisition complexity is central.** The theoretical and empirical literatures converge on the prediction that the superiority of tradeoff over LPTA is contingent on the complexity and uncertainty of the acquisition. For simple, well-defined, commodity-like procurements, LPTA and tradeoff produce similar outcomes, and the speed advantage of LPTA may make it the preferred approach. For complex services, IT systems, and professional expertise, the quality and lifecycle cost advantages of tradeoff are most pronounced (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Williamson, 1985; Baker, 2016; Osman et al., 2017).

**Theme 4: Competition is both an outcome of procurement design and a determinant of procurement value.** The design of solicitations—including source selection strategy, requirements clarity, response timelines, and evaluation criteria—shapes the intensity and quality of competition, which in turn affects the value the government receives. Competition is not merely the number of bids but the quality and intensity of competitive engagement, which depends on the procurement mechanism's ability to reward quality, innovation, and capability alongside price (Che, 1993; Fazekas & Blum, 2021).

**Theme 5: The empirical literature has significant gaps that this dissertation addresses.** The existing evidence is narrow (concentrated in defense acquisition), methodologically limited (lacking rigorous quasi-experimental designs), and incomplete (examining individual outcome dimensions in isolation rather than composite public value). This dissertation provides the first large-scale, cross-agency, multi-dimensional analysis of the relationship between source selection strategy and public value outcomes in U.S. federal procurement.

### 2.10.2 The Conceptual Model

Figure 2.1 presents the conceptual model that guides the empirical analysis. The model specifies the relationships among the primary independent variable (source selection strategy), mediating variables (solicitation quality, competition intensity, protest dynamics, post-award cost adjustment, oversight burden), moderating variables (acquisition complexity, agency capacity, contract type, market conditions), and the dependent variable (public value outcomes, operationalized as a composite index of cost growth, timeliness, performance quality, protest incidence, and service continuity).

**[Figure 2.1: Conceptual Model — Source Selection Strategy and Public Value Outcomes]**

The model posits the following causal structure:

1. **Direct effect**: Source selection strategy (LPTA vs. tradeoff) has a direct effect on public value outcomes, with tradeoff expected to produce higher public value on average.

2. **Mediated effects**: The direct effect is partially mediated by:
   - *Solicitation quality*: Tradeoff methods are associated with more thorough requirements development and clearer evaluation criteria, which improve outcomes.
   - *Competition intensity*: Tradeoff methods attract a broader and more capable pool of competitors by enabling non-price differentiation.
   - *Post-award cost adjustment*: LPTA methods create stronger incentives for strategic underbidding and post-award cost recovery.

3. **Moderated effects**: The direct and mediated effects are moderated by:
   - *Acquisition complexity*: The advantage of tradeoff over LPTA is increasing in acquisition complexity.
   - *Contract type*: The effects differ across firm-fixed-price, cost-reimbursement, and time-and-materials contracts.
   - *Agency capacity*: Agencies with higher procurement maturity may be better able to implement tradeoff effectively.
   - *Market conditions*: The number of qualified suppliers and the competitiveness of the market affect the marginal value of tradeoff methods.

4. **Protest dynamics**: Protests serve as both an accountability mechanism (improving outcomes through error correction) and a source of delay (reducing timeliness), with the net effect depending on the procurement context.

5. **Oversight burden**: Administrative oversight layers affect the timeliness of procurement and may moderate the relationship between source selection strategy and outcomes.

### 2.10.3 Formal Hypotheses

Drawing on the theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence reviewed in this chapter, ten formal hypotheses are proposed. These hypotheses are organized into two groups: those that are directly testable with the archival procurement data available for this study (H1, H2, H5, H6, H8) and those that require survey, interview, or institutional-comparison data and are therefore positioned as directions for future research (H3, H4, H7, H9, H10).

#### Hypotheses Testable with Archival Data

**Hypothesis 1 (H1):** *Best-value tradeoff procurements will exhibit higher after-award performance and higher public-value indices than comparable LPTA procurements, holding constant product/service category, acquisition complexity, and market conditions.*

Theoretical grounding: This is the central hypothesis of the dissertation, derived from the convergent predictions of public value theory (Moore, 1995), scoring auction theory (Che, 1993), transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985), and incomplete contract theory (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001). When the procurement mechanism evaluates quality alongside price, it selects contractors with higher capability and stronger incentives to deliver value, producing better lifecycle outcomes. The empirical evidence from defense procurement (Osman et al., 2017; Watson, 2015; Baker, 2016) supports this prediction but has not been tested across civilian agencies or with composite public value measures.

**Hypothesis 2 (H2):** *LPTA procurements will be associated with higher rates of post-award renegotiation and change orders and a larger gap between initial award price and realized total contract cost than comparable tradeoff procurements.*

Theoretical grounding: This hypothesis follows directly from the theory of strategic underbidding (Jung et al., 2013), the winner's curse in price-only auctions (Thaler, 1988; Kagel & Levin, 1986), and incomplete contract theory (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Hart & Moore, 1988). LPTA's price-only selection criterion maximizes the incentive for underbidding and minimizes the government's ability to screen for pricing realism through technical evaluation. The cost-shifting mechanism—from pre-award to post-award—creates a predictable pattern of higher cost growth in LPTA contracts. Bajari et al. (2014) provide supporting evidence from the construction context.

**Hypothesis 5 (H5):** *Additional post-award administrative oversight layers will increase schedule delays and overall lifecycle cost growth.*

Theoretical grounding: This hypothesis draws on the theory of oversight costs and diminishing returns to monitoring (Kelman, 1990, 2005; McCue, 2003) and the empirical evidence from Calvo et al. (2019) showing that additional oversight layers increase delays and costs without improving quality outcomes. The hypothesis tests whether this pattern holds in the U.S. federal procurement context, using variation in agency-level oversight requirements as the source of identifying variation. It is measured through the number of review and approval steps documented in the procurement record and through agency-level regulatory burden indicators.

**Hypothesis 6 (H6):** *Tender design features indicative of incumbent advantage—including short response windows, narrow specifications, and single-bid outcomes—will directly correlate with less competition and lower public value.*

Theoretical grounding: This hypothesis derives from the literature on incumbency advantage (Iossa, 2019; Klemperer, 2002), tender steering (Fazekas & Blum, 2021), and the OECD's anti-bias guidance (OECD, 2009). When solicitations are designed in ways that favor incumbents—whether through short response timelines, narrow specifications, or excessive experience requirements—competition is suppressed and the government receives less value. The hypothesis is testable using FPDS data on solicitation response periods, the number of offers received, and the incidence of single-bid awards, linked to outcome measures of public value.

**Hypothesis 8 (H8):** *The adverse effects of LPTA on renegotiation and public value will be stronger for complex service and IT contracts than for commodity-like procurements.*

Theoretical grounding: This interaction hypothesis is central to the theoretical framework and is derived from the convergent predictions of Bajari and Tadelis (2001), Williamson (1985), and the empirical findings of Baker (2016) and Osman et al. (2017). The theoretical expectation is that the disadvantages of LPTA are concentrated in acquisitions where contract incompleteness is high, quality variation among offerors is significant, and the consequences of poor performance are substantial—conditions that characterize complex services and IT but not commodity purchases. The hypothesis is testable by estimating the interaction between source selection method and product/service category complexity in the outcome models.

#### Hypotheses for Future Research

The following hypotheses address important mediating and institutional mechanisms but require data beyond the archival procurement datasets available for this study—specifically, survey or interview data on solicitation quality, institutional data on state-level protest regimes, or natural experiment data on policy changes. They are presented here to complete the conceptual model and to identify important directions for future research.

**Hypothesis 3 (H3):** *Solicitation quality, operationalized as requirements clarity and alignment, will mediate the relationship between source-selection method and performance outcomes; higher clarity and alignment will reduce protests and improve performance.*

Theoretical grounding: Derived from the literature on requirements quality (Johansson & Lahtinen, 2012), the OECD's tender design guidance (OECD, 2009), and the GAO's protest data showing that ambiguous requirements are a leading cause of sustained protests (GAO, 2025). Solicitation quality mediates the source-selection-to-outcome pathway because tradeoff methods incentivize more thorough requirements development and because better requirements reduce the ambiguity that drives both protests and strategic underbidding. Testing this hypothesis requires direct measurement of solicitation quality, which is not systematically available in archival datasets but could be obtained through content analysis of solicitation documents or surveys of procurement professionals. This hypothesis is therefore designated for future research, with the methodological approach outlined in Chapter 5.

**Hypothesis 4 (H4):** *States and agencies with clearer and faster bid-protest timelines will have shorter overall procurement-cycle times net of protest incidence.*

Theoretical grounding: Derived from the comparative analysis of protest regimes (Yukins, 2012; NASPO, 2019) and the theoretical prediction that institutional clarity reduces transaction costs (Williamson, 1985). When the rules governing protests are clear and the timeline for resolution is predictable, the uncertainty associated with potential protests is reduced, enabling faster procurement processing. This hypothesis requires cross-jurisdictional institutional data on protest regime characteristics—data that could be compiled from state procurement statutes and agency protest procedures but is not currently available in standardized form. It is designated for future research.

**Hypothesis 7 (H7):** *Competition intensity will mediate the relationship between solicitation design and public value, such that procurement designs that promote broader and more intensive competition will achieve higher public value outcomes.*

Theoretical grounding: Derived from auction theory (Che, 1993; Krishna, 2010), the empirical evidence on single-bid contracts (Fazekas & Blum, 2021), and public value theory's emphasis on fair access and market stewardship (Moore, 1995). While the archival data provide measures of competition intensity (number of offers received), the mediating mechanism through which solicitation design affects competition requires more granular data on solicitation design features—such as the specificity of requirements, the breadth of eligible firms, and the accessibility of the solicitation—that are not systematically available in FPDS. Partial testing is possible with available data, but full mediation analysis requires additional solicitation-level data. This hypothesis is therefore positioned as partially testable, with full testing designated for future research.

**Hypothesis 9 (H9):** *Higher protest incidence will be associated with higher vendor participation costs and higher procurement lead time; however, protest availability will correlate with fewer post-award performance failures in high-discretion procurements.*

Theoretical grounding: This two-part hypothesis captures the dual nature of protests discussed in Section 2.5, drawing on Gordon (2006), Yukins (2012), Schwartz and Manuel (2018), and the GAO's protest outcome data (GAO, 2025). The first part (protests increase costs and delay) is uncontroversial and supported by direct evidence. The second part (protest availability improves outcomes) reflects the accountability mechanism through which the threat of protest disciplines agency behavior and improves the quality of procurement decisions. Testing the second part requires variation in protest availability—which exists across state-level procurement systems but not within the federal system, where protest rights are uniform. This hypothesis is designated for future research, with the institutional comparison design outlined in Chapter 5.

**Hypothesis 10 (H10):** *Implementing structured best-value guidance and standardized evaluation rubrics will reduce variance in award outcomes across agencies and states and reduce strategic underbidding behavior.*

Theoretical grounding: This hypothesis addresses the policy implications of the dissertation's findings, drawing on the literature on procurement standardization (DAU, 2023; NASPO, 2019), the evidence that variance in procurement outcomes is driven partly by inconsistency in evaluation practices (Rendon, 2009), and the theory of strategic underbidding (Jung et al., 2013). Testing this hypothesis requires a policy change—the implementation of standardized guidance—and a pre-post or difference-in-differences evaluation design. While such natural experiments may arise (e.g., the DoD LPTA restrictions in the FY2017 and FY2018 NDAAs provide a potential natural experiment), the primary empirical analysis in this dissertation does not test this hypothesis directly. It is designated for future research, with the evaluation design outlined in Chapter 5.

### 2.10.5 Alternative Explanations and Rival Hypotheses

Rigorous hypothesis testing requires consideration of alternative explanations that could account for observed patterns without supporting the proposed theoretical model. Several rival hypotheses deserve explicit acknowledgment:

**Selection effects**: Agencies may choose tradeoff methods for acquisitions that are inherently more likely to succeed—because they are better funded, have more experienced program managers, or are higher organizational priorities—creating an appearance of tradeoff superiority that reflects selection rather than treatment effects. The empirical strategy addresses this through propensity score matching and control variables, but residual confounding cannot be entirely eliminated with observational data.

**Workforce quality**: Better-trained or more experienced procurement workforces may both prefer tradeoff methods and produce better outcomes, with the correlation between method and outcome reflecting workforce quality rather than the causal effect of the method itself. Agency-level controls for procurement workforce characteristics partially address this alternative.

**Market conditions**: Variations in the competitive environment across agencies, geographies, or time periods may drive both the choice of source selection method and the observed outcomes. Market structure controls and fixed effects address this concern but may not fully capture all relevant variation.

**Regulatory compliance**: The observed patterns may reflect not the inherent superiority of tradeoff but rather the regulatory attention devoted to tradeoff procurements (more thorough documentation, more rigorous review) that disciplines the procurement process independent of the source selection method itself. This alternative is particularly relevant given the findings in Section 2.7 on oversight.

These alternative explanations are addressed through the research design described in Chapter 3, which employs multiple identification strategies to strengthen causal inference. However, the limitations inherent in observational research are acknowledged, and the conclusions are stated with appropriate epistemic humility.

Ten formal hypotheses have been stated, with clear identification of the theoretical grounding and data requirements for each. The five hypotheses testable with archival data (H1, H2, H5, H6, H8) constitute the primary empirical contribution of the dissertation, while the five hypotheses requiring additional data (H3, H4, H7, H9, H10) define a research agenda for future work.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter has provided a comprehensive review of the theoretical and empirical literatures relevant to the central question of this dissertation: whether and under what conditions best-value source selection strategies produce higher public value than lowest-price technically acceptable methods in government procurement. The review has drawn on multiple theoretical traditions—public value theory, auction and mechanism design theory, transaction cost economics, incomplete contract theory, and principal-agent models—and has surveyed the empirical evidence from defense acquisition studies, international procurement research, and cross-disciplinary analyses of procurement processes and outcomes.

The key findings of the review can be summarized as follows:

1. **Historical and regulatory context**: The tension between constraining discretion through price-based competition and enabling informed judgment through multi-factor evaluation is a defining feature of the U.S. procurement system, with regulatory reforms over the past three decades gradually expanding the use of best-value approaches (Section 2.1).

2. **Outcome frameworks**: Public value theory and value-for-money frameworks provide the conceptual foundation for evaluating procurement outcomes multidimensionally, moving beyond simple price comparisons to consider quality, timeliness, lifecycle cost, process integrity, and service reliability (Section 2.2).

3. **Theoretical predictions**: Economic theory—including auction theory, TCE, incomplete contract theory, and principal-agent models—converges on the prediction that price-only procurement mechanisms produce inferior outcomes for complex, uncertain, and difficult-to-specify acquisitions (Section 2.3).

4. **Empirical evidence**: The limited empirical literature consistently supports the theoretical predictions, finding that tradeoff methods are associated with better performance outcomes than LPTA, particularly for complex acquisitions—but the evidence base is narrow and methodologically limited (Section 2.4).

5. **Mediating and moderating factors**: Bid protests (Section 2.5), solicitation quality (Section 2.6), oversight burden (Section 2.7), strategic underbidding (Section 2.8), and incumbency advantage (Section 2.9) are critical factors that shape the pathway from source selection strategy to public value outcomes and that must be incorporated into empirical analyses.

6. **Significant gaps**: The literature exhibits major gaps in civilian agency evidence, cross-sector comparison, causal identification, composite outcome measurement, and mediating mechanism analysis—gaps that this dissertation directly addresses.

The conceptual model and hypotheses presented in Section 2.10 translate these findings into a testable framework that guides the empirical analysis described in the following chapter. By employing large-scale archival data from multiple federal agencies, propensity score matching for causal identification, and a composite public value index as the primary outcome measure, this dissertation provides the most comprehensive empirical test to date of the relationship between source selection strategy and public value in U.S. government procurement.
