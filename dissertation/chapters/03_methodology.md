# Chapter 3: Methodology

## 3.1 Overall Research Design

This chapter presents the research methodology employed to investigate whether best-value tradeoff source selection yields superior public-value outcomes compared with lowest-price technically acceptable (LPTA) source selection in U.S. federal government procurement. The study addresses a consequential policy question --- whether the evaluation method used to award competitive contracts systematically influences downstream performance, competition, and cost outcomes --- through rigorous analysis of administrative records generated by the federal procurement system itself.

### 3.1.1 Sequential Mixed-Methods Framework

The broader research agenda from which this dissertation emerges follows a sequential mixed-methods design (Creswell & Creswell, 2018). In this paradigm, an initial quantitative phase establishes empirical patterns across a large population of contract awards, and a subsequent qualitative phase explores the mechanisms, contextual factors, and practitioner rationales that underlie those patterns. The present dissertation constitutes **Phase 1** of this design: a quantitative archival analysis of federal contract data spanning fiscal years 2020 through 2025. Phase 2 --- comprising a practitioner survey of federal contracting officers and semi-structured interviews with acquisition leaders --- is noted as planned future work that will require a separate Institutional Review Board (IRB) application and is beyond the scope of this document.

The decision to focus the dissertation on Phase 1 reflects several considerations. First, the quantitative archival analysis is self-contained and capable of generating meaningful contributions to both theory and policy. The hypotheses derived in Chapter 2 concern observable, measurable relationships between source selection method and procurement outcomes. Testing these hypotheses requires statistical analysis of contract data, not interview transcripts. Second, the archival approach leverages a data infrastructure that has only recently become viable. The Defense Procurement and Acquisition Policy (DPAP) and the Office of Federal Procurement Policy's Data Procedures Committee (DPC) memorandum of 2020 mandated the reporting of source selection process type in the Federal Procurement Data System (FPDS), creating a field that enables the core comparison at the heart of this study. The window of data availability --- roughly five to six fiscal years at the time of analysis --- is sufficiently large for cross-sectional and panel estimation but still new enough that the scholarly literature has not yet exploited it. Third, grounding the qualitative phase in quantitative findings ensures that practitioner interviews can be designed to probe specific empirical patterns rather than abstract opinions, strengthening the sequential logic of the mixed-methods architecture.

### 3.1.2 Justification for the Archival Quantitative Approach

Archival research designs occupy a well-established position in public administration, management, and economics scholarship (Shadish et al., 2002). When administrative records are generated as a byproduct of routine government operations, they offer several advantages over primary data collection. The records are comprehensive: USAspending.gov contains data on virtually every federal contract action above the micro-purchase threshold, providing near-population coverage rather than a sample. They are contemporaneous: procurement data are reported at or near the time of award and modification, reducing the recall bias inherent in surveys. They are standardized: FPDS data elements follow government-wide data dictionaries, enabling cross-agency comparisons that would be infeasible through ad hoc survey instruments. And they are publicly available: the transparency mandates of the Federal Funding Accountability and Transparency Act (FFATA) of 2006 and the DATA Act of 2014 ensure that these records can be accessed without restricted-use agreements, facilitating replication.

At the same time, archival data impose constraints that must be acknowledged at the outset. The analyst cannot control the data-generating process, meaning that the variables available for measurement are limited to those that agencies chose (or were required) to report. Outcome measurement is circumscribed: cost growth and modification patterns are observable, but subjective assessments of quality, timeliness, and mission contribution are not available in public records. And the assignment of the treatment of interest --- the choice between LPTA and tradeoff source selection --- is endogenous. Agencies select their evaluation method in light of the same factors (complexity, risk, market conditions) that may independently influence outcomes. The identification strategy described in Section 3.5 addresses this endogeneity through propensity score matching, within-category comparisons, and difference-in-differences estimation, but the observational nature of the data means that causal claims require careful qualification.

These trade-offs notwithstanding, the archival approach is the most appropriate design for the research questions at hand. A randomized experiment in which agencies are assigned to use LPTA or tradeoff on comparable procurements would be the ideal identification strategy but is infeasible in practice: the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) requires agencies to select their evaluation method based on the characteristics of each procurement, not at random. A purely qualitative approach would lack the statistical power to detect systematic differences across thousands of contract awards. A survey-only approach would suffer from low response rates among federal contracting officers, social desirability bias in self-reported outcomes, and an inability to link responses to specific contract performance data. The archival design provides the scale, objectivity, and replicability needed to advance the empirical literature on source selection policy.

### 3.1.3 Research Questions Revisited

The methodology is organized around the following research questions, which correspond to the hypotheses developed in Chapter 2:

1. Do contracts awarded through best-value tradeoff source selection exhibit different cost growth patterns compared with contracts awarded through LPTA?
2. Do tradeoff-awarded contracts experience different modification intensity than LPTA-awarded contracts?
3. Does the choice of source selection method affect the intensity of competition, measured by the number of offers received and the probability of a single-bid outcome?
4. Do these relationships vary by procurement complexity, as proxied by NAICS sector, contract type, and indefinite delivery vehicle status?

Each question admits a null hypothesis (no systematic difference) and a directional alternative informed by the theoretical framework of Chapter 2. The statistical models developed in Sections 3.6 and 3.7 test these hypotheses while controlling for confounding factors and addressing selection bias.

## 3.2 Data Sources

The empirical analysis draws on three categories of data: (a) contract-level procurement records from USAspending.gov and the underlying FPDS extracts, which constitute the primary dataset; (b) aggregate bid protest statistics from the U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO), which provide contextual evidence on the protest environment; and (c) the Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS), which is discussed as a noted limitation and motivation for future research rather than a data source available for this study.

### 3.2.1 Archival Procurement Data

#### USAspending.gov and the Federal Procurement Data System

The primary dataset for this dissertation is drawn from USAspending.gov, the public-facing portal mandated by FFATA and the DATA Act. USAspending.gov aggregates data from multiple federal financial systems, but the procurement records originate in the Federal Procurement Data System --- Next Generation (FPDS-NG), the government-wide database that captures detailed information on every contract action above the micro-purchase threshold ($10,000 for most agencies as of FY2025). FPDS data are reported by contracting officers at the time of award or modification and are subject to agency-level quality review and periodic government-wide data validation exercises.

USAspending.gov provides two primary mechanisms for accessing procurement data: a browser-based search and download interface and a RESTful Application Programming Interface (API). This study uses both mechanisms. Bulk extracts of award and subaward data are downloaded through the Custom Award Data Download feature, which produces comma-separated value (CSV) files at the transaction level. These bulk files contain the universe of contract transactions for specified fiscal years, agencies, and award types. The API is used for targeted queries, data validation, and supplementary pulls of specific data elements not included in the standard bulk extracts.

#### Source Selection Process Field

The variable of central importance to this study --- the source selection process used to evaluate offerors --- was added to FPDS reporting requirements effective fiscal year 2020. The addition followed a memorandum from the Data Procedures Committee (DPC), which recognized that the existing FPDS data schema captured whether a contract was competed but not how it was competed. The new field, `source_selection_process`, contains the following valid values:

- **LPTA** (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable): The offeror that submits the lowest-priced proposal meeting the minimum technical requirements receives the award. No credit is given for exceeding technical thresholds.
- **TO** (Tradeoff): The agency evaluates proposals on the basis of price and non-price factors (e.g., technical approach, past performance, management capability) and may award to other than the lowest-priced offeror if the additional value justifies the price premium. This is synonymous with "best-value tradeoff" in the FAR.
- **O** (Other): A residual category for evaluation approaches that do not fit cleanly into LPTA or tradeoff, or for awards where the source selection process is not reported.

The availability of this field makes the present study possible. Prior to 2020, researchers seeking to distinguish LPTA from tradeoff awards had to rely on manual coding of solicitation documents, text analysis of contract descriptions, or agency-specific datasets --- approaches that limited sample size and introduced measurement error. The FPDS field provides a standardized, government-wide classification that can be merged with the full suite of contract variables.

#### Variables Available

The FPDS extracts accessible through USAspending.gov contain hundreds of data elements. The variables relevant to this study are listed below, grouped by their role in the analysis. A formal operationalization table appears in Section 3.4.

**Award Identification and Structure:**
- `award_id_piid`: Procurement Instrument Identifier, the unique contract number
- `award_id_parent_award_piid`: Parent award identifier for task and delivery orders
- `modification_number`: Sequential identifier for contract modifications
- `action_date`: Date of the contract action (award or modification)
- `period_of_performance_start_date` and `period_of_performance_current_end_date`
- `type_of_contract_pricing`: Pricing arrangement (firm fixed price, cost-plus-fixed-fee, time and materials, etc.)
- `idv_type`: Indefinite delivery vehicle type, if applicable

**Competition and Source Selection:**
- `source_selection_process`: LPTA, TO, or O (the key independent variable)
- `extent_competed`: Competition status (full and open, full and open after exclusion of sources, not competed, etc.)
- `number_of_offers_received`: Count of proposals submitted by offerors
- `type_of_set_aside`: Small business set-aside category, if applicable

**Financial Data:**
- `federal_action_obligation`: Dollar amount obligated for each transaction
- `total_dollars_obligated`: Cumulative obligations on the contract
- `base_and_all_options_value`: Total potential value including all options
- `base_exercised_options_value`: Value of the base contract and exercised options

**Classification:**
- `naics_code`: North American Industry Classification System code (6-digit)
- `product_or_service_code`: PSC code identifying the type of product or service
- `awarding_agency_name` and `awarding_agency_code`
- `awarding_sub_agency_name` and `awarding_sub_agency_code`

**Contractor Information:**
- `recipient_name`: Legal name of the awardee
- `recipient_uei`: Unique Entity Identifier
- `business_types`: Small business, woman-owned, veteran-owned, etc.

These variables are extracted at the transaction (action) level and aggregated to the award level as described in Section 3.3.

### 3.2.2 GAO Bid Protest Statistics

The U.S. Government Accountability Office publishes an annual report on bid protests filed under the Competition in Contracting Act (CICA). These reports provide aggregate statistics on the number of protests filed, the number of cases decided on the merits, sustain rates (the proportion of decided cases where GAO sustained the protest), and effectiveness rates (the proportion of cases where the protester obtained some form of relief, including voluntary corrective action by the agency). The reports cover each fiscal year and provide breakdowns by agency and protest outcome.

GAO protest data serve a contextual rather than primary-analytic function in this study. The reports document the overall protest environment --- whether protest activity is increasing or decreasing, which agencies face the most protests, and whether the mix of protest grounds is shifting --- but they do not link individual protests to specific contract numbers or source selection methods. The inability to match protests to individual awards at the contract level means that protest-related hypotheses can be examined only at the aggregate level (e.g., whether years or agencies with higher tradeoff usage exhibit different protest rates). This limitation is discussed further in Section 3.10.

The GAO report data are collected manually from the annual publications posted on GAO's website (gao.gov). Because the data are aggregate and published in tabular form, extraction involves straightforward transcription into a structured dataset. Each fiscal year's report provides approximately one page of relevant summary statistics.

### 3.2.3 CPARS (Noted Limitation)

The Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS) is the federal government's primary repository for post-award evaluations of contractor performance. Contracting officers and their designated representatives use CPARS to rate contractors on dimensions including quality, schedule, cost control, management, and small business subcontracting. Ratings follow a standardized scale: Exceptional, Very Good, Satisfactory, Marginal, and Unsatisfactory. These evaluations are stored in the Past Performance Information Retrieval System (PPIRS) and are used by agencies in evaluating past performance during source selection for future awards.

CPARS data would be the ideal dependent variable for testing whether tradeoff source selection produces higher-quality contractor performance. However, CPARS records are not publicly available. Access is restricted to federal government personnel with a need to know, and the data are explicitly excluded from FOIA disclosure under exemptions for pre-decisional and procurement-sensitive information. No mechanism exists for academic researchers to obtain CPARS data without an interagency agreement or direct collaboration with a government agency.

The unavailability of CPARS data represents the most significant measurement limitation of this study. In the absence of direct performance ratings, the analysis relies on indirect proxies: cost growth (the degree to which the final contract value exceeds the initial value) and modification intensity (the frequency of contract changes, which may reflect scope instability, performance deficiencies, or changed requirements). These proxies capture important dimensions of contract performance but do not substitute for the nuanced, multidimensional assessments contained in CPARS. This limitation is acknowledged throughout the analysis and identified as a primary motivation for future research partnerships with government agencies that could provide access to CPARS data under appropriate data-sharing agreements.

## 3.3 Sampling Frame and Inclusion Criteria

### 3.3.1 Population

The target population for this study is the universe of U.S. federal contract actions recorded in FPDS for fiscal years 2020 through 2025 (October 1, 2019, through September 30, 2025). This six-year window corresponds to the period during which the `source_selection_process` field has been required in FPDS reporting, ensuring that the key independent variable is available for all observations. The population encompasses all executive branch agencies that report to FPDS, including the Department of Defense (DoD), civilian agencies, and independent establishments.

### 3.3.2 Inclusion Criteria

From this population, the analytic sample is constructed by applying the following inclusion criteria:

1. **Above the Simplified Acquisition Threshold (SAT).** Only contract awards with a total base-and-all-options value exceeding $250,000 are included. The SAT --- set at $250,000 by the 2018 National Defense Authorization Act --- defines the threshold above which full competition, source selection, and documentation requirements apply. Below the SAT, agencies may use simplified acquisition procedures that do not involve formal source selection evaluation; the distinction between LPTA and tradeoff is therefore less meaningful for smaller awards. Restricting the sample to above-SAT awards ensures that the source selection process field captures a deliberate evaluation decision rather than a procedural default.

2. **Competitive procurements.** Only awards where `extent_competed` equals "Full and Open Competition" or "Full and Open after Exclusion of Sources" are included. This criterion excludes sole-source awards, awards resulting from unsolicited proposals, and other noncompetitive actions. The rationale is that source selection method is a concept that applies only when multiple offerors compete; in a sole-source environment, there is no "selection" among alternatives.

3. **Service and IT NAICS codes.** The sample is restricted to awards classified under NAICS codes in the following sectors:
   - **541xxx** --- Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services (management consulting, computer systems design, research and development, engineering services, accounting, legal services, and related categories)
   - **518xxx** --- Data Processing, Hosting, and Related Services (cloud computing, data center operations, web hosting)
   - **511xxx** --- Software Publishers (software development, software licensing)
   - **561xxx** --- Administrative and Support Services (facilities support, document preparation, call centers, staffing services)

   These sectors were selected because they represent the contract categories where the LPTA-versus-tradeoff decision is most policy-relevant and most contested. Service contracts involve substantial human capital, intellectual contribution, and management complexity, making the choice between evaluating on price alone (LPTA) versus evaluating on price and quality (tradeoff) consequential. Commodity supply contracts --- where product specifications are standardized and differentiation among offerors is minimal --- present a different procurement context in which LPTA may be the normatively appropriate choice. By focusing on professional services and IT, the study examines the domain where the theoretical arguments for tradeoff source selection are strongest and where policy debates have been most active.

4. **Source selection process = LPTA or TO (Tradeoff).** Only awards with a valid `source_selection_process` code of LPTA or TO are included in the primary analysis. Awards coded as "O" (Other) or with a missing/null source selection process field are excluded from the main sample. The "Other" category is heterogeneous, potentially encompassing lowest-price methods that do not meet the technical definition of LPTA, subjective tradeoff methods that agencies chose not to classify, and data entry errors. Including this category would introduce measurement noise. Awards with missing values may reflect non-reporting by specific agencies or data quality gaps. Both categories are examined in robustness checks (Section 3.8) but are excluded from the primary specification.

### 3.3.3 Exclusion Criteria

The following categories of awards are excluded from the analytic sample:

- **Commodity supply purchases.** Awards under NAICS codes outside the four sectors specified above are excluded, as discussed in inclusion criterion 3.
- **Sole-source awards.** Awards where `extent_competed` indicates no competition are excluded, as discussed in inclusion criterion 2.
- **Below-SAT awards.** Awards with total value below $250,000 are excluded, as discussed in inclusion criterion 1.
- **"Other" and missing source selection.** Awards with `source_selection_process` = "O" or null are excluded from the primary analysis, as discussed in inclusion criterion 4.

### 3.3.4 Unit of Analysis

The unit of analysis is the **individual contract award**, aggregated from transaction-level records. FPDS data are recorded at the transaction level: each contract action --- initial award, funding modification, administrative modification, option exercise, cost adjustment --- generates a separate record. A single contract may have dozens or hundreds of associated transactions over its lifecycle. For the purposes of this study, transactions sharing the same `award_id_piid` (and, for task orders, the same `award_id_piid` and `award_id_parent_award_piid` combination) are aggregated to a single observation representing the contract award.

Aggregation follows these rules:

- **Source selection process** is taken from the initial award action (the record with the earliest `action_date` and `modification_number` = 0 or the lowest modification number).
- **Financial variables** are computed as of the most recent transaction: `total_dollars_obligated` and `base_and_all_options_value` reflect the cumulative contract value after all recorded modifications.
- **Base value** is taken from the initial award action to establish the denominator for cost growth calculations.
- **Number of offers received** is taken from the initial award action.
- **Modification count** is computed as the total number of distinct transactions (excluding the initial award) associated with the contract identifier.
- **Period of performance** is taken from the most recent transaction to capture any extensions.
- **Classification variables** (NAICS, PSC, agency) are taken from the initial award action, as these should not change across modifications.

This aggregation approach is consistent with standard practice in the federal procurement research literature (e.g., Carril & Duggan, 2020; Liebman & Mahoney, 2017) and reflects the fact that the contract award --- not the individual transaction --- is the meaningful unit at which source selection decisions are made and outcomes accrue.

### 3.3.5 Expected Sample Size

Based on preliminary exploration of USAspending.gov data for FY2020--FY2024, the expected sample size after applying all inclusion criteria is approximately 80,000 to 150,000 contract awards. The wide range reflects uncertainty about the proportion of records with valid `source_selection_process` coding, as reporting compliance has improved over time but remains imperfect. Even at the lower bound, this sample size provides substantial statistical power for detecting economically meaningful effect sizes across the primary models. A formal power analysis is presented in Section 3.6.4.

### 3.4.1 Independent Variable: Source Selection Method

The independent variable of primary interest is the source selection method, operationalized as a binary indicator equal to 1 if the contract was awarded using best-value tradeoff (TO) evaluation and 0 if the contract was awarded using LPTA. This binary coding reflects the fundamental policy distinction at the heart of the study: LPTA awards to the lowest-priced technically acceptable offeror, while tradeoff awards may go to a higher-priced offeror whose non-price merits justify the premium.

The binary operationalization excludes the "Other" category, which is heterogeneous and difficult to interpret. Sensitivity analyses examine whether including "Other" as a third category (via multinomial models) or collapsing "Other" into LPTA or tradeoff changes the results.

### 3.4.2 Dependent Variables

#### Cost Growth

Cost growth is the primary continuous outcome variable. It measures the degree to which the final (or current) contract value exceeds the initial base value at the time of award. Cost growth is computed as:

$$\text{Cost Growth}_i = \frac{V_{i,\text{current}} - V_{i,\text{base}}}{V_{i,\text{base}}}$$

where $V_{i,\text{current}}$ is the `base_and_all_options_value` as of the most recent contract action and $V_{i,\text{base}}$ is the `base_exercised_options_value` recorded at the initial award. A value of 0 indicates no cost growth; a value of 0.25 indicates that the contract value grew by 25% relative to the initial base.

Cost growth captures an important dimension of contract performance: the degree to which the final cost to the government exceeded the expected cost at award. Positive cost growth may reflect scope changes, design deficiencies, poor cost estimation, contractor performance problems, or changed government requirements. While not all cost growth is undesirable --- legitimate scope additions represent new value --- systematic differences in cost growth between LPTA and tradeoff awards are informative about the downstream consequences of source selection method.

Cost growth distributions are typically right-skewed, with a small number of contracts experiencing very large overruns. To mitigate the influence of extreme outliers, the variable is **winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles**: values below the 1st percentile are set to the 1st percentile value, and values above the 99th percentile are set to the 99th percentile value. This approach preserves the ordering and relative magnitude of observations while capping the influence of extreme values that may reflect data errors or idiosyncratic circumstances (e.g., emergency contract expansions during the COVID-19 pandemic). Sensitivity analyses examine alternative winsorization thresholds (5th/95th percentile) and trimming (dropping extreme observations entirely).

#### Modification Intensity

Modification intensity is measured as the count of contract modifications recorded for each award. Each transaction in FPDS with a modification number greater than zero represents a contract modification. Modifications encompass a wide range of actions: funding adjustments, option exercises, scope changes, no-cost extensions, administrative changes, and terminations. The count of modifications serves as a proxy for administrative burden and contract instability.

A higher modification count, holding contract size and duration constant, suggests greater post-award management complexity. Contracts that require frequent modifications impose transaction costs on both the government and the contractor and may indicate that the initial contract terms were poorly specified or that performance problems required corrective action.

Because modification count is a non-negative integer with potential overdispersion (the variance may exceed the mean), it is modeled using negative binomial regression rather than ordinary least squares. An exposure offset for contract duration (measured in months from start to current end date) is included to account for the fact that longer contracts mechanically accumulate more modifications.

#### Competition Intensity

Competition intensity is measured by `number_of_offers_received`, the count of proposals submitted by offerors in response to the solicitation. This variable is reported by the contracting officer at the time of award. A higher number of offers suggests a more robust competitive environment, which tends to improve price outcomes and contractor quality through market pressure.

The relationship between source selection method and competition intensity is theoretically ambiguous. LPTA's simplicity may attract more offerors (lower bid preparation costs), but tradeoff's ability to reward quality may attract higher-quality offerors or encourage more firms to compete because they perceive a fair chance of winning on non-price factors. The empirical analysis tests this relationship without imposing a directional prior.

Like modification count, the number of offers received is a count variable that is modeled using negative binomial or Poisson regression, depending on the degree of overdispersion observed in the data. Poisson regression is used if the conditional mean and variance are approximately equal; negative binomial is used if overdispersion is present, as tested by likelihood ratio or Lagrange multiplier tests (Cameron & Trivedi, 2005).

#### Single-Bid Indicator

The single-bid indicator is a binary variable equal to 1 if only one offer was received and 0 if two or more offers were received. Single-bid contracts --- sometimes termed "competitions of one" --- represent a failure of the competitive process. Even when the solicitation is issued as a full-and-open competition, a single-bid outcome means that the government lacks the market pressure of competing alternatives. Single-bid contracts have been associated with higher prices, reduced innovation, and diminished contractor accountability in prior research.

This variable is modeled using logistic regression. The coefficient of interest captures the difference in the log-odds of a single-bid outcome between tradeoff and LPTA awards, controlling for other factors.

### 3.4.3 Moderating Variables

#### Procurement Complexity (NAICS Sector)

Procurement complexity is proxied by the NAICS sector of the contract award. Four categories are defined based on the included NAICS codes:

1. **IT Services** (NAICS 518xxx, 511xxx): Cloud computing, data processing, software development, and related services. These procurements involve rapidly evolving technologies, high technical risk, and substantial vendor differentiation.
2. **Management and Technical Consulting** (NAICS 5416, 5417): Management consulting and scientific/technical consulting. These engagements require professional judgment, domain expertise, and customized deliverables.
3. **Other Professional Services** (remaining NAICS 541xxx): Engineering, R&D, accounting, legal, and other professional services. Complexity varies within this category but is generally moderate to high.
4. **Administrative Support** (NAICS 561xxx): Facilities support, staffing, document preparation, and call center operations. These services are relatively standardized and may be more amenable to LPTA evaluation.

The theoretical expectation is that the advantage of tradeoff over LPTA (if any) is larger for more complex procurements, where quality differentiation among offerors is greater and the risk of selecting a low-quality provider at the lowest price is highest.

#### Contract Type

Contract pricing type serves as both a moderator and a control variable. The FAR defines several pricing arrangements, which are grouped into four categories for this analysis:

1. **Firm Fixed Price (FFP):** The contractor agrees to deliver the specified scope at a fixed price, bearing the full risk of cost overruns. FFP contracts are used when requirements are well-defined and the technical risk is low.
2. **Cost-Plus-Fixed-Fee (CPFF):** The government reimburses the contractor's allowable costs plus a fixed fee. Used when requirements are uncertain and cost risk is high.
3. **Time and Materials (T&M):** The government pays fixed hourly rates for labor and reimburses materials at cost. Used when the scope of work cannot be precisely defined.
4. **Other:** All remaining pricing types, including cost-plus-incentive-fee, cost-plus-award-fee, and fixed-price-incentive.

Cost-type and T&M contracts signal greater complexity and uncertainty. The interaction between contract type and source selection method tests whether the tradeoff advantage varies with the degree of contractual risk-sharing.

#### IDV Indicator

The indefinite delivery vehicle (IDV) indicator is a binary variable equal to 1 if the contract is a task order or delivery order issued under an indefinite delivery/indefinite quantity (IDIQ) contract or a Government-Wide Acquisition Contract (GWAC), and 0 if it is a standalone (definitive) contract. IDV orders represent a growing share of federal procurement and involve a two-stage competition process: vendors first compete for a place on the IDV, and then compete again (or are directed) for individual task orders. The source selection process for the task order may differ from the source selection process used for the parent IDV.

### 3.4.4 Control Variables

#### Log(Award Amount)

Contract size is measured as the natural logarithm of the initial base award value (`base_exercised_options_value` at the time of the initial award action). The logarithmic transformation addresses the severe right skew of contract dollar values and allows the regression coefficients to be interpreted in terms of proportional (rather than absolute) changes in value. Contract size is controlled because larger contracts may exhibit systematically different cost growth patterns, modification frequencies, and competitive dynamics.

#### Agency Fixed Effects

Agency fixed effects are included as a set of dummy variables, one for each unique awarding agency (with one agency serving as the reference category). These fixed effects absorb all time-invariant, agency-specific factors that might influence both the choice of source selection method and procurement outcomes: agency culture, workforce expertise, mission complexity, risk tolerance, organizational structure, and regulatory interpretation. By comparing LPTA and tradeoff awards *within* the same agency, the analysis controls for unobserved agency heterogeneity that cannot be captured by observable covariates.

#### Fiscal Year Fixed Effects

Fiscal year fixed effects (dummy variables for each fiscal year in the sample) control for government-wide temporal trends: changes in the procurement regulatory environment, shifts in the overall budget climate, macroeconomic conditions (e.g., the COVID-19 pandemic's impact on the contractor workforce), and secular trends in source selection practice. The interaction of fiscal year fixed effects with agency fixed effects (agency-by-year fixed effects) is examined in robustness checks as a more stringent specification.

#### Set-Aside Type

Set-aside type is a categorical variable indicating whether the procurement was set aside for small businesses or a specific socioeconomic category (8(a) Business Development, HUBZone, Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business, Women-Owned Small Business) or was unrestricted (open to large and small businesses alike). Set-aside status is controlled because it shapes the competitive pool: set-aside contracts restrict competition to a subset of potential offerors, which may influence both the number of offers received and the quality of the competitive outcome.

## 3.5 Identification Strategy and the Threat of Selection Bias

### 3.5.1 The Fundamental Identification Challenge

The central methodological challenge in this study is that agencies do not randomly assign source selection methods to procurements. The FAR directs contracting officers to select the evaluation method that is most appropriate for the specific procurement, considering factors such as complexity, risk, market conditions, and the availability of performance benchmarks. This means that the "treatment" --- the use of tradeoff versus LPTA --- is endogenous: the same factors that drive the choice of source selection method may independently influence the outcomes of interest. More complex procurements may be systematically routed to tradeoff evaluation, and complexity itself may cause higher cost growth, more modifications, or different competitive dynamics. A naive comparison of outcomes between LPTA and tradeoff awards would confound the effect of the source selection method with the effect of procurement characteristics that drove the method choice.

Formally, the concern is that:

$$E[Y_i | \text{Tradeoff}_i = 1] - E[Y_i | \text{Tradeoff}_i = 0] \neq E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]$$

where $Y_i(1)$ and $Y_i(0)$ denote the potential outcomes under tradeoff and LPTA, respectively. The left-hand side is the observed difference in means; the right-hand side is the average treatment effect (ATE). The two differ whenever the assignment to tradeoff is correlated with unobserved determinants of outcomes.

To address this challenge, the study employs three complementary identification strategies: (a) within-category comparisons using agency, NAICS/PSC, and fiscal year fixed effects; (b) propensity score matching (PSM); and (c) difference-in-differences (DiD) estimation exploiting policy shocks. No single strategy is definitive, but convergence of results across strategies strengthens confidence in the findings.

### 3.5.2 Within-Category Comparisons

The simplest and most transparent approach to addressing selection bias is to compare LPTA and tradeoff awards *within narrow categories* defined by agency, NAICS sector, fiscal year, and contract type. By conditioning on these observable characteristics through fixed effects and interaction terms, the analysis compares contracts that are similar in their observable determinants of source selection method. The specification:

$$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Tradeoff}_i + \gamma \mathbf{X}_i + \alpha_{\text{agency}} + \delta_{\text{year}} + \theta_{\text{NAICS}} + \varepsilon_i$$

estimates $\beta_1$ as the within-category difference in outcomes between tradeoff and LPTA awards, conditional on the vector of controls $\mathbf{X}_i$ and the fixed effects. The identifying assumption is that, within a given agency, NAICS sector, and fiscal year, the assignment to tradeoff versus LPTA is uncorrelated with unobserved determinants of outcomes. This assumption is strong --- agencies may still route individual procurements within a category differently based on contract-specific factors not captured by the covariates --- but the fixed effects absorb a substantial share of the confounding variation.

More demanding specifications include agency-by-year fixed effects ($\alpha_{\text{agency} \times \text{year}}$) and agency-by-NAICS fixed effects ($\alpha_{\text{agency} \times \text{NAICS}}$), which restrict the comparison to within agency-year cells or within agency-NAICS cells. The trade-off is that finer fixed effects reduce the variation available for estimation and may reduce precision.

### 3.5.3 Propensity Score Matching

Propensity score matching (PSM) provides a more structured approach to constructing comparable treatment and control groups (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). The propensity score is the conditional probability of receiving the treatment (tradeoff) given observed covariates:

$$e(\mathbf{X}_i) = P(\text{Tradeoff}_i = 1 | \mathbf{X}_i)$$

Under the assumption that treatment assignment is independent of potential outcomes conditional on the propensity score (the "ignorability" or "unconfoundedness" assumption), matching treated and control units with similar propensity scores eliminates selection bias on observables.

#### Estimation of the Propensity Score

The propensity score is estimated using logistic regression with the following covariates:

$$\text{logit}[P(\text{Tradeoff}_i = 1)] = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \ln(\text{Award Amount}_i) + \alpha_2 \text{NAICS Sector}_i + \alpha_3 \text{Agency}_i + \alpha_4 \text{Fiscal Year}_i + \alpha_5 \text{Contract Type}_i + \alpha_6 \text{IDV}_i + \alpha_7 \text{Set-Aside Type}_i$$

These covariates are selected because they represent the primary observable factors that contracting officers consider when choosing a source selection method. The logistic model produces a predicted probability (propensity score) for each observation in the sample.

#### Matching Algorithm

The study employs **nearest-neighbor matching without replacement** with a caliper constraint. Each tradeoff (treated) observation is matched to the LPTA (control) observation with the closest propensity score, subject to the constraint that the absolute difference in propensity scores (on the logit scale) does not exceed 0.2 standard deviations of the logit propensity score distribution. The caliper prevents poor matches: treated units without a sufficiently close control match are dropped from the analysis. Matching without replacement ensures that each control unit is used at most once, reducing bias from overuse of a few control observations at the cost of potentially discarding some treated units.

Alternative matching specifications --- including one-to-many matching (1:3 and 1:5), matching with replacement, kernel matching, and Mahalanobis distance matching on key covariates --- are estimated as robustness checks.

#### Balance Diagnostics

The quality of the matched sample is assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs) for each covariate. Following the convention established by Rosenbaum and Rubin (1985) and widely adopted in the causal inference literature, an SMD below 0.1 (10% of a standard deviation) is considered acceptable balance. Variance ratios for continuous covariates should fall between 0.5 and 2.0 (Rubin, 2001). Visual diagnostics include love plots (dot plots of SMDs before and after matching) and histograms of propensity score distributions in the treated and control groups before and after matching. If balance is inadequate for specific covariates, the propensity score model is re-specified --- for example, by adding interaction terms, polynomial terms, or additional covariates --- until acceptable balance is achieved.

#### Treatment Effect Estimation on the Matched Sample

On the matched sample, treatment effects are estimated using the same regression models described in Section 3.6, with the addition of matching weights. The average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) is the primary estimand:

$$\text{ATT} = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) | \text{Tradeoff}_i = 1]$$

This estimand captures the expected difference in outcomes for tradeoff contracts relative to what would have been observed had those same contracts been awarded using LPTA. The ATT is the most policy-relevant estimand because it addresses the question: for contracts where agencies chose tradeoff, would outcomes have been different under LPTA?

#### Sensitivity Analysis: Rosenbaum Bounds

The PSM approach addresses selection on observables but cannot rule out selection on unobservables --- that is, unmeasured factors that jointly influence source selection choice and outcomes. To assess the sensitivity of the PSM results to potential hidden bias, the study employs Rosenbaum bounds (Rosenbaum, 2002). This method computes the critical value of $\Gamma$ --- the factor by which the odds of treatment assignment may differ between matched pairs due to an unobserved confounder --- at which the estimated treatment effect becomes statistically insignificant. A large critical $\Gamma$ (e.g., $\Gamma > 2$) indicates that the result is robust to substantial hidden bias; a small critical $\Gamma$ (e.g., $\Gamma < 1.5$) indicates that even modest unmeasured confounding could explain away the finding.

### 3.5.4 Difference-in-Differences Estimation

The difference-in-differences (DiD) design exploits temporal variation in the adoption of tradeoff source selection to identify the causal effect of interest (Angrist & Pischke, 2009). Unlike PSM, which relies on cross-sectional comparisons of matched units, DiD leverages within-unit changes over time, controlling for both time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity and common temporal trends.

#### Potential Policy Shocks

Two categories of policy variation are candidates for DiD estimation:

1. **The 2020 DPC Memorandum.** The DPC memorandum that mandated `source_selection_process` reporting in FPDS was accompanied by broader guidance encouraging agencies to use the evaluation method best suited to each procurement. While the memorandum did not mandate a shift from LPTA to tradeoff, it increased the visibility of source selection decisions and may have created pressure on agencies to justify their choices. If the memorandum induced a shift in the mix of LPTA and tradeoff awards --- particularly among agencies that had previously defaulted to LPTA --- this shift can be exploited in a DiD framework.

2. **Agency-level policy shifts.** Individual agencies and sub-agencies have issued guidance at various times during the sample period encouraging or requiring the use of tradeoff evaluation for specific contract categories. For example, the Department of Defense's Better Buying Power initiative and subsequent acquisition reform efforts have shifted agency posture toward or away from LPTA for specific categories. If these shifts can be documented and dated, they provide quasi-experimental variation in the adoption of tradeoff source selection at the agency-category level.

#### Specification

The DiD model takes the following general form:

$$Y_{ict} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Post}_{ct} + \beta_2 \text{Treat}_{c} + \beta_3 (\text{Post}_{ct} \times \text{Treat}_{c}) + \gamma \mathbf{X}_{ict} + \alpha_c + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{ict}$$

where $i$ indexes contracts, $c$ indexes agency-category cells, and $t$ indexes fiscal years. $\text{Post}_{ct}$ is an indicator for the period after the policy shock in cell $c$, $\text{Treat}_{c}$ is an indicator for cells affected by the policy shock, and $\beta_3$ is the DiD estimand. Agency-category fixed effects $\alpha_c$ absorb time-invariant heterogeneity, and fiscal year fixed effects $\delta_t$ absorb common time trends.

#### Parallel Trends Assumption

The validity of the DiD design rests on the parallel trends assumption: in the absence of the policy shock, outcomes in treated and control cells would have followed parallel trajectories. This assumption is not directly testable but is assessed through pre-trend tests. The study examines whether pre-treatment trends in outcomes differ between treated and control cells by estimating event-study specifications:

$$Y_{ict} = \sum_{k=-K}^{K} \beta_k \cdot \mathbf{1}[t - t_c^* = k] \times \text{Treat}_c + \gamma \mathbf{X}_{ict} + \alpha_c + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{ict}$$

where $t_c^*$ is the year of the policy shock in cell $c$ and $k$ indexes years relative to the shock. If the estimated $\beta_k$ coefficients for $k < 0$ (pre-treatment periods) are close to zero and statistically insignificant, this provides supporting evidence for the parallel trends assumption. Significant pre-trends would undermine the DiD design and motivate alternative specifications or a focus on the PSM results.

#### Staggered Adoption

If the policy shock occurs at different times for different agency-category cells (staggered adoption), the standard two-way fixed effects (TWFE) DiD estimator may be biased in the presence of heterogeneous treatment effects (Goodman-Bacon, 2021; de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfoeuille, 2020). The study addresses this concern by employing recently developed estimators that are robust to staggered adoption and heterogeneous effects, including the Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) estimator and the Sun and Abraham (2021) interaction-weighted estimator. These methods provide group-time average treatment effects that avoid the problematic comparisons inherent in the TWFE estimator.

## 3.6 Primary Quantitative Models

This section specifies the regression models used to test the hypotheses derived in Chapter 2. Models are organized by the distributional characteristics of each dependent variable.

### 3.6.1 Continuous Outcome: Cost Growth

For the continuous cost growth variable, the primary specification is a generalized linear model (GLM) estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS) with robust standard errors clustered at the agency-NAICS level:

$$\text{Cost Growth}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Tradeoff}_i + \beta_2 \ln(\text{Award}_i) + \beta_3 \text{Contract Type}_i + \beta_4 \text{IDV}_i + \beta_5 \text{Set-Aside}_i + \alpha_{\text{agency}} + \delta_{\text{year}} + \varepsilon_i$$

The coefficient $\beta_1$ is the primary parameter of interest. It captures the average difference in cost growth between tradeoff and LPTA awards, conditional on the control variables and fixed effects. A negative $\beta_1$ would indicate that tradeoff awards experience less cost growth, consistent with the hypothesis that best-value source selection leads to better contractor selection and fewer post-award problems. A positive $\beta_1$ would indicate higher cost growth for tradeoff awards, potentially reflecting the greater complexity of procurements routed to tradeoff.

Standard errors are clustered at the agency-NAICS level to account for within-cluster correlation in the error term. Contracts awarded by the same agency in the same NAICS sector are likely to share unobserved factors (e.g., the expertise of the contracting team, the characteristics of the supplier market) that induce correlation among their error terms. Clustering at this level produces valid inference even in the presence of arbitrary within-cluster correlation (Cameron & Miller, 2015).

Robustness checks include: (a) two-way clustering by agency and fiscal year; (b) heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC3) standard errors; (c) quantile regression at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles to assess whether the treatment effect varies across the cost growth distribution; and (d) a Tobit specification to account for censoring at zero (many contracts exhibit no cost growth).

### 3.6.2 Count Outcomes: Modification Intensity and Competition Intensity

For count outcomes --- the number of modifications and the number of offers received --- the primary specification is the negative binomial regression model (Cameron & Trivedi, 2005):

$$\ln(E[\text{Count}_i | \mathbf{X}_i]) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Tradeoff}_i + \beta_2 \ln(\text{Award}_i) + \beta_3 \text{Contract Type}_i + \beta_4 \text{IDV}_i + \beta_5 \text{Set-Aside}_i + \alpha_{\text{agency}} + \delta_{\text{year}}$$

The negative binomial model is preferred over Poisson regression when the data exhibit overdispersion (the conditional variance exceeds the conditional mean). Overdispersion is common in count data and, if present, leads to understated standard errors and inflated significance levels under the Poisson model. The degree of overdispersion is assessed using the likelihood ratio test of $\alpha = 0$ in the negative binomial model, where $\alpha$ is the overdispersion parameter.

For the modification count model, an **exposure offset** for contract duration (measured in months) is included:

$$\ln(E[\text{Mods}_i | \mathbf{X}_i]) = \ln(\text{Duration}_i) + \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Tradeoff}_i + \cdots$$

The offset constrains the coefficient on log(duration) to 1, effectively modeling the modification *rate* (modifications per month) rather than the raw count. This accounts for the mechanical relationship between contract length and modification accumulation.

Coefficients in the negative binomial model are interpreted as log rate ratios. Exponentiated coefficients, $\exp(\beta_1)$, represent the incidence rate ratio (IRR) --- the multiplicative factor by which the expected count differs for tradeoff relative to LPTA awards. An IRR of 0.85 would indicate that tradeoff awards have 15% fewer modifications (or offers) on average.

### 3.6.3 Binary Outcome: Single-Bid Indicator

For the binary single-bid outcome, the primary specification is logistic regression:

$$\text{logit}[P(\text{Single-Bid}_i = 1)] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Tradeoff}_i + \beta_2 \ln(\text{Award}_i) + \beta_3 \text{Contract Type}_i + \beta_4 \text{IDV}_i + \beta_5 \text{Set-Aside}_i + \alpha_{\text{agency}} + \delta_{\text{year}}$$

The exponentiated coefficient $\exp(\beta_1)$ is the odds ratio for a single-bid outcome comparing tradeoff to LPTA awards. For rare events (if single-bid outcomes occur in fewer than 10% of observations), the odds ratio approximates the relative risk. Average marginal effects are reported alongside odds ratios to provide interpretable estimates of the absolute difference in single-bid probability.

A potential concern with logistic regression and fixed effects is the incidental parameters problem: in panels with many fixed effects and few observations per group, maximum likelihood estimates of the fixed effects are inconsistent, which can bias the coefficients of interest (Neyman & Scott, 1948). This concern is mitigated in the present study by the large number of observations per agency-year cell. Nevertheless, conditional logistic regression (which conditions out the fixed effects) is estimated as a robustness check for specifications with fine-grained fixed effects.

### 3.6.4 Power Analysis

A formal power analysis is conducted to determine the minimum detectable effect size given the expected sample size, the significance level ($\alpha = 0.05$), and the desired power ($1 - \beta = 0.80$). For the OLS model with cost growth as the dependent variable, the minimum detectable effect size depends on the residual variance after absorbing fixed effects and controls, the proportion of observations in the treatment group, and the sample size.

With an expected sample of 80,000 to 150,000 observations and assuming (conservatively) that 40% of the sample uses tradeoff and 60% uses LPTA, the study has power to detect differences in cost growth on the order of 1 to 2 percentage points --- well below the economically meaningful threshold of 5 percentage points that would be relevant for policy purposes. For count models and logistic regression, the corresponding minimum detectable effects are even smaller due to the efficiency gains from modeling the appropriate distributional family.

### 3.6.5 Multi-Level Models

An alternative modeling framework considers the hierarchical structure of the data: contracts are nested within agencies and within NAICS categories. Multi-level (hierarchical linear) models with random intercepts capture this nesting explicitly (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002):

**Level 1 (Contract):**
$$Y_{ij} = \beta_{0j} + \beta_1 \text{Tradeoff}_{ij} + \gamma \mathbf{X}_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}$$

**Level 2 (Agency):**
$$\beta_{0j} = \gamma_{00} + u_{0j}$$

where $j$ indexes agencies, $u_{0j} \sim N(0, \tau^2)$ is the random agency intercept, and $\varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$ is the residual. The random intercept allows each agency to have its own baseline outcome level, decomposing the total variance into between-agency and within-agency components.

Cross-classified models extend this framework to accommodate the fact that contracts are nested within both agencies and NAICS categories, which are not hierarchically related. The cross-classified random effects model (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002) allows random intercepts for both agency and NAICS:

$$Y_{ijk} = \gamma_{000} + \beta_1 \text{Tradeoff}_{ijk} + \gamma \mathbf{X}_{ijk} + u_{0j} + v_{0k} + \varepsilon_{ijk}$$

where $j$ indexes agencies, $k$ indexes NAICS categories, and $u_{0j}$ and $v_{0k}$ are the random intercepts. Cross-level interactions between source selection method and agency-level variables (e.g., agency procurement volume) or NAICS-level variables (e.g., average complexity) test whether the treatment effect varies systematically across clusters.

The multi-level approach is complementary to the fixed effects approach. Fixed effects provide the most robust control for unobserved cluster heterogeneity but do not allow estimation of cluster-level predictors (since they are absorbed by the fixed effects). Multi-level models allow estimation of cross-level interactions and variance decomposition but impose distributional assumptions on the random effects. Results are compared across both frameworks; convergence strengthens confidence in the findings.

## 3.7 Moderation and Mediation Tests

### 3.7.1 Moderation Analysis

The moderation hypotheses test whether the relationship between source selection method and procurement outcomes varies by procurement complexity. The theoretical expectation, developed in Chapter 2, is that the advantage of tradeoff over LPTA (if any) is larger for more complex procurements, where quality differentiation among offerors is greater and the consequences of selecting a low-quality provider are more severe.

Moderation is tested by including interaction terms between the tradeoff indicator and the complexity proxies:

**IT Services Interaction:**
$$\text{Cost Growth}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Tradeoff}_i + \beta_2 \text{IT}_i + \beta_3 (\text{Tradeoff}_i \times \text{IT}_i) + \gamma \mathbf{X}_i + \alpha_{\text{agency}} + \delta_{\text{year}} + \varepsilon_i$$

In this specification, $\beta_1$ captures the average tradeoff effect for non-IT contracts (the reference category), $\beta_2$ captures the baseline difference between IT and non-IT contracts among LPTA awards, and $\beta_3$ captures the *differential* tradeoff effect for IT contracts. A significant negative $\beta_3$ would indicate that the tradeoff advantage in reducing cost growth is *larger* for IT contracts, consistent with the moderation hypothesis.

Analogous interaction models are estimated for each dependent variable and each complexity proxy:

- Tradeoff $\times$ NAICS Sector (four categories)
- Tradeoff $\times$ Contract Type (FFP vs. cost-type vs. T&M)
- Tradeoff $\times$ IDV indicator

For categorical moderators with more than two levels, the interaction generates multiple coefficients. Joint significance is assessed using the Wald test of the null hypothesis that all interaction terms are simultaneously zero. Marginal effects plots are constructed to visualize the estimated treatment effect at each level of the moderator, with 95% confidence intervals. These plots facilitate interpretation by showing how the predicted difference between tradeoff and LPTA varies across the complexity spectrum, holding other variables at their means.

To guard against multiple testing concerns, the study reports both unadjusted $p$-values and $p$-values adjusted for the family-wise error rate using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). This procedure controls the false discovery rate (FDR) at 5%, balancing the risk of Type I errors against the loss of power associated with more conservative corrections (e.g., Bonferroni).

### 3.7.2 Mediation Analysis (Exploratory)

The study includes an exploratory mediation analysis examining whether competition intensity (number of offers received) mediates the relationship between source selection method and cost outcomes. The theoretical logic is that tradeoff source selection may attract a different competitive field (in terms of number or quality of offerors), and the characteristics of this competitive field may in turn influence cost growth and modification patterns.

The mediation framework follows the four-step approach of Baron and Kenny (1986), supplemented by modern bootstrap-based inference:

**Step 1:** Establish the total effect of tradeoff on cost growth (the "c path"):
$$\text{Cost Growth}_i = c_0 + c_1 \text{Tradeoff}_i + \gamma_1 \mathbf{X}_i + \varepsilon_{1i}$$

**Step 2:** Establish the effect of tradeoff on the mediator (the "a path"):
$$\text{Offers Received}_i = a_0 + a_1 \text{Tradeoff}_i + \gamma_2 \mathbf{X}_i + \varepsilon_{2i}$$

**Step 3:** Establish the effect of the mediator on cost growth, controlling for tradeoff (the "b path"):
$$\text{Cost Growth}_i = c_0' + c_1' \text{Tradeoff}_i + b_1 \text{Offers Received}_i + \gamma_3 \mathbf{X}_i + \varepsilon_{3i}$$

**Step 4:** Assess whether the indirect effect ($a_1 \times b_1$) is statistically significant using nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals (Preacher & Hayes, 2008). If the 95% bootstrap confidence interval for the indirect effect excludes zero, mediation is supported.

An important caveat applies to this analysis. In observational data, mediation analysis requires strong assumptions: the mediator must not be confounded with the outcome conditional on the treatment and covariates (the "sequential ignorability" assumption; Imai et al., 2010). Because both competition intensity and cost growth are endogenous to the procurement process, this assumption is difficult to defend. The mediation analysis is therefore designated as **exploratory** and is interpreted with caution. Significant indirect effects are discussed as suggestive of potential mechanisms rather than definitive causal pathways. Sensitivity analysis for unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding is conducted following the methods of Imai et al. (2010), which quantify the degree of confounding that would be needed to explain away the indirect effect.

## 3.8 Robustness and Sensitivity Analyses

The credibility of empirical findings in observational research depends on their robustness to alternative methodological choices. This section describes a comprehensive battery of robustness checks designed to assess the sensitivity of the primary results to key analytic decisions.

### 3.8.1 Alternative Variable Operationalizations

**Cost growth operationalization.** The primary measure uses `base_and_all_options_value` as the numerator and `base_exercised_options_value` as the denominator. Alternative specifications include: (a) using `total_dollars_obligated` instead of `base_and_all_options_value` as the numerator (reflecting actual disbursements rather than the total contract ceiling); (b) computing cost growth relative to the `base_and_all_options_value` at the time of initial award (reflecting the total estimated contract value including unexercised options); and (c) a binary operationalization (1 if cost growth exceeds a threshold, such as 10% or 20%, and 0 otherwise), estimated by logistic regression.

**Competition intensity operationalization.** The primary measure uses the raw count of offers received. Alternative specifications include: (a) a log-transformed count, $\ln(\text{Offers} + 1)$, estimated by OLS; (b) a categorical variable (1 offer, 2--3 offers, 4--5 offers, 6+ offers) estimated by ordered logit; and (c) a binary "robust competition" indicator (1 if three or more offers received, 0 otherwise).

### 3.8.2 Outlier Treatment

**Winsorization thresholds.** The primary analysis winsorizes cost growth at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Sensitivity analyses examine winsorization at the 5th/95th percentile (more aggressive) and the 0.5th/99.5th percentile (less aggressive). Additionally, the analysis is re-estimated after **trimming** --- dropping rather than capping --- observations in the extreme tails. Comparison of results across winsorization and trimming thresholds assesses whether findings are driven by extreme values.

**High-leverage observations.** Cook's distance and DFBETAS diagnostics identify observations with disproportionate influence on the estimated coefficients. The primary models are re-estimated after excluding observations with Cook's distance exceeding the conventional threshold of $4/n$. If the results are qualitatively unchanged, this provides evidence that the findings are not driven by a small number of atypical contracts.

### 3.8.3 Placebo Tests

Placebo tests assess the internal validity of the identification strategy by applying the same models to settings where no treatment effect is expected. Two placebo tests are designed:

1. **Commodity supply contracts.** For standardized commodity procurements (e.g., NAICS codes in manufacturing sectors), the theoretical argument for tradeoff superiority is weak: products are homogeneous, and LPTA is normatively appropriate. If the primary models are estimated on commodity contracts and yield a significant "tradeoff effect," this would suggest that the main results may reflect confounding rather than a genuine treatment effect.

2. **Pre-treatment period (DiD specification).** In the DiD framework, applying the treatment indicator to a period before the policy shock should produce a null effect. A significant pre-treatment "effect" would indicate a violation of the parallel trends assumption.

### 3.8.4 Rosenbaum Bounds for Unobserved Confounding

As described in Section 3.5.3, Rosenbaum bounds assess the sensitivity of PSM results to potential hidden bias from unmeasured confounders. For each outcome variable, the analysis reports the critical $\Gamma$ value at which the treatment effect first becomes insignificant at the 5% level. The critical $\Gamma$ is interpreted in context: a $\Gamma$ of 2.0, for example, means that an unobserved confounder would need to double the odds of tradeoff assignment (relative to a comparable LPTA contract) to explain away the observed treatment effect.

### 3.8.5 Subsample Replication

The primary results are replicated on theoretically motivated subsamples to assess generalizability and stability:

- **DoD vs. civilian agencies:** The Department of Defense accounts for the largest share of federal procurement spending and has its own acquisition regulations (the Defense FAR Supplement). Separate estimation for DoD and civilian agencies tests whether the source selection effect differs by procurement regime.
- **IT services vs. consulting services:** The two largest NAICS sectors in the sample may exhibit different competitive dynamics and quality differentiation. Separate estimation tests whether the results are driven by a single sector.
- **Large vs. small contracts:** Contracts above and below the median award amount may operate in different market environments. Separate estimation tests stability across contract size.
- **Early vs. late fiscal years:** Reporting quality for the `source_selection_process` field has likely improved over time as agencies gained experience with the new data element. Separate estimation for FY2020--FY2022 and FY2023--FY2025 tests whether results are sensitive to data quality improvements.

### 3.8.6 Comparison of Estimation Strategies

The study explicitly compares results across the three identification strategies: (a) full-sample regression with fixed effects, (b) PSM-matched sample, and (c) DiD estimation. Convergence of estimates across strategies --- in terms of sign, magnitude, and statistical significance --- provides the strongest evidence for the robustness of the findings. Divergence motivates investigation into the sources of difference (e.g., whether DiD estimates differ because the DiD sample is restricted to specific agencies or categories) and tempers the strength of causal claims.

## 3.9 Ethics, Data Security, and Feasibility

### 3.9.1 Ethical Considerations and IRB Status

All data used in Phase 1 of this study are publicly available government records. USAspending.gov data are published under the transparency mandates of FFATA and the DATA Act and are accessible to any member of the public without restriction. GAO bid protest reports are published documents available on GAO's public website. No data in the Phase 1 analysis are derived from human subjects, and no interactions with or observations of individuals are involved. The unit of analysis is the contract award, not the contracting officer, contractor employee, or any other individual.

Accordingly, Phase 1 does not require Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval under the Common Rule (45 CFR 46), which defines human subjects research as research involving living individuals about whom an investigator obtains (a) data through intervention or interaction with the individual, or (b) identifiable private information. Federal procurement data do not contain individually identifiable private information; contractor names and award details are matters of public record.

Phase 2 of the broader research agenda --- the practitioner survey and semi-structured interviews --- will involve human subjects (federal contracting officers and acquisition leaders) and will require a separate IRB application to the University of Denver's IRB. Phase 2 is beyond the scope of this dissertation and will be pursued as future work upon IRB approval.

### 3.9.2 Data Security

Although the data are publicly available, responsible data management practices are followed. Downloaded datasets are stored on an encrypted local drive (FileVault-encrypted macOS volume) in the researcher's secure workspace. No personally identifiable information (PII) is included in the analysis dataset; while contractor names are technically available in USAspending data, they are used only for deduplication purposes during data cleaning and are not included in regression models or reported in results. Data processing and analysis scripts are maintained in a version-controlled repository to ensure reproducibility.

Intermediate and final datasets, analysis scripts, and output files are organized in a structured directory:

```
phd-research/
  data/
    usaspending/        # Raw and processed USAspending extracts
    gao/                # GAO protest report data
  analysis/
    notebooks/          # Jupyter analysis notebooks
    scripts/            # Python analysis scripts
    output/             # Regression results, tables, figures
  dissertation/
    chapters/           # Dissertation chapter drafts
```

This structure facilitates reproducibility and ensures that the analytical pipeline from raw data to published results is transparent and auditable.

### 3.9.3 Feasibility

The feasibility of the proposed research design is supported by several factors. First, the data infrastructure has been established and tested. The researcher has successfully accessed USAspending.gov bulk download files and the API, confirming that the required data elements are available and that the download process is reliable. A Python-based download script (`data/usaspending/download_awards.py`) automates the extraction of contract data by fiscal year, agency, and NAICS sector. A separate script (`data/usaspending/download_gao_protests.py`) collects GAO protest data.

Second, the expected sample size is large enough to support the proposed statistical models with adequate power (see Section 3.6.4) but not so large as to create computational challenges beyond the capabilities of standard statistical software. The analysis is conducted in Python using the `statsmodels`, `linearmodels`, and `scikit-learn` libraries for regression and matching, and `pandas` and `numpy` for data manipulation. Jupyter notebooks provide the interactive analysis environment.

Third, the temporal window (FY2020--FY2025) is now sufficiently mature that the data include multiple years of stabilized reporting, reducing concerns about data quality in the earliest years of the `source_selection_process` field.

Fourth, the researcher's professional background in federal procurement provides domain expertise that informs the interpretation of data patterns, the selection of appropriate control variables, and the assessment of data quality issues. This expertise is complemented by the methodological training provided by the Daniels College of Business Executive PhD program.

## 3.10 Limitations of the Archival Approach

Every research design involves trade-offs, and the archival quantitative approach adopted in this study is no exception. This section catalogs the principal limitations and discusses how each is addressed or acknowledged.

### 3.10.1 Observational Data and Causal Inference

The most fundamental limitation is that the data are observational rather than experimental. The assignment of source selection method to contracts is endogenous: agencies choose LPTA or tradeoff based on factors that may independently influence outcomes. While the identification strategies described in Section 3.5 --- within-category comparisons, propensity score matching, and difference-in-differences --- mitigate this concern, they cannot fully eliminate it. PSM addresses selection on observables but not selection on unobservables. DiD controls for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity but requires the parallel trends assumption. Within-category comparisons reduce confounding but cannot rule out within-category selection on contract-specific factors.

The study therefore characterizes its findings as **conditional associations** or **quasi-causal estimates** rather than definitive causal effects. The convergence (or divergence) of results across multiple identification strategies provides a basis for assessing the plausibility of causal interpretations, but the reader should interpret the estimates with an awareness of the residual confounding risk. This limitation motivates the planned Phase 2 qualitative work, which will provide practitioner perspectives on the causal mechanisms that the quantitative analysis can only suggest.

### 3.10.2 Temporal Window

The `source_selection_process` field was added to FPDS in 2020, limiting the available data to approximately five to six fiscal years at the time of analysis. This window is sufficient for cross-sectional estimation and for basic panel models but constrains the power of DiD designs (which require adequate pre-treatment data) and limits the ability to detect long-run trends. The temporal window also coincides with the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted federal procurement in ways that may confound the analysis (e.g., emergency procurements, workforce disruptions, accelerated contracting timelines). Fiscal year fixed effects absorb common pandemic effects, but heterogeneous agency- or sector-level pandemic impacts may not be fully controlled.

As the data mature and additional fiscal years become available, future research can extend the analysis to a longer panel, improving the precision of DiD estimates and enabling the detection of long-run effects that may not manifest in the initial years of a contract.

### 3.10.3 Missing Outcome Variables

The most consequential measurement limitation is the absence of direct performance quality data. CPARS ratings --- the government's primary assessment of contractor quality, schedule compliance, cost control, and management capability --- are not publicly available. The study's proxy outcomes (cost growth and modification intensity) capture important but incomplete dimensions of performance. Cost growth may reflect legitimate scope additions rather than contractor failings. Modification intensity may result from evolving government requirements rather than poor contractor performance. Neither proxy directly measures service quality, user satisfaction, or mission impact.

Schedule compliance data are also unavailable in the public FPDS records. While FPDS includes period of performance dates, it does not include planned versus actual delivery milestones at the task or deliverable level. Contract extensions (observable as modifications to the period of performance end date) provide a crude proxy for schedule performance but do not capture day-to-day timeliness.

These gaps mean that the study can speak to the cost and administrative dimensions of the LPTA-versus-tradeoff question but cannot directly address the quality dimension that is arguably the most important from a public-value perspective. This limitation is a primary motivation for future research that secures access to CPARS data or develops alternative measures of quality (e.g., through text analysis of contract descriptions or through the practitioner surveys planned in Phase 2).

### 3.10.4 Source Selection Coding Quality

The reliability of the `source_selection_process` field depends on accurate reporting by contracting officers. As a relatively new data element (introduced in 2020), the field may be subject to inconsistent interpretation and data entry errors, particularly in the early fiscal years. Some agencies may apply the codes differently: for example, a procurement that uses a two-phase evaluation with price-related factors might be coded as LPTA by one agency and tradeoff by another. The "Other" category may serve as a catch-all for procurements where the contracting officer is uncertain about the correct code.

To assess the severity of this concern, the analysis examines the distribution of source selection codes by agency and fiscal year, looking for anomalous patterns (e.g., agencies that report 100% LPTA or 100% tradeoff, which may indicate systematic miscoding). Sensitivity analyses that include or exclude specific agencies with questionable reporting patterns test whether the results are robust to coding quality concerns.

### 3.10.5 Aggregation from Transaction to Award Level

The aggregation of transaction-level data to the award level introduces measurement choices that affect the results. The rules described in Section 3.3.4 --- using the initial transaction for source selection and competition variables, using the most recent transaction for financial totals --- are standard but not the only possibility. For example, some contracts undergo "novation" (transfer to a different contractor) or "re-procurement" (cancellation and re-award) that may make the concept of a single continuous "award" ambiguous. The study identifies and flags such cases during data cleaning and examines sensitivity to their inclusion or exclusion.

Additionally, the aggregation from NAICS 6-digit codes to the sector level (3-digit) for the complexity proxy represents a loss of granularity. Two contracts in the same 3-digit NAICS sector may differ substantially in complexity at the 6-digit level. Robustness checks using 4-digit NAICS codes as fixed effects (rather than 3-digit sector categories) provide a more granular control for within-sector heterogeneity.

### 3.10.6 Generalizability

The analytic sample is restricted to federal contracts in four service and IT NAICS sectors above the simplified acquisition threshold. The findings may not generalize to: (a) commodity supply procurements, where product homogeneity diminishes the quality-differentiation rationale for tradeoff; (b) below-SAT procurements, where simplified procedures apply; (c) construction contracts, which follow distinct FAR provisions; (d) state and local government procurement, which operates under different statutory frameworks; or (e) international government procurement, which involves different regulatory regimes.

The restriction to specific NAICS sectors is a deliberate design choice that prioritizes internal validity (meaningful comparison within a defined domain) over external validity (broad generalizability). The trade-off is acknowledged, and the findings are interpreted as applicable to the specific procurement domain studied, with extensions to other domains left as directions for future research.

### 3.10.7 Absence of Phase 2 Qualitative Validation

The quantitative analysis can identify statistical associations between source selection method and procurement outcomes but cannot explain the mechanisms through which these associations arise. Why do tradeoff awards exhibit different cost growth patterns? Is it because agencies that choose tradeoff are more careful evaluators in general? Because the tradeoff process itself produces better contractor matches? Because offerors respond strategically to the evaluation criteria? Because tradeoff awards tend to have more detailed statements of work that reduce post-award ambiguity? The archival data alone cannot distinguish among these explanations.

The planned Phase 2 qualitative research --- practitioner surveys and semi-structured interviews --- is designed to address this interpretive gap. Contracting officers can provide firsthand accounts of how they choose between LPTA and tradeoff, what factors they weigh, how the choice affects the proposals they receive, and how the evaluation process influences post-award management. Until Phase 2 is completed, the quantitative findings stand as empirical patterns in need of mechanistic explanation. This limitation is inherent in any purely quantitative study of a complex institutional phenomenon and is a well-recognized motivation for mixed-methods research designs (Creswell & Creswell, 2018).

## 3.11 Chapter Summary

This chapter has presented the methodology for an empirical investigation of whether best-value tradeoff source selection produces different public-value outcomes than LPTA in U.S. federal procurement. The study employs a quantitative archival design, analyzing contract-level data from USAspending.gov and FPDS for fiscal years 2020 through 2025. The analytic sample comprises competitive, above-SAT contract awards in professional service and IT NAICS sectors where the source selection process is coded as LPTA or tradeoff.

The identification strategy addresses the endogeneity of source selection method through three complementary approaches: within-category comparisons using agency, NAICS, and fiscal year fixed effects; propensity score matching with nearest-neighbor algorithms and Rosenbaum bounds sensitivity analysis; and difference-in-differences estimation exploiting policy shocks in source selection practice. Primary models include OLS for continuous outcomes, negative binomial regression for count outcomes, and logistic regression for binary outcomes, with multi-level model extensions that account for the hierarchical nesting of contracts within agencies and NAICS categories.

Moderation tests examine whether the source selection effect varies by procurement complexity, and an exploratory mediation analysis investigates whether competition intensity transmits the effect of source selection to cost outcomes. A comprehensive battery of robustness checks --- including alternative variable operationalizations, outlier treatments, placebo tests, subsample replications, and cross-strategy comparisons --- assesses the stability and credibility of the findings.

The methodology is designed to maximize the inferential value of publicly available administrative data while transparently acknowledging the limitations of observational research. The findings will provide the most comprehensive empirical evidence to date on the relationship between source selection method and procurement outcomes in U.S. federal government contracting, contributing to both academic scholarship and policy deliberation on acquisition reform.
