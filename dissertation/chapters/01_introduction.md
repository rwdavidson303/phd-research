# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Opening: The Scale and Stakes of Public Procurement

Every year, the United States federal government obligates more than $700 billion in contract spending, making it the single largest buyer of goods and services in the world (USAspending.gov, 2025). When state, local, and quasi-governmental expenditures are included, the annual public procurement figure exceeds $2 trillion domestically and approaches $13 trillion globally (OECD, 2019). These sums are not abstract line items in a ledger. They fund the information systems that process veterans' disability claims, the cybersecurity architectures that protect critical infrastructure, the cloud platforms that host census data, and the consulting engagements that advise agencies on organizational transformation. Public procurement, in short, is the mechanism through which democratic governments convert taxpayer revenue into public services, and the rules governing how contracts are awarded determine, in large part, whether those services are delivered effectively, efficiently, and equitably.

Despite the enormity of these expenditures, the academic literature on public procurement remains surprisingly fragmented. Economists have produced elegant models of auction design and contract incentives (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Laffont & Tirole, 1993), yet these models frequently abstract away the institutional complexity of real government solicitations. Public administration scholars have examined procurement reform through the lens of governance and accountability (Kelman, 2002; Thai, 2001), but their analyses often lack the large-sample empirical identification strategies that would permit causal inference. Management and strategy scholars have explored buyer-supplier relationships extensively in the private sector (Dyer & Singh, 1998; Williamson, 1985), yet they have given comparatively little attention to the distinctive constraints of public-sector contracting—competitive bidding mandates, transparency requirements, protest mechanisms, and political oversight—that fundamentally alter the dynamics of the exchange. The result is a body of knowledge that is theoretically rich in parts but empirically thin where it matters most: at the intersection of source selection method, contract performance, and public value.

This dissertation addresses that gap. It examines, with large-scale archival data from the Federal Procurement Data System (FPDS) and USAspending.gov, whether the method by which the government selects a contractor—specifically, whether it uses a lowest-price technically acceptable (LPTA) evaluation or a best-value tradeoff analysis—has a measurable effect on the outcomes that taxpayers care about. Those outcomes include not only the initial price paid but also the frequency and magnitude of post-award contract modifications, the timeliness of delivery, the incidence of bid protests, the degree of competitive participation, and the broader concept that Moore (1995) termed "public value": the net benefit that a government action creates for the citizenry it serves.

The question is urgent. Over the past two decades, congressional oversight bodies, inspectors general, and the Government Accountability Office (GAO) have repeatedly flagged concerns about the overuse of LPTA source selection in complex service and technology procurements (Congressional Research Service [CRS], 2024; GAO, 2014). Section 813 of the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) for Fiscal Year 2017 imposed statutory restrictions on the Department of Defense's use of LPTA for certain categories of acquisitions, reflecting a legislative judgment that the method was being applied in contexts for which it was ill-suited (Pub. L. 114-328, 2016). Practitioners on both the government and vendor sides of the procurement table have long observed that awarding a complex technology contract to the lowest bidder frequently produces a cascade of downstream costs—change orders, schedule delays, re-competitions, and diminished service quality—that more than offset the initial savings (Rendon & Snider, 2019). Yet rigorous empirical evidence on this question remains scarce, and the policy debate has proceeded largely on the basis of anecdote, case study, and professional intuition.

The international context reinforces the urgency. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has devoted increasing attention to public procurement as a strategic lever for achieving policy objectives that extend beyond cost minimization, including innovation, sustainability, and social equity (OECD, 2009, 2019). The European Union's procurement directives explicitly provide for "most economically advantageous tender" (MEAT) evaluation, which is functionally equivalent to best-value tradeoff, and several member states have moved to restrict lowest-price-only awards for complex services (Malacina et al., 2022). Yet even in jurisdictions that formally embrace value-based evaluation, the gravitational pull of price competition remains strong, and the empirical evidence on the relative performance of different evaluation methods is thin. The gap between procurement policy aspirations and empirical knowledge is a global phenomenon, and closing it requires the kind of rigorous, data-intensive research that this dissertation undertakes.

This study aims to replace intuition with evidence. Drawing on transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985, 1996), public value theory (Moore, 1995; Benington & Moore, 2011), and the economics of procurement contracts (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Bajari, McMillan, & Tadelis, 2009), it constructs a theoretical framework that predicts when and why best-value tradeoff source selection should outperform LPTA—and when it should not. It then tests those predictions using a quasi-experimental research design applied to a comprehensive dataset of federal service and technology contracts awarded between fiscal years 2020 and 2025. The goal is not to declare one source selection method universally superior, but to identify the institutional, market, and contractual conditions under which each method maximizes public value, and to provide actionable guidance to policymakers and acquisition professionals.

The stakes of getting this question right are considerable. If best-value tradeoff demonstrably produces superior outcomes for complex acquisitions, then the institutional pressures that push agencies toward LPTA are not merely inconvenient—they are costly, and the cost is borne by the public. Conversely, if the advantages of tradeoff evaluation are modest or contingent on conditions that are difficult to satisfy in practice, then the additional administrative burden it imposes may not be justified, and efforts to restrict LPTA may be misguided. Either way, the policy debate should be informed by evidence rather than assumption, and producing that evidence is the fundamental purpose of this work.


## 1.2 Problem Statement

The Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), which governs civilian agency procurement, and its Defense supplement (DFARS) provide contracting officers with two principal methods for evaluating competitive proposals. The first, lowest price technically acceptable (LPTA), establishes a set of minimum technical requirements and awards the contract to the offeror that meets all requirements at the lowest evaluated price. The second, best-value tradeoff, allows the government to conduct a comparative assessment of proposals across multiple evaluation factors—technical approach, management capability, past performance, staffing qualifications, and price—and to award the contract to the offeror whose proposal represents the best overall value, even if that offeror did not submit the lowest price (FAR 15.101-1; FAR 15.101-2).

In principle, the choice between these two methods should be governed by the nature of the requirement. FAR Part 15 instructs contracting officers to use LPTA when the requirement is well-defined, the risk of unsuccessful performance is minimal, and price is the dominant consideration. Best-value tradeoff, by contrast, is appropriate when the requirement is complex, the government values technical or managerial differentiation among offerors, and the consequences of poor performance are significant (FAR 15.101). In practice, however, a number of institutional pressures push agencies toward LPTA even in contexts where the regulation's own logic would counsel a tradeoff approach. These pressures include the desire to minimize protest risk (since LPTA awards are perceived as more defensible), the administrative burden of conducting detailed tradeoff analyses, workforce shortages in the acquisition corps, and political pressure to demonstrate cost discipline (CRS, 2024; Rendon & Snider, 2019).

The consequences of this misalignment between method and context are potentially severe. When a complex, technology-intensive requirement is awarded on the basis of lowest price alone, the winning contractor may have achieved that price by underestimating the scope of work, understaffing the effort, relying on less experienced personnel, or deferring difficult technical challenges to the post-award period. The result is a contract that appears inexpensive at the point of award but accumulates costs over its performance period through modifications, claims, disputes, and—in the worst cases—termination for default and re-procurement. The phenomenon is well documented in the defense acquisition literature (Arena et al., 2006; RAND Corporation, 2013) and has been identified as a significant contributor to the federal government's persistent cost growth problem (GAO, 2020).

The problem, stated formally, is as follows: **Lowest-price technically acceptable source selection is the default or strongly favored method in a substantial share of federal service and technology procurements, yet there are strong theoretical reasons and considerable practitioner testimony suggesting that, for complex acquisitions, this method does not maximize public value. The empirical evidence base is insufficient to resolve the question, to quantify the magnitude of the effect, or to identify the boundary conditions under which the relationship holds.**

Several specific dimensions of this problem merit elaboration.

First, the relationship between source selection method and post-award contract performance has not been rigorously established with large-sample federal data. Existing studies tend to rely on case analyses, surveys of contracting professionals, or small samples drawn from individual agencies (e.g., Dunn, 2009; Rendon, 2015). While these studies are valuable, they do not permit the kind of statistical identification—controlling for selection effects, contract characteristics, and agency-level heterogeneity—that is necessary to support causal claims.

Second, the role of the bid protest system in shaping procurement outcomes is poorly understood from an empirical standpoint. The GAO sustains approximately 13-15% of the protests it adjudicates on the merits each year (GAO, 2025), but the broader effects of protest activity—on procurement timelines, vendor participation rates, agency risk aversion, and source selection method choice—have not been comprehensively modeled. Calvo et al. (2019) demonstrated that oversight mechanisms can have paradoxical effects on efficiency, and the protest system may represent a case in point.

Third, the quality of the solicitation itself—the clarity of requirements, the coherence of evaluation criteria, the alignment between stated needs and contractual structure—is widely regarded by practitioners as a critical determinant of procurement success, yet it is rarely measured or included in quantitative analyses of contract outcomes (Hawkins et al., 2011; Tkachenko et al., 2018). If solicitation quality is correlated with source selection method (as seems plausible, given that tradeoff evaluations require more detailed solicitation development), then the omission of this variable from prior analyses may have biased estimates of the method's effect.

Fourth, the post-award oversight environment—including contract administration, compliance monitoring, and the layered review processes that characterize federal acquisitions—may interact with source selection method in ways that affect outcomes. A contract awarded through best-value tradeoff may perform better not only because of the selection method itself but also because the more detailed pre-award evaluation generates better information for post-award management. Conversely, the additional oversight layers may impose transaction costs that offset any performance gains (Calvo et al., 2019; Girth, 2014).

Fifth, the incumbency dynamics embedded in many federal procurements create a structural challenge that interacts with source selection method. When solicitation requirements are written in ways that closely mirror an incumbent contractor's existing approach, new entrants face a disadvantage that may reduce competition and innovation regardless of the evaluation method used (Yukins, 2008). Understanding how RFP design features that advantage incumbents interact with source selection method is essential for a complete picture of the procurement value equation.

Sixth, and most broadly, the concept of "public value" itself remains underoperationalized in the procurement context. Moore's (1995) original formulation of public value was developed in the context of strategic management in government and was not intended as an empirical measurement framework for contract outcomes. Subsequent scholars have elaborated the concept in various directions—emphasizing democratic legitimacy (Bozeman, 2007), co-production (Alford, 2009), and network governance (Bryson et al., 2014)—but the operationalization of public value as a set of measurable procurement outcome indicators remains an open challenge. Without a credible operationalization, claims about which source selection method better serves the public interest remain unanchored. This study addresses the operationalization challenge by constructing a multi-dimensional outcome framework grounded in both theoretical considerations and the practical realities of available data.

These six dimensions of the problem—source selection method effects, protest system impacts, solicitation quality, oversight costs, incumbency dynamics, and public value operationalization—are interconnected. They constitute a system of institutional and market factors that collectively determine whether a given procurement achieves its potential for creating public value. This dissertation examines that system empirically, with the aim of producing evidence that is both academically rigorous and practically useful.


## 1.3 Purpose of the Study

The purpose of this study is to empirically test whether best-value tradeoff source selection produces superior public-value outcomes compared to lowest-price technically acceptable (LPTA) evaluation in federal service and technology procurements, and to identify the institutional and market conditions under which this advantage holds. The study advances this purpose through three interrelated objectives.

The first objective is to measure the effect of source selection method on a composite set of public-value outcome variables. Rather than treating contract performance as a single-dimensional construct (e.g., cost growth alone), this study constructs a multi-dimensional outcome framework that includes cost growth (the ratio of final obligated amount to initial award value), modification intensity (the number and cumulative dollar value of post-award modifications), procurement administrative lead time (PALT), competitive participation (the number of offers received), protest incidence and outcomes, and—where data permit—performance ratings from the Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS). This multi-dimensional approach reflects the insight from public value theory that the worth of a government action cannot be reduced to a single metric but must be assessed across the range of outcomes that matter to the public (Moore, 1995; Benington & Moore, 2011).

The second objective is to move beyond simple comparison of means toward causal identification. The central empirical challenge in studying source selection method effects is that the method is not randomly assigned. Contracting officers choose LPTA or tradeoff based on the characteristics of the requirement, the competitive landscape, and agency preferences—factors that are themselves correlated with outcomes. A naive comparison of average outcomes across the two methods would therefore be confounded by selection bias. This study addresses the identification challenge through multiple quasi-experimental strategies, including propensity score matching (PSM) to construct comparable treatment and control groups, difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis exploiting the NDAA Section 813 policy change as a natural experiment, and instrumental variable approaches where suitable instruments can be identified. The use of multiple identification strategies provides a check on the robustness of findings and strengthens the basis for causal inference (Angrist & Pischke, 2009).

The third objective is to examine the moderating and mediating mechanisms through which source selection method affects outcomes. The theoretical framework developed in Chapter 2 predicts that the advantage of best-value tradeoff over LPTA should be greatest when transaction complexity is high, when the requirement involves significant uncertainty or customization, and when the market is characterized by heterogeneous supplier capabilities. Conversely, the advantage should diminish—and LPTA may be preferred—when the requirement is standardized, the market is competitive with homogeneous suppliers, and the government's primary concern is minimizing acquisition cost. By testing these moderating effects, the study moves beyond a binary "which method is better" question to a more nuanced understanding of method-context fit.

The study draws its data from the Federal Procurement Data System-Next Generation (FPDS-NG) and USAspending.gov, which together provide detailed records of every federal contract action above the micro-purchase threshold. The analytic sample is restricted to competitive service and technology contracts above the simplified acquisition threshold ($250,000) awarded in fiscal years 2020 through 2025, with North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes in the 541xxx (Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services), 518xxx (Computing Infrastructure Providers, Data Processing, Web Hosting, and Related Services), 511xxx (Publishing Industries, including software), and 561xxx (Administrative and Support Services) categories. These categories were selected because they represent the domains in which the LPTA-versus-tradeoff debate is most consequential—complex, knowledge-intensive services where contractor quality and approach can vary substantially and where the consequences of poor performance extend well beyond the immediate contract.


## 1.4 Research Questions

This dissertation is organized around seven research questions. These questions are sequenced to build cumulatively: the first establishes the primary relationship of interest, the second through sixth explore the mechanisms and boundary conditions, and the seventh synthesizes the findings into policy-relevant guidance.

**RQ1: Do best-value tradeoff source selection methods produce higher public-value outcomes than LPTA?**

This is the central empirical question. "Public-value outcomes" are operationalized as a composite of cost growth, modification intensity, procurement administrative lead time, competitive participation, protest incidence, and available performance ratings. The hypothesis, derived from transaction cost economics and public value theory, is that best-value tradeoff will be associated with superior outcomes on most dimensions for complex service and technology contracts, while the methods will converge in performance for more standardized requirements. This question requires careful attention to the endogeneity of method choice: because contracting officers select the evaluation method based on characteristics of the requirement, naive comparisons may confound method effects with requirement effects. The quasi-experimental strategies described in Chapter 3 are designed to address this identification challenge.

**RQ2: How do bid-protest and appeal systems affect procurement lead time, vendor participation, and public-value outcomes?**

The federal bid protest system, administered primarily by the GAO and the Court of Federal Claims, provides a mechanism for disappointed offerors to challenge award decisions. While the system serves important due-process and accountability functions, it also imposes costs in the form of procurement delays, stay-of-performance orders, and agency risk aversion. This question examines the empirical relationship between protest activity and procurement outcomes, testing whether protest exposure varies by source selection method and whether protest-prone procurements exhibit different outcome profiles.

**RQ3: How does solicitation quality—specifically, requirements clarity and evaluation criteria alignment—affect bidding behavior, protest incidence, and contract performance?**

Solicitation quality is a latent construct that this study approximates through observable indicators including amendment frequency, questions-and-answers volume (where available), evaluation factor specificity, and the degree of alignment between stated requirements and the eventual contract structure. The question examines whether solicitation quality mediates the relationship between source selection method and outcomes, and whether it independently predicts performance.

**RQ4: What is the net effect of post-award oversight layers on delays, cost growth, and contract performance?**

Federal contracts are subject to multiple layers of post-award oversight, including contracting officer technical representative (COTR) monitoring, Defense Contract Audit Agency (DCAA) reviews (for cost-type contracts), inspector general investigations, and congressional reporting requirements. This question asks whether these oversight mechanisms, in the aggregate, improve or impair contract outcomes, and whether their effect differs by source selection method.

**RQ5: To what extent does reliance on lowest initial price increase the frequency and magnitude of post-award renegotiation and change orders?**

A core prediction of transaction cost economics is that contracts awarded on the basis of lowest price will be more susceptible to post-award opportunism, because the winning bidder has an incentive to bid aggressively and recover margin through change orders and contract modifications (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Williamson, 1985). This question tests that prediction directly, examining whether LPTA-awarded contracts exhibit higher modification rates and greater cost growth than tradeoff-awarded contracts, controlling for contract type, complexity, and other covariates.

**RQ6: How do RFP design features that advantage incumbent contractors influence competition, innovation, and public value?**

Many federal solicitations contain requirements—such as detailed knowledge of existing systems, transition-period constraints, or security clearance prerequisites—that effectively limit competition to the incumbent and a small number of established firms. This question examines whether such features are more prevalent in LPTA or tradeoff procurements, and whether they affect the competitive dynamics and ultimate outcomes of the procurement.

**RQ7: Which reforms to current source selection practices most plausibly increase taxpayer value while preserving transparency and due process?**

This is an integrative question that draws on the empirical findings from RQ1 through RQ6, combined with the theoretical framework and institutional analysis, to identify policy recommendations. While the primary purpose of the dissertation is empirical rather than prescriptive, the research questions are motivated by a practical concern with improving procurement outcomes, and the final question provides a vehicle for translating findings into actionable guidance. The question is deliberately phrased in terms of "plausibility" rather than "proof," reflecting the reality that policy recommendations must weigh considerations—political feasibility, implementation cost, workforce capacity—that extend beyond the scope of any single empirical study. Nevertheless, evidence-based guidance, even when qualified, is preferable to the current state of affairs, in which source selection policy is shaped primarily by anecdote, tradition, and risk aversion.

The seven research questions are designed to be mutually reinforcing. RQ1 establishes the baseline relationship; RQ2 through RQ6 probe the mechanisms, moderators, and boundary conditions; and RQ7 synthesizes the findings into actionable implications. Together, they represent a comprehensive examination of the source selection question that, to the author's knowledge, has not been attempted in the prior literature at this level of empirical rigor.


## 1.5 Significance of the Study

This study makes four principal contributions to the scholarly and practitioner communities.

### 1.5.1 Theoretical Integration

The first contribution is theoretical. The study integrates three bodies of theory that have developed largely in parallel: public value theory from public administration (Moore, 1995; Benington & Moore, 2011), transaction cost economics from organizational economics (Williamson, 1985, 1996), and the economics of procurement contracts (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Bajari et al., 2009; Laffont & Tirole, 1993). Each of these traditions offers important but incomplete insight into the source selection question. Public value theory provides the normative framework—the definition of what "good" procurement outcomes look like—but is largely silent on the microeconomic mechanisms that produce them. Transaction cost economics explains why contractual governance structures matter and predicts when market-based (price-driven) mechanisms will fail, but it does not engage deeply with the distinctive institutional features of public procurement. Procurement economics offers rigorous models of auction design and contract incentives, but these models often assume away the institutional complexity—transparency mandates, protest rights, political oversight—that characterizes real government acquisitions.

By bringing these three traditions into conversation, this dissertation develops a theoretical framework that is both normatively grounded and empirically tractable. The framework predicts not merely that best-value tradeoff "is better" than LPTA in some generic sense, but specifies the conditions—characterized by transaction complexity, asset specificity, uncertainty, and market structure—under which each method is more likely to maximize public value. This conditional prediction is the framework's central contribution, and it distinguishes this study from prior work that has tended to treat source selection method choice as a simple binary.

### 1.5.2 Large-Scale Empirical Evidence

The second contribution is empirical. To the author's knowledge, this is the first study to apply quasi-experimental identification strategies to a comprehensive dataset of federal service and technology contracts for the purpose of estimating the causal effect of source selection method on a multi-dimensional set of public-value outcomes. Prior quantitative studies of federal procurement have tended to focus on defense hardware acquisitions (e.g., Arena et al., 2006; RAND Corporation, 2013), to use simple regression without addressing selection bias (e.g., Dunn, 2009), or to examine single outcome dimensions such as cost growth without considering the full range of public-value indicators. This study's use of FPDS/USAspending data covering fiscal years 2020-2025, combined with propensity score matching, difference-in-differences analysis, and robustness checks, provides a level of empirical rigor that has been absent from the source selection debate.

The dataset itself represents a contribution. The study constructs a research-ready analytic file from raw FPDS and USAspending records, with variables coded for source selection method, contract type, NAICS code, agency, competition level, modification history, and outcome indicators. This dataset, appropriately documented and (where permissible) shared, can support future research on a range of procurement questions beyond those addressed here.

### 1.5.3 Quasi-Experimental Identification

The third contribution is methodological. The study demonstrates the feasibility and value of applying quasi-experimental methods—specifically, propensity score matching and difference-in-differences—to federal procurement data. These methods are well established in economics, education, and health policy research (Angrist & Pischke, 2009; Imbens & Rubin, 2015), but they have been used only sparingly in the procurement context. The NDAA Section 813 policy restriction on LPTA for certain defense acquisitions provides a natural experiment that is particularly well suited to difference-in-differences analysis, because it created an exogenous shift in the source selection method used for affected contract categories while leaving unaffected categories as a comparison group. By exploiting this policy variation, the study can estimate the causal effect of shifting from LPTA to tradeoff in a way that is not confounded by the contracting officer's endogenous method choice.

The methodological contribution extends to the operationalization of "public value" as an empirical construct. While public value theory has generated considerable conceptual discussion (Alford & O'Flynn, 2009; Bryson et al., 2014; Hartley et al., 2017), efforts to measure public value quantitatively have been limited. This study's multi-dimensional outcome framework—combining cost, schedule, competition, protest, and performance indicators—offers one approach to operationalization that other researchers can adapt and extend.

### 1.5.4 Triangulation of Quantitative and Field Evidence

The fourth contribution is practical. The study is designed as the first phase of a larger mixed-methods research program. While this dissertation reports the quantitative, archival analysis, the research questions have been formulated with an eye toward subsequent qualitative investigation. The findings from this phase will inform the design of a second phase involving interviews with contracting officers, program managers, and industry proposal professionals to probe the mechanisms underlying the quantitative patterns. This triangulation strategy—using large-scale data to establish whether an effect exists and field evidence to explain how and why it operates—reflects best practice in applied organizational research (Creswell & Creswell, 2018; Edmondson & McManus, 2007) and enhances the practical relevance of the findings.

For procurement policymakers, the study provides evidence-based guidance on when LPTA is appropriate and when tradeoff evaluation is worth the additional administrative investment. In a political environment in which procurement reform proposals are frequently debated but rarely grounded in rigorous empirical analysis, this evidence has the potential to shift the conversation from ideological preference to data-informed decision-making. For acquisition professionals—contracting officers, program managers, and industry capture and proposal teams—the study offers empirical benchmarks for the downstream costs associated with different source selection approaches, enabling more informed decisions at the critical moment when the acquisition strategy is being developed. For the academic community, it opens a research agenda at the intersection of public value theory, institutional economics, and procurement management that has been underexplored relative to its practical importance.

The significance of this study extends beyond any single finding. Even if the empirical results are nuanced or conditional—as most honest social science is—the act of applying rigorous methods to a question that has been debated for decades on the basis of anecdote and professional judgment represents an advance. The procurement community needs not certainty but calibrated evidence: estimates of effect sizes, identification of boundary conditions, and transparent reporting of the assumptions and limitations that attend any empirical analysis. This dissertation is designed to provide exactly that.


## 1.6 Researcher Positionality and Practical Motivation

Transparency about the researcher's background, assumptions, and motivations is a hallmark of responsible scholarship, particularly when the research addresses questions that intersect with the investigator's professional experience (Creswell & Creswell, 2018). I have spent more than 25 years working in and around government procurement, occupying positions on both the public and vendor sides of the acquisition process. On the government side, I have served in roles that involved developing requirements, participating in source evaluation boards, and managing contracts. On the industry side, I have led proposal strategy and managed the response to more than 20 major federal technology RFP competitions, working with capture teams to interpret solicitations, develop technical approaches, price solutions, and navigate the evaluation process.

This experience gives me intimate familiarity with the phenomena under investigation. I have participated in procurements where a best-value tradeoff evaluation produced a thoughtful, well-reasoned award decision that selected a contractor whose technical approach was demonstrably superior to lower-priced alternatives. I have also participated in procurements where an LPTA evaluation reduced a complex technology requirement to a checklist exercise, eliminated meaningful technical differentiation, and awarded the contract to a bidder whose pricing rested on assumptions that proved untenable in execution. These experiences—and the frustration they engendered—are the practical motivation for this research.

It is important, however, to distinguish between motivation and evidence. My professional experience has shaped the research questions I ask and the theoretical frameworks I find plausible, but it is not offered as evidence for any empirical claim. The dissertation is designed to test propositions about source selection method effects using publicly available archival data and transparent analytic methods that any qualified researcher could replicate. Where my professional knowledge informs the interpretation of results—for example, in understanding why certain patterns appear in the data—I identify those interpretive contributions explicitly and distinguish them from the empirical findings themselves.

I also acknowledge several potential biases that my background may introduce. First, as someone who has spent much of my career on the industry side of procurement, I may be predisposed to view LPTA unfavorably, because LPTA evaluations limit the value that industry proposal teams can demonstrate. I guard against this bias by formulating testable hypotheses, using objective outcome measures derived from government administrative data, and subjecting my analyses to robustness checks designed to detect specification-dependent results. Second, my experience is concentrated in technology and professional services procurements, which may lead me to overgeneralize findings from these categories to procurement as a whole. I address this concern by delimiting the study's scope explicitly and by testing for heterogeneity across contract categories and complexity levels. Third, as a practitioner-scholar, I am motivated by a desire to produce research that is "useful"—that changes practice—which could, if unchecked, lead me to overstate the policy implications of findings that are statistically significant but substantively modest. I commit to reporting effect sizes alongside significance levels and to characterizing the practical significance of results honestly.

The DBA program at Indiana University's Kelley School of Business is designed precisely for individuals who bring deep professional experience to doctoral research. The program's pedagogy recognizes that practitioner knowledge, when disciplined by rigorous methods and theoretical framing, can generate research questions and interpretive insights that purely academic researchers might miss. My positionality is therefore not a limitation to be apologized for but a resource to be leveraged—carefully, transparently, and in the service of evidence that meets the highest scholarly standards.


## 1.7 Scope and Delimitations

Every empirical study involves choices about what to include and exclude, and these choices have consequences for the generalizability of findings. This section describes the study's scope and the rationale for its delimitations.

### 1.7.1 Federal Contracts Only

The study is limited to contracts awarded by United States federal executive-branch agencies. State, local, and tribal government procurements are excluded, as are procurements by legislative and judicial branch entities. This delimitation is driven by data availability: FPDS-NG provides comprehensive, standardized data on federal contract actions, whereas state and local procurement data are fragmented across thousands of jurisdictions with inconsistent data standards. While the theoretical framework developed in Chapter 2 is applicable in principle to any public procurement context, the empirical tests are specific to the federal system, and readers should exercise caution in generalizing findings to other governmental levels.

### 1.7.2 Fiscal Years 2020-2025

The study examines contracts awarded between October 1, 2019 (the start of federal fiscal year 2020) and September 30, 2025 (the end of fiscal year 2025). This six-year window was selected for several reasons. First, it provides a sufficiently large sample to support the statistical methods employed, including subgroup analyses and interaction effects. Second, it encompasses the implementation period for NDAA Section 813 restrictions on LPTA, enabling the difference-in-differences analysis described above. Third, it captures a period of significant disruption and adaptation in federal procurement practices due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which introduced both exogenous shocks to procurement timelines and policy innovations (e.g., expanded use of emergency acquisition flexibilities) that provide additional variation for analysis. Fourth, the data quality and completeness of FPDS records have improved over time, and the post-2019 data are generally more reliable than earlier years.

The choice of this window does, however, impose limitations. Contracts awarded before FY2020 are excluded, which means the study cannot examine long-term trends in source selection method usage or outcomes. Contracts whose performance periods extend beyond the data collection date may have incomplete modification and outcome data. The study addresses this limitation by conducting sensitivity analyses that restrict the sample to contracts with completed or substantially completed performance periods.

### 1.7.3 Service and Technology Categories

The study focuses on contracts in four NAICS code families: 541xxx (Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services), 518xxx (Computing Infrastructure Providers, Data Processing, Web Hosting, and Related Services), 511xxx (Publishing Industries, including software publishing), and 561xxx (Administrative and Support Services). These categories were selected because they represent the procurement domains in which the choice between LPTA and best-value tradeoff is most consequential and most contested. Technology and professional services contracts are characterized by high uncertainty, significant quality variation among contractors, substantial customization requirements, and considerable potential for post-award scope changes—precisely the conditions under which transaction cost economics predicts that price-based selection will be suboptimal (Williamson, 1985, 1996).

The exclusion of other procurement categories—including construction, manufactured goods, weapons systems, and commodities—is intentional. These categories have different competitive dynamics, different sources of cost growth, and different relationships between source selection method and outcomes. While a comprehensive study of source selection across all procurement categories would be valuable, it would require a substantially different research design and is beyond the scope of a single dissertation.

### 1.7.4 Competitive Procurements Above the Simplified Acquisition Threshold

The study includes only competitive procurements—those in which two or more offerors submitted proposals in response to a solicitation—with initial award values above the simplified acquisition threshold (SAT) of $250,000. Sole-source contracts are excluded because the source selection method question is not applicable when there is only one offeror. Contracts below the SAT are excluded because they are subject to simplified acquisition procedures that do not involve formal source selection (FAR Part 13) and because the data quality for these smaller actions is less reliable.

### 1.7.5 Archival Quantitative Analysis

This dissertation reports the results of an archival quantitative analysis using secondary data from FPDS-NG and USAspending.gov. It does not include primary data collection through surveys, interviews, or experiments. As noted above, the study is designed as the first phase of a planned mixed-methods research program; subsequent phases will incorporate qualitative field evidence. The limitation of the current phase to quantitative archival analysis means that some constructs of theoretical interest—such as contracting officer decision-making processes, agency organizational culture, and industry perceptions of solicitation fairness—cannot be directly measured and must be either proxied by observable variables or deferred to future research.

### 1.7.6 Generalizability Considerations

Readers should be attentive to several boundaries on the generalizability of this study's findings. The restriction to federal contracts means that results may not transfer directly to state and local procurement, which operates under different legal frameworks and institutional constraints. The focus on service and technology categories means that findings should not be assumed to hold for construction, manufactured goods, or weapons systems procurement, where cost structures, quality metrics, and competitive dynamics differ substantially. The fiscal year 2020-2025 window encompasses a period marked by the COVID-19 pandemic, supply chain disruptions, and significant workforce changes in the federal acquisition corps; the extent to which findings from this period generalize to more stable conditions is an empirical question that only future research can answer. Finally, while the quasi-experimental methods employed in this study are substantially more rigorous than the descriptive and correlational approaches used in most prior work, they rely on assumptions (e.g., conditional independence in PSM, parallel trends in DiD) that, if violated, could bias the estimates. The robustness checks described in Chapter 3 are designed to probe the sensitivity of results to these assumptions, but no observational study can achieve the certainty of a randomized experiment.


## 1.8 Definition of Key Terms

The following definitions are provided to ensure clarity and consistency in the use of specialized terminology throughout the dissertation.

**Best-Value Tradeoff.** A source selection method, authorized under FAR 15.101-1, in which the government evaluates proposals against multiple factors (e.g., technical approach, management capability, past performance, and price) and selects the offeror whose proposal offers the best overall value, considering the relative importance of the evaluation factors. Under this method, the government may award to an offeror other than the one that proposed the lowest price, if the technical or other advantages of the higher-priced proposal are judged to be worth the price premium.

**Cost Growth.** The percentage increase in a contract's total obligated amount from the initial award value to the final obligated amount at contract completion. Formally, cost growth is calculated as: (Final Obligated Amount - Initial Award Value) / Initial Award Value x 100. Cost growth may result from contract modifications, change orders, scope changes, claims, or other post-award actions. It is a standard outcome measure in procurement research (Arena et al., 2006; GAO, 2020).

**Difference-in-Differences (DiD).** A quasi-experimental statistical method that estimates the causal effect of a treatment or policy change by comparing the change in outcomes over time between a treatment group (affected by the change) and a control group (not affected). In this study, DiD is applied to the NDAA Section 813 policy restricting LPTA in certain defense procurements, comparing outcomes for affected contract categories before and after the policy change to outcomes in unaffected categories over the same period (Angrist & Pischke, 2009).

**Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR).** The principal body of regulations governing the acquisition process for executive-branch federal agencies. The FAR is codified in Title 48 of the Code of Federal Regulations and is issued jointly by the Department of Defense, the General Services Administration, and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration. Part 15 of the FAR addresses contracting by negotiation, including source selection procedures.

**Federal Procurement Data System-Next Generation (FPDS-NG).** The U.S. government's central repository for information on federal contract actions. FPDS-NG captures detailed data on each contract award and modification, including the contracting agency, vendor, contract type, competition level, NAICS code, dollar value, and (since 2004) the source selection method used. Data from FPDS-NG are made publicly available through USAspending.gov.

**Lowest Price Technically Acceptable (LPTA).** A source selection method, authorized under FAR 15.101-2, in which the government establishes minimum technical requirements (acceptability standards) and awards the contract to the offeror that meets all requirements at the lowest evaluated price. Under LPTA, technical proposals are evaluated on a pass/fail basis; there is no comparative assessment of technical merit among offerors that meet the minimum standards. The method prioritizes price competition over technical differentiation.

**Modification Intensity.** The frequency and cumulative dollar value of post-award contract modifications. Modification intensity serves as a proxy for post-award contractual disruption, including scope changes, renegotiations, and change orders. High modification intensity may indicate initial contract incompleteness, post-award opportunism, or unforeseen changes in requirements.

**Procurement Administrative Lead Time (PALT).** The elapsed time from the issuance of a solicitation to the award of a contract. PALT is a measure of the efficiency of the procurement process. While longer PALT is not inherently undesirable (a more thorough evaluation may take more time and produce better outcomes), excessive PALT imposes costs on both the government and industry, including delayed capability delivery, increased proposal costs, and workforce planning uncertainty. The Office of Federal Procurement Policy tracks PALT as a key procurement performance metric.

**Propensity Score Matching (PSM).** A statistical technique used to reduce selection bias in observational studies by matching treated and untreated observations on their estimated probability (propensity score) of receiving the treatment, conditional on observed covariates. In this study, PSM is used to match LPTA-awarded contracts with best-value tradeoff-awarded contracts that have similar observable characteristics (agency, NAICS code, contract value, competition level, and year), so that differences in outcomes can be more credibly attributed to the source selection method rather than to confounding factors (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983).

**Public Value.** The net benefit that a government action, policy, or program creates for the citizenry. The concept, introduced by Moore (1995) and elaborated by Benington and Moore (2011), extends beyond economic efficiency to encompass fairness, accountability, trust, and the substantive quality of public services. In the procurement context, public value encompasses not only cost economy but also contract performance quality, competitive access, procedural fairness, and the alignment of procurement outcomes with broader public interests. This study operationalizes public value through a multi-dimensional outcome framework that captures these diverse elements.

**Source Selection.** The process by which the government evaluates competing proposals or bids and selects a contractor for award. Source selection is governed by FAR Part 15 for negotiated procurements and includes the appointment of a source selection authority (SSA), the establishment of evaluation criteria, the evaluation of proposals, and the award decision. The two principal source selection methods for negotiated procurements are best-value tradeoff and LPTA.

**USAspending.gov.** The official public database of federal spending information, mandated by the Federal Funding Accountability and Transparency Act of 2006 and the Digital Accountability and Transparency Act (DATA Act) of 2014. USAspending.gov integrates data from FPDS-NG with other federal financial data sources and provides a publicly accessible interface for searching and downloading federal contract, grant, loan, and other spending data.


## 1.9 Organization of the Dissertation

The remainder of this dissertation is organized into four chapters.

**Chapter 2: Literature Review and Theoretical Framework.** Chapter 2 reviews the scholarly literatures on public value theory, transaction cost economics, procurement contract design, and public procurement institutions, with particular attention to the empirical evidence on source selection method effects. The review is organized into four major streams: (a) the public value literature, tracing the concept from Moore's (1995) original formulation through subsequent elaborations and critiques; (b) transaction cost economics as applied to contractual governance, with emphasis on Williamson's (1985, 1996) framework and its predictions about the conditions under which market-based versus hierarchy-based governance mechanisms are efficient; (c) the economics of procurement contracts, including auction theory, contract design, and the empirical work of Bajari and Tadelis (2001) and Bajari et al. (2009) on the choice between competitive bidding and negotiation; and (d) the institutional and regulatory context of federal procurement, including the FAR framework, the bid protest system, and recent reform efforts. The chapter develops an integrated theoretical framework that combines insights from these traditions to generate testable predictions about when and why best-value tradeoff should outperform LPTA. It also identifies the gaps in the existing literature that the study's research questions are designed to address. The chapter concludes with the formal hypotheses that the empirical analysis will test.

**Chapter 3: Research Methodology.** Chapter 3 describes the research design, data sources, sample construction, variable operationalization, and analytic methods in detail. The chapter begins by situating the study within the post-positivist research paradigm and justifying the quantitative, archival approach. It then describes the FPDS-NG and USAspending.gov data sources, the procedures used to construct the analytic sample, and the operationalization of all independent, dependent, moderating, and control variables. The core of the chapter explains the rationale for the quasi-experimental approach, describes the propensity score matching and difference-in-differences strategies, presents the regression specifications, and discusses threats to internal and external validity. Particular attention is given to the assumptions underlying each identification strategy and the sensitivity analyses designed to assess whether violations of those assumptions could alter the substantive conclusions. The chapter also addresses the ethical considerations associated with the use of publicly available administrative data and the study's compliance with Indiana University's Institutional Review Board requirements.

**Chapter 4: Results.** Chapter 4 presents the empirical findings, organized by research question. The chapter begins with descriptive statistics and visualizations that characterize the sample and illustrate key patterns in the data, including the distribution of source selection methods across agencies, NAICS codes, and fiscal years, and the univariate relationships between method choice and outcome variables. It then reports the results of the propensity score matching analysis, including covariate balance diagnostics and average treatment effect estimates; the difference-in-differences estimates exploiting the NDAA Section 813 policy change; and the regression models with interaction terms that test the moderating hypotheses. Sensitivity analyses and robustness checks—including alternative matching specifications, placebo tests, and subgroup analyses—are presented alongside the primary results. Each set of results is interpreted in relation to the theoretical predictions and research hypotheses developed in Chapter 2.

**Chapter 5: Discussion and Conclusions.** Chapter 5 synthesizes the empirical findings, discusses their implications for theory, policy, and practice, acknowledges the study's limitations, and identifies directions for future research. The chapter returns to the research questions posed in this introduction and provides integrated answers based on the totality of the evidence. It discusses the theoretical implications for public value theory, transaction cost economics, and procurement contract design, identifying areas where the findings confirm, extend, or challenge existing theory. It also offers specific, evidence-based recommendations for acquisition policymakers and practitioners, addressing Research Question 7. The chapter discusses the study's limitations with candor, distinguishing between limitations that can be addressed in future research and those that are inherent to the archival quantitative approach. It concludes with a reflection on the study's contribution to the broader project of improving public procurement and enhancing public value, and outlines a research agenda for the next phase of investigation.
