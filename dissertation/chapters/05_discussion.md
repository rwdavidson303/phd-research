# Chapter 5: Discussion and Conclusions

## 5.1 Summary of Key Findings

This dissertation set out to answer a deceptively simple question: Does the method by which the federal government selects a contractor—specifically, whether it employs a quality-evaluating best-value tradeoff (QE) or a negotiated fixed-price lowest-price technically acceptable (NFP) approach—have a measurable effect on the outcomes that matter to taxpayers? The theoretical framework developed in Chapter 2, drawing on transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985, 1996), public value theory (Moore, 1995; Benington & Moore, 2011), auction theory (Vickrey, 1961; Che, 1993), and incomplete contract theory (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Hart & Moore, 1988), predicted that best-value tradeoff source selection would produce superior public-value outcomes for complex service and technology procurements. The prediction rested on a coherent causal logic: by evaluating quality alongside price, tradeoff mechanisms should select higher-capability contractors, reduce strategic underbidding, and produce better lifecycle cost performance. The empirical evidence reviewed in Chapter 2—principally from defense acquisition studies (Osman et al., 2017; Watson, 2015; Baker, 2016)—offered suggestive but methodologically limited support for this prediction.

The present study tested these predictions with a large-sample quasi-experimental analysis of 15,477 competitive federal service and information technology contract awards above $250,000 from the second quarter of fiscal year 2024, drawn from USAspending.gov archival data. The primary comparison was between Quality-Evaluating (QE, n = 1,379) procurement designs—operationalized through the FPDS `source_selection_process` field as tradeoff awards—and Negotiated Fixed-Price (NFP, n = 2,490) designs. The identification strategy employed propensity score matching (PSM) to address selection on observables, supplemented by OLS, negative binomial, and logistic regression models with agency and NAICS fixed effects.

The results, presented in full in Chapter 4, can be summarized as follows:

**Hypothesis 1 (Cost Growth)—Not Supported.** The central hypothesis of the dissertation predicted that QE procurement design would be associated with lower post-award cost growth compared to NFP design. The OLS estimate in the full sample was small, positive, and statistically nonsignificant (coefficient = 0.413, *p* = 0.377). In the PSM-matched sample, the estimate was marginally significant but in the *opposite* direction from the theoretical prediction (coefficient = 1.033, *p* = 0.061), suggesting that QE design may be associated with slightly *higher* rather than lower cost growth. The null main effect on cost growth was the most robust finding of the study: it held across all specifications, subsamples, and robustness checks.

**Hypothesis 2 (Modification Intensity)—Not Supported.** The negative binomial generalized linear model found no significant difference in modification intensity between QE and NFP designs (coefficient = -0.034, *p* = 0.449). The direction of the point estimate was consistent with theory (slightly fewer modifications for QE), but the magnitude was trivial and the confidence interval comfortably spanned zero.

**Hypothesis 6 (Single-Bid Rates)—Not Supported at alpha = 0.05.** The logistic regression yielded a marginally significant result (*p* = 0.085) with an odds ratio of 0.752, suggesting that QE design is associated with approximately 25% lower odds of a single-bid outcome. While the direction of the effect was consistent with the theoretical prediction that tradeoff evaluation encourages broader competitive participation, the result did not reach conventional statistical significance thresholds.

**Hypothesis 8 (Complexity Moderation)—Supported.** The one clearly supported hypothesis was the interaction effect. Award size—used as a proxy for procurement complexity—significantly moderated the relationship between procurement design and cost growth (interaction coefficient = 0.619, *p* = 0.003). At smaller award sizes, QE design was associated with lower cost growth relative to NFP; at larger award sizes, this advantage reversed, with QE design associated with higher cost growth. This crossover interaction was the most substantively important finding of the study.

**Hypotheses 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, and 10—Not Directly Testable.** Six of the ten hypotheses articulated in Chapter 2 were not directly testable with the archival data available for this study. These hypotheses concerned solicitation quality as a mediator (H3), bid protest regime clarity and procurement cycle times (H4), oversight layer effects on schedule and cost (H5), competition intensity as a mediator of solicitation design effects (H7), the dual effects of protest availability on delay and accountability (H9), and the effects of standardized best-value guidance on outcome variance (H10). The data requirements for these hypotheses—including CPARS performance ratings, content analysis of solicitation documents, cross-jurisdictional institutional data, and natural experiment identification—exceed what is available in the FPDS and USAspending.gov public datasets. These hypotheses are addressed as future research directions in Section 5.6.

The propensity score matching diagnostics indicated that 1,093 of the 1,379 treated (QE) units were successfully matched (79.3%). Most covariates achieved satisfactory balance after matching, with standardized mean differences reduced by 57% to 80%. NAICS sector balance was less improved (approximately 15% reduction in the standardized mean difference for `naics_2digit`), indicating residual industry-level confounding that the matching procedure did not fully resolve. All robustness checks—including full-sample estimation, agency subsamples, NAICS subsamples, and outlier exclusion—confirmed the pattern of null main effects and the significant complexity moderation.

In summary, the empirical results of this dissertation are characterized by a striking pattern: the theoretical predictions derived from multiple converging intellectual traditions were largely not confirmed by the data. The main effects of source selection method on cost growth and modification intensity were null, the competition effect was marginal, and only the complexity moderation hypothesis received clear empirical support. The implications of these findings—for theory, for policy, and for future research—are the subject of the sections that follow.

## 5.2 Interpretation of Results

### 5.2.1 The Non-Finding on Cost Growth (H1): Why Source Selection Method May Not Drive Post-Award Cost Outcomes

The null result on Hypothesis 1 is the most consequential finding of this study, and it warrants careful interpretation. The theoretical prediction was unambiguous: transaction cost economics, auction theory, and incomplete contract theory all converge on the expectation that price-only selection mechanisms will produce higher post-award cost growth for complex procurements, because such mechanisms select for aggressive pricing, attract bidders prone to strategic underbidding, and fail to screen for contractor capability (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Williamson, 1985; Thaler, 1988). The prior empirical literature, though methodologically limited, was generally consistent with this prediction (Osman et al., 2017; Watson, 2015). The finding that QE design was not associated with lower cost growth—and was, in the matched sample, marginally associated with *higher* cost growth—challenges these expectations and demands explanation.

Several interpretations merit consideration. The first and most straightforward is that the theoretical prediction, while logically sound in the abstract, rests on assumptions about the procurement environment that may not hold in the contemporary federal context. The theory assumes that source selection method is the binding constraint on contractor selection—that is, that the choice between LPTA and tradeoff is the principal determinant of which contractor wins and at what price. In practice, however, the federal procurement system embeds numerous other mechanisms that influence contractor selection and post-award performance: responsibility determinations, past performance information systems, small business set-aside programs, organizational conflict of interest screening, security clearance requirements, and task-order competition under indefinite-delivery vehicles. These mechanisms collectively impose quality floors that may reduce the marginal contribution of the source selection method itself. If the federal procurement system already screens out the lowest-quality contractors through non-source-selection mechanisms, then the incremental benefit of adding quality evaluation to the source selection process may be smaller than theory predicts.

A second interpretation relates to the endogeneity of method choice. Although the propensity score matching procedure was designed to address selection on observables, it cannot eliminate selection on unobservables. Contracting officers who choose QE/tradeoff evaluation may do so precisely because the procurement in question is inherently more complex, risky, or uncertain than observably similar procurements awarded through NFP/LPTA. If unobserved complexity drives both the choice of tradeoff evaluation and higher post-award cost growth, then the true treatment effect of QE design may be negative (cost-reducing), but the observed estimate is biased upward by the confounding influence of unobserved complexity. Under this interpretation, the null or marginally positive estimate represents the net effect of a genuine cost-reducing treatment effect partially offset by a positive selection effect. This interpretation is consistent with the theoretical framework but cannot be confirmed or rejected with observational data alone.

A third interpretation involves the distinction between cost growth as a signal of poor performance and cost growth as a reflection of legitimate scope evolution. Not all modifications that increase contract value represent contractor failure or strategic underbidding. Many modifications reflect changing government requirements, additional work the government wishes the contractor to perform, or the exercise of pre-priced options that were always part of the intended procurement. If QE-awarded contracts tend to involve requirements that evolve more over the contract period—because, for example, complex technology procurements are inherently less predictable—then higher cost growth in QE contracts may reflect scope evolution rather than cost control failure. The archival data do not permit the analyst to distinguish between these types of modifications, a limitation acknowledged in Chapter 3 and elaborated in Section 5.5.

A fourth interpretation concerns the temporal scope of the data. The study analyzed a single quarter of FY2024, and the cost growth variable captures only the modifications that had been processed as of the data extraction date. Many contracts in the sample were still in active performance, meaning that their ultimate cost growth had not yet been realized. If the cost growth patterns of QE and NFP contracts diverge over time—with QE contracts exhibiting lower cost growth in later years as their initial quality advantages compound—then a cross-sectional snapshot may fail to detect an effect that would emerge in a longer panel. Conversely, if QE contracts front-load their modifications (because tradeoff evaluations tend to result in more detailed statements of work that are refined through early-period modifications), the snapshot may overestimate QE cost growth relative to its long-run trajectory.

A fifth interpretation, informed by the broader institutional economics literature, is that the distinction between LPTA and tradeoff may simply matter less than the quality of the procurement process as a whole. Kelman (1990, 2005) argued that the federal procurement system's emphasis on procedural compliance and rule-following, while valuable for preventing corruption and ensuring transparency, can create a bureaucratic environment in which the formal evaluation method is less consequential than the skill, judgment, and effort of the acquisition professionals who implement it. Under this view, a well-executed LPTA procurement by an experienced contracting officer with clear requirements may outperform a poorly executed tradeoff by an inexperienced officer with vague requirements. The source selection method is a necessary but not sufficient condition for good outcomes; its effect is contingent on the human capital, organizational support, and institutional context in which it is applied.

The robustness of the null finding across all specifications and subsamples strengthens the inference that the result reflects a genuine empirical pattern rather than a statistical artifact. The null was observed in the full sample, in the PSM-matched sample, in agency subsamples, in NAICS subsamples, and with and without outlier exclusion. This consistency across multiple analytical frames suggests that whatever the mechanism underlying the null result, it is pervasive rather than confined to a particular subset of the data.

### 5.2.2 Modification Intensity (H2): Contract Management vs. Contract Selection

The null result on Hypothesis 2—no significant difference in modification intensity between QE and NFP designs—extends the interpretive challenge raised by the cost growth finding. The theory of strategic underbidding (Jung et al., 2013; Bajari et al., 2014) predicts that contractors selected on price alone will be more likely to engage in post-award cost recovery through change orders and modifications, because their initial pricing was strategically low. The null finding on modification intensity suggests that this mechanism, while theoretically compelling, does not manifest as a detectable difference in modification counts across source selection methods in the federal procurement data.

Several factors may explain this result. First, modification intensity in the federal system is driven as much by the government's behavior as by the contractor's. Federal contracts are modified for a wide variety of reasons: funding increments, option exercises, scope additions, administrative changes, novations, and changes in the period of performance. Many of these modifications originate with the government rather than the contractor and reflect the government's evolving needs rather than the contractor's post-award strategy. If government-initiated modifications dominate the modification count, then the contractor's source-selection-induced behavior may be swamped by the government's contract management behavior, making it difficult to detect a source-selection effect.

Second, the federal contract administration system imposes constraints on post-award behavior that may attenuate the strategic underbidding mechanism. Contracting officers are trained to scrutinize modification requests, and the FAR provides procedural safeguards (including the changes clause, equitable adjustment provisions, and constructive change doctrines) that structure the renegotiation process in ways that limit a contractor's ability to extract rents through opportunistic modification requests (Cibinic et al., 2015). These institutional safeguards may function as a substitute for the screening function that tradeoff evaluation is theorized to provide, reducing the observable difference between QE and NFP contracts in post-award modification patterns.

Third, the modification count variable used in this study is a blunt instrument that does not distinguish between types of modifications. A contract with ten modifications, all of which are routine funding increments, has a very different post-award profile than a contract with ten modifications reflecting disputed change orders and scope disputes. Without data on modification type and the underlying reason for each modification, the count variable captures administrative activity rather than contractual dysfunction. Future research that can classify modifications by type—distinguishing between administrative, scope-expanding, cost-adjusting, and dispute-related modifications—would provide a more precise test of the strategic underbidding hypothesis.

The null result on modification intensity, considered alongside the null result on cost growth, suggests a broader conclusion: the source selection method, by itself, may not be a primary determinant of post-award contract behavior. This does not mean that source selection is irrelevant—the complexity moderation finding (discussed in Section 5.2.4) indicates that it matters under certain conditions—but it does suggest that the contract management environment, the quality of requirements, and the competence of the acquisition workforce may be more consequential for post-award outcomes than the formal evaluation method used to select the contractor.

### 5.2.3 The Marginal Competition Effect (H6): Procurement Design and Market Participation

The marginal result on Hypothesis 6—QE design associated with approximately 25% lower odds of a single-bid outcome, with *p* = 0.085—is perhaps the most intriguing of the study's findings precisely because it straddles the boundary between statistical significance and non-significance. At conventional thresholds (alpha = 0.05), the result does not warrant rejection of the null hypothesis. At more permissive thresholds sometimes used in exploratory or policy-relevant research (alpha = 0.10), the result would be considered significant. The appropriate interpretation depends on both the statistical evidence and the substantive context.

The theoretical rationale for expecting a competition effect is strong. Auction theory predicts that when evaluations consider quality alongside price, a broader range of firms can compete effectively, because firms with higher quality but higher costs are no longer automatically excluded (Che, 1993; Asker & Cantillon, 2008). Under LPTA, firms that invest in quality, experienced personnel, and innovative approaches face a systematic disadvantage because these investments increase their cost base, making them uncompetitive against low-cost bidders. The LPTA mechanism thus creates a selection environment that discourages quality-oriented firms from participating, narrowing the competitive field. Under tradeoff evaluation, the same quality investments may be rewarded through higher technical scores, encouraging participation by a wider range of firms.

The marginal empirical result is directionally consistent with this theoretical prediction. The odds ratio of 0.752 suggests that QE procurement design is associated with a meaningful—if not statistically certain—reduction in the probability of receiving only a single bid. A 25% reduction in the odds of a single-bid outcome, if real, would have considerable practical significance for the federal procurement system, where single-bid rates have been a persistent concern for the Government Accountability Office and congressional oversight bodies (GAO, 2024).

The failure to reach statistical significance at conventional thresholds may reflect insufficient statistical power rather than a genuinely null effect. The single-bid outcome variable is binary, and logistic regression on binary outcomes typically requires larger sample sizes than linear regression on continuous outcomes to detect effects of comparable magnitude. The subsample of QE contracts (n = 1,379) may not provide sufficient power to detect a competition effect that genuinely exists but is modest in magnitude. Alternatively, the marginal significance may reflect genuine heterogeneity in the competition effect across procurement contexts: QE design may substantially increase competition for some types of procurements while having little effect on others, producing a diluted average effect that is only marginally significant in the pooled sample.

These considerations counsel against dismissing the competition finding entirely while also cautioning against over-interpreting it. The result warrants further investigation with larger samples, longer time horizons, and finer-grained measures of competitive participation (e.g., the number of offers received, rather than the binary single-bid indicator). It also motivates qualitative inquiry into the decision processes of potential offerors: Do firms report that they are more likely to bid on tradeoff solicitations than on LPTA solicitations? If so, what features of the tradeoff process encourage their participation? These questions are addressed as future research directions in Section 5.6.

### 5.2.4 The Complexity Moderation Finding (H8): When Source Selection Method Matters

The one clearly supported hypothesis—that procurement complexity, proxied by award size, moderates the relationship between source selection method and cost growth—is the most substantively important finding of this study. The interaction coefficient of 0.619 (*p* = 0.003) indicates that award size significantly modifies the QE-NFP cost growth differential. At smaller award sizes, QE design is associated with lower cost growth relative to NFP; as award size increases, this advantage diminishes and eventually reverses, with QE design associated with higher cost growth at the largest award sizes.

This crossover interaction pattern invites several interpretations, and its implications for theory and practice are considerable.

The most straightforward interpretation, consistent with transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985, 1996) and incomplete contract theory (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001), is that the value of quality-based evaluation depends on the contractual context. For smaller, less complex procurements, the tradeoff evaluation process may genuinely select higher-capability contractors who perform with less cost growth, validating the theoretical prediction. For larger, more complex procurements, however, the relationship may be confounded by factors that the simple QE-NFP distinction does not capture. Larger procurements tend to involve longer performance periods, more stakeholders, greater requirements uncertainty, and more extensive government-furnished support—all of which increase the probability of legitimate scope changes and modifications regardless of which contractor was selected and how. The tradeoff evaluation process, while potentially selecting a better contractor, may not be sufficient to contain the inherent cost volatility of large, complex procurement programs.

An alternative interpretation invokes the concept of diminishing marginal returns to evaluation sophistication. For small procurements, the incremental information value of a tradeoff evaluation (relative to LPTA) is high: the difference between a well-qualified contractor and a barely-qualified low bidder can be decisive in determining whether the contract succeeds. For large procurements, additional factors—contract type, incentive structure, program management capability, organizational governance—may dominate the outcome to such a degree that the source selection method becomes a secondary consideration. In the language of statistical explanation, the variance in outcomes attributable to source selection method may represent a smaller share of total variance for large procurements than for small ones.

A third interpretation relates to the measurement of complexity itself. Award size is used as a proxy for procurement complexity, but the relationship between size and complexity is not monotonic. Some very large procurements are straightforward (e.g., large-volume commodity buys), while some smaller procurements are extremely complex (e.g., specialized cybersecurity assessments). If the proxy imperfectly captures the underlying complexity construct, the observed interaction pattern may reflect the imperfection of the proxy rather than a genuine crossover in the treatment effect. More precise measures of complexity—including NAICS-level differentiation, contract type, and performance period duration—could refine the finding.

Regardless of the precise mechanism, the moderation finding carries an important substantive message: source selection method does not have a uniform effect across the procurement landscape. The effect is context-dependent, and the context that matters most is the scale and complexity of the procurement. This finding has direct implications for policy and practice, discussed in Section 5.4, and for theoretical refinement, discussed in Section 5.3.

## 5.3 Theoretical Implications

### 5.3.1 Implications for Public Value Theory

The results of this study contribute to the ongoing operationalization and empirical testing of public value theory (Moore, 1995, 2013) in the procurement context. Moore's original formulation posited that public managers should seek to create value across multiple dimensions—substantive, political, and operational—but the framework was developed as a conceptual guide for strategic management rather than as a testable empirical model. The procurement literature has embraced public value as a normative lens for evaluating outcomes (Benington & Moore, 2011; Malacina et al., 2022; Wylie, 2021), but the empirical grounding of public value claims in procurement has remained thin.

This study's findings complicate the application of public value theory to source selection policy in two important ways. First, the null main effects on cost growth and modification intensity challenge the assumption that the procurement mechanism is a primary lever for creating public value. If the method by which contractors are selected does not significantly affect the cost performance of the resulting contracts, then the locus of public value creation in procurement may lie elsewhere—in requirements development, contract management, workforce competence, or organizational governance. This does not invalidate public value theory, but it redirects its application: rather than focusing on the source selection method as the critical design choice, public value theory may be more productively applied to the broader procurement system, including the pre-solicitation planning, post-award oversight, and institutional infrastructure that surrounds the evaluation decision.

Second, the complexity moderation finding suggests that public value creation through procurement mechanism design is contingent on context in ways that Moore's framework does not explicitly theorize. Moore's strategic triangle (substantive value, political legitimacy, operational feasibility) provides a useful heuristic for evaluating whether a particular management strategy is likely to create public value, but it does not specify the conditions under which one procurement mechanism will outperform another. The empirical finding that source selection method matters more for smaller, less complex procurements than for larger, more complex ones provides an empirical anchor for a more nuanced application of public value theory—one that recognizes that the value-creating potential of any particular institutional design depends on the characteristics of the transactions it governs.

For the broader public value literature, this study illustrates both the promise and the challenge of moving from theoretical prescription to empirical demonstration. The promise lies in the ability to use large-scale administrative data to test claims about institutional design and value creation. The challenge lies in the complexity of the causal pathways connecting institutional design to outcomes: the relationship is mediated by multiple intervening variables (contractor behavior, government management, market conditions, requirements quality) and moderated by contextual factors (complexity, size, sector) that make simple main-effect tests insufficient. Future applications of public value theory to procurement should adopt the kind of conditional, context-sensitive approach that the moderation finding supports.

### 5.3.2 Implications for Transaction Cost Economics

Transaction cost economics (TCE) provides the most direct theoretical predictions tested in this study. Williamson (1985, 1996) argued that the efficiency of different governance structures depends on the characteristics of the transactions they govern—specifically, asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency. Applied to procurement, TCE predicts that simple, well-specified, low-uncertainty transactions are efficiently governed by price competition (analogous to market governance), while complex, uncertain, and relationship-specific transactions require more elaborate governance mechanisms that evaluate quality, capability, and relational fitness (analogous to hybrid or hierarchical governance). The best-value tradeoff mechanism, which evaluates multiple dimensions of contractor capability, is the procurement analog of Williamson's hybrid governance form.

The null main effect on cost growth does not directly contradict TCE, because TCE's prediction is conditional rather than unconditional: the theory predicts that tradeoff will outperform LPTA *when transaction complexity is high*, not that tradeoff will always outperform LPTA regardless of context. The complexity moderation finding (H8) is, in fact, broadly consistent with TCE's conditional logic. The finding that QE design is associated with lower cost growth for smaller procurements and higher cost growth for larger ones can be interpreted through a TCE lens: for smaller, less complex transactions, the quality screening provided by tradeoff evaluation adds value that outweighs the additional transaction costs of the evaluation process; for larger, more complex transactions, the governance challenges overwhelm the screening benefit, and other institutional mechanisms (contract type, incentive structure, program management) become more consequential.

However, the specific pattern of the interaction—with the QE advantage *reversing* rather than merely attenuating at higher complexity levels—is not straightforwardly predicted by TCE. The theory would predict that the advantage of tradeoff evaluation increases with complexity, not that it reverses. The reversal suggests that additional mechanisms are at work. One possibility, consistent with Williamson's (1996) recognition of measurement difficulties, is that the complexity proxy (award size) captures not only transaction complexity but also organizational and political complexity—the number of stakeholders, the intensity of oversight, the political salience of the procurement—that introduce governance costs unrelated to the source selection method. Another possibility is that the federal procurement system's existing governance infrastructure (contract administration, audit, inspection, compliance review) provides adequate quality assurance for large procurements regardless of source selection method, making the incremental contribution of tradeoff evaluation negligible or even counterproductive (by adding evaluation costs without commensurate screening benefits) at higher complexity levels.

These findings suggest that TCE's application to public procurement requires refinement. The theory's emphasis on matching governance structure to transaction characteristics remains sound, but the specific mapping—price competition for simple transactions, quality evaluation for complex ones—may need to account for the institutional context in which the governance decision is embedded. In the federal procurement system, that context includes regulatory constraints, oversight mechanisms, workforce characteristics, and political dynamics that are absent from Williamson's private-sector-focused framework. TCE's predictive power in the procurement domain may be enhanced by incorporating these institutional moderators explicitly into the theoretical model.

### 5.3.3 Implications for Auction Theory and Mechanism Design

Auction theory provides the most formal and mathematically precise predictions tested in this study. The seminal work of Che (1993) demonstrated that scoring auctions—which evaluate both price and quality—can outperform price-only auctions when quality is variable and verifiable, because scoring rules induce firms to compete on quality as well as price. Bajari and Tadelis (2001) showed that when contract specifications are incomplete, competitive bidding (analogous to LPTA) induces strategic underbidding and costly renegotiation, while negotiated procurement (analogous to tradeoff) can produce more efficient outcomes by allowing the buyer to screen on capabilities and relational attributes. Bajari, McMillan, and Tadelis (2009) extended this analysis with empirical evidence from private-sector construction, finding that negotiation outperforms competitive bidding for complex projects.

The empirical results of this study are only partially consistent with these theoretical predictions. The null main effect on cost growth is inconsistent with the strong form of the Bajari and Tadelis (2001) prediction, which implies that competitive bidding should produce *more* renegotiation and cost growth than negotiated procurement for complex acquisitions. The marginal competition effect is directionally consistent with Che's (1993) prediction that scoring auctions attract broader participation, but the effect does not reach statistical significance. The complexity moderation finding is consistent with the Bajari and Tadelis (2001) framework insofar as it demonstrates that the source selection method's effect depends on contractual complexity, but the specific pattern—QE advantage at low complexity, QE disadvantage at high complexity—is the reverse of what the framework predicts. Bajari and Tadelis predict that the advantage of negotiation (tradeoff) should be *greater* for more complex contracts, not lesser.

This discrepancy between theory and evidence may reflect important differences between the institutional settings studied. The auction theory literature typically models procurement as a bilateral exchange between a buyer and sellers, with the auction mechanism as the primary governance instrument. In the federal procurement context, the auction mechanism (source selection process) operates within a dense institutional environment that includes pre-solicitation market research, responsibility determinations, debriefings, protest rights, contract administration, and post-award oversight. These institutional features may substitute for some of the functions that auction theory assigns to the mechanism itself—particularly the screening and incentive functions. If the institutional environment already provides significant quality screening (through responsibility determinations and past performance reviews) and incentive alignment (through contract type, award fee provisions, and performance monitoring), then the incremental contribution of the source selection mechanism may be smaller than auction theory—which abstracts away from institutional context—would predict.

The findings also speak to a question that has received less attention in the theoretical literature: the conditions under which the *costs* of scoring auctions outweigh their *benefits*. Scoring auctions are more expensive to conduct than price-only auctions—they require more elaborate evaluation criteria, larger source selection teams, longer evaluation periods, and more detailed documentation. These costs are justified when the screening benefit is large, but when the institutional environment already provides adequate screening, the additional costs may reduce net value. The crossover interaction pattern observed in this study is consistent with this cost-benefit interpretation: for smaller procurements, the screening benefit of tradeoff evaluation exceeds its cost; for larger procurements, the cost of the more elaborate evaluation process may not be justified by incremental screening benefits, particularly when other governance mechanisms are already operating.

For auction theory, these findings suggest that the transition from theoretical models to empirical prediction in institutional settings requires explicit modeling of the institutional context. Pure mechanism design results derived from stripped-down models may not translate directly to procurement environments where the mechanism operates alongside a complex system of governance institutions. This is not a criticism of auction theory per se—Milgrom (2004) explicitly recognized the importance of practical market design considerations that extend beyond formal models—but it is a call for greater attention to the institutional embeddedness of procurement mechanisms in both theoretical and empirical work.

## 5.4 Practical Implications for Procurement Policy

### 5.4.1 Implications for Federal Acquisition Professionals

The findings of this study carry several practical implications for the federal acquisition workforce—the contracting officers, contracting officer's representatives, program managers, and procurement executives who make daily decisions about source selection method, contract structure, and post-award management.

**The null main effect counsels against dogmatism in either direction.** Practitioners who believe that tradeoff evaluation is always superior to LPTA, and those who believe that LPTA should be the default for most procurements, will find that the data do not support either categorical position. The absence of a significant main effect on cost growth or modification intensity suggests that the source selection method, by itself, is not the dominant determinant of post-award contract performance. This does not mean that source selection is irrelevant—the complexity moderation finding demonstrates that it matters under specific conditions—but it does mean that acquisition professionals should focus at least as much attention on other determinants of procurement success: the quality of the requirements document, the rigor of the market research, the competence of the evaluation team, the structure of the contract type and incentive provisions, and the quality of post-award surveillance and contract administration.

**The complexity moderation finding provides actionable guidance.** The empirical evidence suggests that source selection method has its greatest impact on smaller, less complex procurements. For these acquisitions, tradeoff evaluation appears to provide genuine screening value that translates into lower cost growth. This finding aligns with a commonsense intuition that may be underappreciated in practice: for a $300,000 professional services contract, the difference between a highly capable contractor and a barely-qualified low bidder can be decisive, and the tradeoff evaluation process provides the mechanism to distinguish between them. For a $50 million systems integration contract, by contrast, the outcome depends on so many factors beyond the contractor's initial selection—requirements stability, funding availability, organizational governance, technology evolution—that the source selection method recedes in relative importance.

The practical implication is that acquisition professionals should be particularly attentive to the source selection method choice for smaller, moderately complex procurements—the very procurements that may receive less institutional attention than the flagship programs. The irony is that the current institutional incentives push in the opposite direction: large, high-visibility procurements receive extensive source selection planning and typically use tradeoff evaluation, while smaller procurements—where the source selection method may matter most—are more likely to default to LPTA for administrative convenience.

**The marginal competition finding warrants practitioner attention.** The suggestive evidence that tradeoff evaluation may reduce single-bid rates is relevant to acquisition professionals who are concerned about the competitiveness of their procurements. While the evidence is not conclusive, it supports the intuition that firms are more willing to invest the effort and cost of preparing proposals when the evaluation criteria give them an opportunity to differentiate on quality rather than competing solely on price. Contracting officers who consistently receive single-bid responses to LPTA solicitations might consider whether switching to a tradeoff approach would broaden the competitive field—particularly for procurements where quality differentiation among potential offerors is significant.

### 5.4.2 Implications for the FAR and Regulatory Policy

The current FAR framework (Part 15) provides guidance on the circumstances favoring LPTA versus tradeoff but does not prescribe rigid rules. This regulatory flexibility is, in light of this study's findings, appropriate. The null main effect suggests that a regulatory mandate favoring one method over the other across the board would not produce systematic improvements in contract outcomes. The complexity moderation finding, however, suggests that more targeted regulatory guidance—specifying the conditions under which tradeoff evaluation adds the most value—could be beneficial.

Specifically, the findings support regulatory refinements along the following lines. First, the FAR's existing guidance that LPTA is appropriate when "the government expects to receive more than one offer" and "price is the dominant consideration" (FAR 15.101-2) could be supplemented with guidance noting that for smaller service and technology contracts in the $250,000 to $5 million range, the quality screening provided by tradeoff evaluation appears to offer meaningful cost-performance benefits. Second, the regulatory framework could be enhanced to require a more explicit analysis of the expected value of tradeoff evaluation relative to LPTA—not a formal cost-benefit calculation, but a documented assessment of whether the procurement context is one in which quality differentiation among offerors is likely to be consequential for downstream outcomes. Third, the data suggest that the legislative restrictions on LPTA use enacted in the FY2017 and FY2018 NDAAs (Pub. L. 114-328, 2016; Pub. L. 115-91, 2017), which applied primarily to DoD acquisitions of knowledge-based services and cybersecurity, were directionally sound in targeting their restrictions at categories where quality variation is significant, but the empirical evidence for the cost-performance benefits of tradeoff in these categories is weaker than anticipated.

The findings also have implications for the ongoing debate about procurement simplification. Some reformers advocate for expanding the use of simplified evaluation procedures—including LPTA and lowest-price approaches—to reduce the administrative burden of the procurement process. The null main effect on cost growth might be interpreted as supporting this position: if tradeoff evaluation does not produce measurably better cost outcomes, why incur the additional administrative cost? However, the complexity moderation finding complicates this argument by demonstrating that the benefits of tradeoff evaluation are real but context-dependent. A simplification agenda that eliminates tradeoff evaluation for smaller, moderately complex procurements—precisely where it appears to add the most value—would be counterproductive.

### 5.4.3 Implications for Congressional Oversight

Congressional oversight of federal procurement has historically focused on headline-grabbing failures: cost overruns on major defense programs, schedule delays on high-profile IT modernization efforts, and vendor performance scandals. The source selection method debate has entered the congressional discourse primarily through the NDAA LPTA restrictions, which reflected a bipartisan concern that agencies were defaulting to LPTA for complex acquisitions with adverse consequences.

This study's findings suggest that the congressional focus on source selection method as a primary lever for improving procurement outcomes may be misdirected—or, more precisely, may be insufficient as a stand-alone reform strategy. The null main effect implies that changing the source selection method, without corresponding improvements in requirements development, contract management, and workforce competence, is unlikely to produce the outcome improvements that policymakers seek. The moderation finding suggests that the NDAA restrictions, by targeting specific categories of complex acquisitions, were aiming at the right targets but may not have addressed the most consequential procurement challenge.

Congressional oversight might more productively focus on several complementary fronts: (a) investing in the acquisition workforce, whose skill and judgment the evidence suggests is at least as consequential as the formal evaluation method; (b) improving the quality and accessibility of contract performance data, including making CPARS data available for research purposes so that the quality dimension of the LPTA-versus-tradeoff question can be addressed empirically; (c) examining the post-award contract management environment, where the evidence suggests that the determinants of cost growth and modification intensity reside; and (d) supporting research on the competition effects of procurement design, where the marginal finding on single-bid rates suggests an avenue through which source selection method may affect outcomes that is distinct from—and potentially more consequential than—the direct cost-performance pathway.

The complexity moderation finding also provides a framework for more targeted oversight. Rather than asking whether agencies are using the "right" source selection method in the aggregate, oversight bodies could examine whether agencies are systematically mismatching method to context—using LPTA for smaller, moderately complex procurements where tradeoff evaluation would add value, or investing in elaborate tradeoff evaluations for large procurements where the source selection method may matter less than other governance decisions.

## 5.5 Limitations

Every empirical study is bounded by the design choices, data constraints, and methodological trade-offs that define its scope. This section provides a candid accounting of the limitations of the present study, both to guide interpretation of the findings and to identify the most productive directions for future research.

### 5.5.1 Temporal Scope (Single Quarter of FY2024)

The most significant design limitation is the study's reliance on a single quarter of fiscal year 2024 data. While the resulting sample (n = 15,477) is large enough to provide adequate statistical power for the primary analyses, the single-quarter design introduces several concerns.

First, the contracts in the sample are at varying stages of their performance periods. Some may be newly awarded, with little or no post-award modification history; others may be near completion, with a full record of modifications and cost adjustments. The cost growth variable therefore captures a mixture of interim and near-final cost trajectories, potentially introducing measurement noise that attenuates the estimated treatment effects. A panel design that tracks contracts from award to completion would provide a more precise measure of lifecycle cost growth.

Second, the single-quarter design prevents the identification of time trends in the relationship between source selection method and outcomes. If the procurement system has been evolving over time—with improvements in data quality, changes in agency source selection practices, or shifts in the competitive landscape—a single cross-section cannot detect these dynamics. The difference-in-differences strategy outlined in Chapter 3 as a supplementary identification approach was not implemented in the final analysis because the single-quarter data did not provide the temporal variation necessary for pre-post comparisons.

Third, the data coincide with a specific macroeconomic and policy context—the post-pandemic federal contracting environment of FY2024—that may not be representative of other periods. Federal procurement in this period was influenced by continuing resolution budgets, workforce reductions, technology modernization initiatives, and evolving cybersecurity requirements. To the extent that these contextual factors differentially affect QE and NFP procurements, the findings may not generalize to other fiscal periods.

### 5.5.2 Proxy Variable Limitations (No Direct Source Selection Process Field)

Although the FPDS includes a `source_selection_process` field that was introduced in 2020, the operationalization of the treatment variable in this study relied on a proxy classification based on the `type_of_contract_pricing` and `extent_competed` fields to distinguish between quality-evaluating (QE) and negotiated fixed-price (NFP) procurement designs. This proxy approach was necessitated by data quality concerns with the `source_selection_process` field, including incomplete reporting and inconsistent coding across agencies.

The proxy classification introduces measurement error. Not all NFP contracts were necessarily awarded through LPTA evaluation, and not all QE contracts necessarily employed a full tradeoff analysis. Some contracts classified as NFP may have used a simplified tradeoff approach; some classified as QE may have involved an evaluation that was closer to LPTA in practice. This measurement error attenuates the estimated treatment effects toward zero, meaning that the null findings may partly reflect classification noise rather than a genuinely null treatment effect. The magnitude of this attenuation bias cannot be estimated without validation data that link the proxy classification to actual source selection method at the contract level.

The proxy variable limitation is particularly consequential because the treatment contrast—QE versus NFP—is the core comparison of the study. If the proxy imperfectly separates "true" tradeoff evaluations from "true" LPTA evaluations, the estimated treatment effects will be diluted. This limitation could be addressed in future research by using the `source_selection_process` field as the primary treatment variable once data quality improves, or by validating the proxy classification against a sample of solicitation documents that explicitly identify the evaluation method.

### 5.5.3 Missing CPARS Performance Data

The absence of Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS) data from this study is a significant limitation that constrains the interpretation of the findings. CPARS provides the federal government's most direct and systematic assessment of contractor performance, with ratings on quality, schedule, cost control, management, and regulatory compliance (FAR 42.1502). The theoretical prediction that tradeoff evaluation produces better contractor performance is, at its core, a prediction about quality—a dimension that cost growth and modification intensity capture only indirectly and imperfectly.

It is entirely possible that QE design produces better contractor performance on the quality, schedule, and management dimensions captured by CPARS, even if it does not produce lower cost growth. If so, the null cost growth finding would be consistent with—rather than contradictory to—the theoretical prediction, because the theory predicts that tradeoff evaluation produces better *overall* public value, of which cost performance is only one dimension. The inability to test this possibility is the single most consequential data gap in the study.

CPARS data are not publicly available; access requires a government user account with appropriate authorization. The data are maintained in the Past Performance Information Retrieval System (PPIRS) and are accessible to authorized government personnel for source selection purposes. Securing research access to CPARS data—potentially through a data use agreement with the General Services Administration or the Office of Federal Procurement Policy—would enable a direct test of the quality-performance hypothesis and is the most important direction for future research arising from this study.

### 5.5.4 Archival-Only Design (No Causal Certainty)

The observational, archival nature of the data imposes a fundamental constraint on causal inference. The source selection method is not randomly assigned to contracts; it is chosen by contracting officers based on the characteristics of the requirement, the competitive environment, and agency preferences. This endogeneity means that the estimated treatment effects reflect the combined influence of the source selection method and the selection process by which it was chosen. Propensity score matching addresses selection on observable covariates but cannot address selection on unobservable factors—such as the contracting officer's private assessment of procurement risk, the quality of the requirements document, or the intensity of management attention devoted to the procurement.

The standard econometric approaches to unobserved confounding—instrumental variable estimation and regression discontinuity designs—were not feasible in this study. No valid instrument for source selection method was identified: any factor that influences the choice of evaluation method is likely to also influence outcomes directly, violating the exclusion restriction. No sharp policy discontinuity in source selection method assignment was available in the single-quarter cross-section.

The causal interpretation of the findings is therefore subject to the standard caveats that apply to observational research. The null main effects may reflect a genuinely null treatment effect, or they may reflect unmeasured confounding that masks a real effect. The moderation finding, while statistically robust, is subject to the same caveat: unmeasured factors correlated with both award size and cost growth could produce the observed interaction pattern. The study's findings should be characterized as conditional associations that are consistent with—but not definitive proof of—the causal interpretations offered in Section 5.2.

### 5.5.5 Selection Bias and Endogeneity Concerns

Beyond the general causal inference limitation, specific forms of selection bias warrant discussion. The propensity score matching procedure successfully matched 1,093 of 1,379 treated (QE) units, leaving 286 treated units unmatched. These unmatched units are QE contracts for which no sufficiently similar NFP comparator was available in the data. If the unmatched QE contracts differ systematically from the matched QE contracts—for example, if they represent the most unusual or complex procurements—then the matched-sample estimates may not generalize to the full population of QE procurements. The analysis of the matched sample is therefore interpretable as the average treatment effect on the treated for the subset of QE contracts that have observable comparators in the NFP population.

The balance diagnostics revealed that NAICS sector balance was less improved by matching than other covariates (approximately 15% reduction in standardized mean difference, compared to 57-80% for other covariates). This residual imbalance suggests that industry sector—a potentially important confound, given that different sectors may have different cost growth dynamics regardless of source selection method—is not fully controlled in the matched-sample analysis. The inclusion of NAICS fixed effects in the regression models partially addresses this concern but cannot fully substitute for balance on the matching variables.

Additionally, the timing of data extraction creates a potential survival bias. The sample includes only contracts that were recorded in USAspending.gov as of the extraction date. Contracts that were terminated for convenience, consolidated into other vehicles, or subject to administrative correction and removal from the database before the extraction date would not appear in the sample. If the probability of such events differs between QE and NFP contracts, the surviving sample may not be representative of the full population of awards.

### 5.5.6 Generalizability Constraints

The findings of this study are bounded by several generalizability constraints. First, the sample is limited to federal service and IT contracts above $250,000, which excludes commodity procurements, construction contracts, and procurements below the simplified acquisition threshold. The source selection method question is most consequential for service and IT contracts, where quality variation among offerors is significant, making this sample restriction analytically appropriate but limiting in scope. Whether the findings generalize to commodity procurements (where LPTA is theoretically more appropriate) or construction contracts (which follow distinct FAR provisions and involve different cost growth dynamics) is an open question.

Second, the sample is drawn exclusively from the federal procurement system, which operates under a specific regulatory framework (the FAR and its supplements), a specific institutional infrastructure (FPDS, USAspending.gov, CPARS), and a specific political context (congressional oversight, protest mechanisms, inspector general reviews). State and local procurement systems operate under different statutory frameworks, with different evaluation method requirements, different oversight mechanisms, and different competitive dynamics. International procurement systems—governed by the European Union's procurement directives, the World Trade Organization's Agreement on Government Procurement, or national procurement codes—differ even more substantially. The findings should not be extrapolated to these contexts without additional empirical investigation.

Third, the temporal limitation to FY2024 Q2 means that the findings may not generalize to other periods, particularly periods characterized by different economic conditions, policy regimes, or procurement workforce compositions. The relationship between source selection method and outcomes may be sensitive to macroeconomic factors (e.g., labor market tightness affecting contractor staffing decisions), policy changes (e.g., LPTA restrictions in the NDAAs), and workforce factors (e.g., contracting officer experience levels) that vary over time.

## 5.6 Future Research

The limitations identified in Section 5.5 and the untested hypotheses from Chapter 2 collectively define a rich agenda for future research. This section outlines the most promising directions, organized by research design and data requirements.

### 5.6.1 Phase 2: Mixed-Methods Extension (Surveys and Interviews)

The sequential mixed-methods design described in Chapter 3 positions the present quantitative analysis as Phase 1, to be followed by a qualitative Phase 2 comprising a practitioner survey of federal contracting officers and semi-structured interviews with acquisition leaders. Phase 2 is designed to address the mechanistic questions that the archival data cannot answer.

The survey component would target a stratified sample of federal contracting officers across civilian and defense agencies, with the sampling frame drawn from the Federal Acquisition Institute's (FAI) workforce data. The survey instrument would probe: (a) the factors that drive source selection method choice in practice, including the relative weight of regulatory guidance, agency culture, protest risk, administrative burden, and procurement complexity; (b) practitioners' perceptions of the relationship between source selection method and contract outcomes, including whether they believe tradeoff evaluation produces better results and under what conditions; (c) the quality of requirements documents, evaluated by practitioners on dimensions including clarity, completeness, and alignment with evaluation criteria; and (d) the perceived adequacy of the acquisition workforce for conducting effective tradeoff evaluations.

The interview component would involve 20-30 semi-structured interviews with senior acquisition professionals, including agency chief procurement officers, heads of contracting activities, and source selection authority officials. The interviews would probe the organizational and institutional factors that shape source selection decisions at the agency level, the mechanisms through which source selection method affects post-award management, and the perceived barriers to effective tradeoff evaluation.

Phase 2 would require a separate Institutional Review Board (IRB) application, given the involvement of human subjects. The study design, sampling strategy, and data collection instruments would be informed by the quantitative findings from Phase 1—specifically, the null main effects and the complexity moderation finding, which would provide anchor points for the qualitative inquiry. This sequential design ensures that the qualitative investigation is empirically grounded rather than based on abstract assumptions about the source selection process.

### 5.6.2 Multi-Year Panel Analysis

The single-quarter cross-sectional design of the present study limits the ability to track contracts over their full lifecycle, identify time trends, and exploit policy changes for identification. A multi-year panel analysis, spanning FY2020 through FY2026 (the full period for which the `source_selection_process` field is available), would address these limitations in several ways.

First, a panel design would enable the measurement of cost growth from award through completion, rather than at a single point in the performance period. Contracts could be tracked longitudinally, with cost growth measured as the cumulative modification-adjusted obligation relative to the initial award value. This approach would capture the full lifecycle cost trajectory and would permit the identification of time-varying patterns—for example, whether QE and NFP contracts exhibit different cost growth trajectories in the early, middle, and late stages of performance.

Second, a panel design would enable difference-in-differences estimation using the NDAA Section 813 LPTA restrictions as a natural experiment. Although these restrictions applied primarily to DoD acquisitions, the policy change created exogenous variation in the use of LPTA for specific categories of procurements. By comparing outcomes before and after the restriction, and between affected and unaffected procurement categories, a DiD design could provide stronger causal identification than cross-sectional PSM.

Third, a panel design would increase statistical power by expanding the sample size across multiple fiscal years and would enable the detection of effects that are small in any single cross-section but cumulative over time. The marginal competition finding (H6), which fell short of conventional significance thresholds in the single-quarter sample, might achieve significance with the additional statistical power of a multi-year sample.

### 5.6.3 CPARS Integration

As discussed in Section 5.5.3, the absence of CPARS data is the most consequential data gap in the present study. Future research that secures research access to CPARS would enable direct testing of the quality-performance hypothesis and would transform the study's outcome framework from a cost-centered analysis to a multidimensional public value assessment.

The integration of CPARS data with FPDS procurement records would require a data use agreement with the administering agency and would need to address confidentiality concerns, given that CPARS ratings are contractor-identifiable and business-sensitive. Several potential pathways exist: (a) a formal research data use agreement modeled on those used for other sensitive government datasets (e.g., IRS return data, Census microdata); (b) collaboration with a government research organization (e.g., the RAND Corporation, the Institute for Defense Analyses, or the Government Accountability Office) that has existing CPARS access; or (c) a congressionally authorized research initiative that mandates the release of anonymized or aggregated CPARS data for procurement research purposes.

The analytical value of CPARS integration would be substantial. With CPARS ratings as outcome variables, the study could test whether QE design produces higher quality ratings, better schedule compliance, and superior management performance—the dimensions of contractor performance that tradeoff evaluation is theorized to improve. If QE design is associated with better CPARS ratings but not lower cost growth, the finding would support a more nuanced interpretation of the tradeoff: QE evaluation selects for quality at a cost that is not lower (and may be slightly higher) than LPTA, but the quality premium is reflected in better performance rather than lower price. This interpretation would be consistent with both public value theory and the value-for-money framework (OECD, 2019; ADB, 2018), which emphasize that the optimal procurement outcome is not the lowest price but the best balance of price and quality.

### 5.6.4 State and International Comparisons

The federal procurement system is one of many procurement regimes worldwide that grapple with the LPTA-versus-tradeoff question. State and local governments in the United States, European Union member states, and countries subject to World Bank or regional development bank procurement rules all operate under frameworks that provide varying degrees of flexibility in source selection method choice (NASPO, 2019; OECD, 2019; European Commission, 2020).

Comparative research across these jurisdictions would provide natural variation in the institutional context that is absent from a single-system study. For example, some U.S. states mandate lowest-price awards for broad categories of procurement, while others have enacted best-value legislation that mirrors the federal FAR framework. The EU's procurement directives explicitly provide for "most economically advantageous tender" (MEAT) evaluation, and several member states have moved to restrict lowest-price-only awards for complex services (Malacina et al., 2022). These cross-jurisdictional differences create quasi-experimental variation that could be exploited to estimate the effect of source selection method in different institutional contexts.

International comparisons would also test the generalizability of the complexity moderation finding. If the pattern observed in this study—QE advantage for smaller procurements, QE disadvantage for larger ones—holds across institutional contexts, it would suggest a robust empirical regularity rooted in the economics of procurement rather than an artifact of the federal system. If the pattern differs across contexts, the comparison would illuminate the institutional factors that moderate the source selection method effect.

### 5.6.5 Natural Experiment Identification

The strongest causal evidence on the source selection method question would come from a natural experiment—a policy change or institutional event that exogenously shifts the use of tradeoff evaluation for a defined population of procurements without simultaneously affecting the outcomes of interest. Several potential natural experiments merit investigation.

The NDAA Section 813 LPTA restrictions, enacted in 2016 and effective in subsequent fiscal years, represent the most prominent candidate. These restrictions required DoD to establish criteria and guidance limiting LPTA use for knowledge-based professional services, cybersecurity, and acquisitions with significant life-cycle costs. A difference-in-differences design comparing affected procurement categories (those subject to the restrictions) with unaffected categories, before and after the restriction took effect, could provide quasi-experimental estimates of the effect of shifting from LPTA to tradeoff evaluation.

Agency-level policy changes offer another source of natural variation. Some federal agencies have issued internal policies restricting or encouraging the use of specific source selection methods for particular categories of procurement. If these policy changes can be identified and dated, they could provide agency-level quasi-experiments that complement the NDAA-based analysis.

Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent federal procurement response created an unusual period in which some agencies relaxed their normal source selection procedures to expedite procurement, while others maintained or strengthened quality evaluation requirements. If these differential agency responses can be documented, they could provide variation in source selection practice that is plausibly exogenous to contract-level outcomes.

The identification and exploitation of natural experiments in procurement policy is a methodological frontier that has received insufficient attention in the literature. The development of a systematic catalog of procurement policy changes—at the federal, agency, and state levels—that could serve as natural experiments for procurement research would be a valuable contribution in its own right.

## 5.7 Conclusion

This dissertation set out to bring empirical evidence to bear on one of the most consequential and persistent debates in public procurement: whether the method by which the government selects a contractor affects the value that taxpayers receive. The question has been debated for decades in practitioner forums, congressional hearings, and academic symposia, but the empirical evidence base has remained thin, fragmented, and methodologically limited. By analyzing 15,477 competitive federal service and IT contract awards with a quasi-experimental research design, this study provides the most comprehensive large-sample empirical examination of the source selection method question to date.

The findings are sobering for those who expected a clear empirical vindication of best-value tradeoff evaluation. The central hypothesis—that quality-evaluating source selection produces lower post-award cost growth than price-focused selection—was not supported. The modification intensity hypothesis was not supported. The competition hypothesis was marginal. Only the complexity moderation hypothesis received clear empirical support. These results do not align neatly with the theoretical predictions of transaction cost economics, auction theory, or the prior empirical literature, and they challenge the assumption—widely held among procurement reformers—that shifting from LPTA to tradeoff evaluation will, by itself, improve procurement outcomes.

It is important, however, to be precise about what the null results do and do not mean. The null finding on cost growth does not mean that source selection method is irrelevant. It means that, in the contemporary federal procurement environment, the source selection method does not appear to be a primary determinant of post-award cost outcomes when considered in isolation from the broader institutional context. The complexity moderation finding demonstrates that the method *does* matter, but its effect is contingent on the scale and complexity of the procurement. The marginal competition finding suggests that the method may affect market participation in ways that warrant further investigation. And the absence of CPARS data means that the most important dimension of the theoretical prediction—that tradeoff evaluation selects for quality—has not been tested.

The study's contribution is therefore as much methodological and agenda-setting as it is substantive. Methodologically, it demonstrates that large-scale quasi-experimental analysis of archival procurement data is both feasible and informative, and it establishes a template for future studies that extend the analysis to longer time periods, additional outcome measures, and alternative institutional contexts. In terms of research agenda, the study identifies the specific data gaps (CPARS, solicitation quality, modification type), design improvements (multi-year panel, natural experiment identification), and institutional comparisons (state, international) that are needed to resolve the questions that remain open.

The practical implications of the findings are nuanced. For acquisition professionals, the message is not that source selection method does not matter but that it is one element of a larger system—a system that includes requirements development, market research, contract structure, workforce competence, and post-award management—and that its contribution to procurement success depends on the context in which it is applied. The complexity moderation finding offers actionable guidance: for smaller, moderately complex service and technology procurements, tradeoff evaluation appears to add screening value that translates into better cost outcomes; for larger procurements, other governance decisions may be more consequential. For policymakers, the findings counsel against simplistic reform strategies that focus exclusively on source selection method and in favor of a systems-level approach that addresses the multiple determinants of procurement performance simultaneously.

The null results are, in their own way, a contribution to the literature. In a research landscape where publication bias favors significant findings (Rosenthal, 1979), and where the policy debate on source selection has been dominated by strong theoretical priors and practitioner intuition, the honest reporting of null results—buttressed by a robust methodological design and comprehensive robustness checks—provides a corrective to assumptions that have shaped procurement policy without adequate empirical foundation. The finding that the source selection method question is more complex, more contingent, and more institutionally embedded than the theoretical literature suggests is itself a substantive contribution that should redirect both scholarly inquiry and policy attention.

The title of this dissertation—"From Lowest Price to Highest Public Value"—frames the aspiration that animates procurement reform. The empirical evidence presented here suggests that the journey from lowest price to highest public value is not accomplished by changing the source selection method alone. It requires attention to the full arc of the procurement lifecycle, from the initial identification of needs through the development of requirements, the conduct of market research, the design of the solicitation, the evaluation of proposals, the structuring of the contract, the management of performance, and the assessment of outcomes. Source selection method is one station on this journey, and the evidence suggests it is neither the first nor the most consequential. The path to public value runs through the entire system, and improving procurement outcomes will require investments in the people, processes, and institutions that constitute that system—not merely a change in the method by which the winning contractor is chosen.
