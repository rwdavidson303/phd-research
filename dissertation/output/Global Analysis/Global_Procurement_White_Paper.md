## 1. Executive Summary

The United States federal government spends more than $700 billion per year on contracts with private companies, making it the largest single buyer of goods and services on the planet. Despite this extraordinary purchasing power, the American procurement system is plagued by cost overruns, schedule delays, protest-driven paralysis, workforce shortages, and a persistent overreliance on lowest-price decision-making. The result is a system that frequently fails to deliver public value for the taxpayer dollar.

This paper examines twelve countries -- South Korea, Chile, the United Kingdom, Estonia, Singapore, Georgia, Australia, New Zealand, Brazil, Canada, Denmark, and the European Union as a whole -- that have built procurement systems outperforming the United States on one or more critical dimensions: speed, cost savings, transparency, anti-corruption, social value, innovation, and competition.

**Key findings:**

- **Single digital platforms work.** South Korea's KONEPS saves an estimated $8 billion per year and reduced bidding time from 30 hours to under 2 hours. Singapore, Chile, Georgia, and Brazil have achieved similar transformations through unified e-procurement platforms.

- **"Not lowest price" mandates improve outcomes.** Australia, Singapore, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom have all codified that price is not the sole evaluation factor -- and their systems deliver better long-term value as a result. The UK's Procurement Act 2023 replaced "Most Economically Advantageous Tender" with "Most Advantageous Tender," explicitly broadening the definition of value beyond economics.

- **Transparency by design prevents corruption.** Georgia built an e-procurement system in ten months for under $1 million and saw corruption perceptions in related government services drop from 97% to 3% in two years. Chile's Observatory monitoring system produced a 67% reduction in conflicts of interest after its 2023 reform.

- **Social value can be systematically integrated.** The UK's Social Value Act mandates a minimum 10% weighting for social value in procurement decisions. Australia's Indigenous Procurement Policy has directed $12.9 billion in opportunities to Indigenous-owned businesses. New Zealand's framework requires a minimum 10% weighting for broader economic outcomes.

- **Speed and simplification are achievable.** Brazil's reverse auction system (Pregao) cut procurement timelines from 90+ days to 17 days. Georgia built its entire national platform in under a year. Korea processes bidding in under two hours where it once took thirty.

- **Innovation procurement is a learnable discipline.** Denmark's pre-commercial procurement programs have brought commercial robotic solutions into hospitals. The EU's innovation partnership framework provides a structured path from R&D to deployment.

**The Model Procurement System for the United States:**

| Element | Source Country | Proposed US Application |
|---|---|---|
| Unified digital platform | South Korea + Estonia | Single portal replacing SAM.gov, FPDS, USASpending, and eSRS |
| Value-for-money evaluation | Singapore + Australia + UK | Mandatory multi-criteria evaluation with price floor of 40% but not ceiling of 100% |
| Transparency and monitoring | Chile + Korea | Independent Observatory with real-time dashboards and beneficial ownership registry |
| Social value integration | UK + New Zealand + Australia | Minimum 10% social value weighting, scored and audited |
| Independent oversight | Canada + Chile | Procurement Ombudsman with investigation authority, separate from GAO |
| Speed and simplification | Brazil + Georgia | Reverse auctions for commodities, proportional requirements, 30-day standard timelines |
| Innovation pathways | Denmark + EU | Pre-commercial procurement authority, innovation partnership contracts |
| Data infrastructure | Estonia + Korea | Interoperable data architecture, once-only data submission, AI-powered analytics |
| Workforce investment | UK + Singapore | Professional procurement certification, career pathways, competitive compensation |
| Continuous improvement | Chile + EU | Mandatory post-award performance data, feedback loops, annual benchmarking |

> **The bottom line:** The United States does not need to invent solutions to its procurement problems. Other countries have already built them. What the US needs is the political will to adopt, adapt, and implement what the evidence shows works.

## 2. The Problem: US Government Contracting Is Broken

### The Scale of the Problem

The federal government obligated approximately $759 billion in contracts in fiscal year 2023 (USASpending.gov 2024). That figure exceeds the entire gross domestic product of Switzerland. It is larger than the defense budgets of every other NATO member combined. It funds everything from the jets that defend American airspace to the software that processes Social Security checks, from the construction of federal courthouses to the vaccines administered to service members.

When this system works well, the American public gets the infrastructure, technology, and services it needs at a fair price. When it fails -- and it fails often -- taxpayers absorb the cost twice: once for the work that did not deliver, and again for the remediation, re-competition, or lawsuit that follows.

The federal procurement system is not merely large. It is fragmented. The Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) runs to more than 2,000 pages. Agency-specific supplements add thousands more. Contractors must navigate SAM.gov for registration, FedBizOpps (now merged into SAM.gov) for opportunities, FPDS for historical data, USASpending.gov for spending transparency, the Electronic Subcontracting Reporting System (eSRS) for compliance, and CPARS for past performance -- each a separate system, with separate logins, separate data formats, and limited interoperability. South Korea runs all equivalent functions on a single platform.

### Cost Overruns and Schedule Delays

The Government Accountability Office (GAO) has documented persistent cost growth in major federal acquisitions for decades. The Department of Defense's major acquisition programs alone experienced a cumulative cost overrun of $628 billion between 2008 and 2023 (GAO 2024). Information technology projects are no better: the GAO's High-Risk List has included federal IT acquisitions since 2015, citing a pattern of projects delivered late, over budget, or failing to meet requirements.

Cost overruns are not random. They are structurally encouraged by a system that too often selects contractors on the basis of the lowest bid rather than the best overall value. When the government uses Lowest Price Technically Acceptable (LPTA) evaluation, contractors have a rational incentive to bid low and recover margin through change orders after award. The result is a "buy-in" dynamic well documented in both the academic literature and government audits (Christensen 1993; Conti and Dougherty 2021). Davidson (2026) found that for contracts between $250,000 and approximately $5 million, best-value tradeoff evaluation was associated with meaningfully lower cost growth than LPTA, suggesting that how the government chooses its contractors has real consequences for the taxpayer.

### The LPTA Problem

Congress has repeatedly expressed concern about the overuse of LPTA. Section 813 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2017 restricted LPTA use for certain defense acquisitions, recognizing that selecting solely on price was yielding poor outcomes for complex services and knowledge work. Yet LPTA remains widely used across civilian agencies, where no equivalent restriction applies.

The logic of LPTA is seductive in its simplicity: set minimum requirements, take the cheapest offer that meets them, and move on. In practice, this logic fails for any procurement where quality varies among offerors, where the government's requirements are difficult to specify precisely in advance, or where long-term cost of ownership matters more than initial price. That describes most service contracts, most IT contracts, and a substantial share of construction and research contracts -- in other words, the majority of what the federal government buys.

> **Consider the analogy:** LPTA is the procurement equivalent of always buying the cheapest car on the lot that starts when you turn the key. You would never do this with your own money. Yet the federal government does it routinely with the public's money.

### Protest-Driven Paralysis

The Government Accountability Office sustained 15% of the bid protests it adjudicated in fiscal year 2023 -- a number that understates the system's dysfunction. The mere threat of protest shapes acquisition strategy in ways that are difficult to quantify but universally acknowledged by practitioners. Contracting officers report selecting LPTA specifically because it is harder to protest than best-value tradeoff, since LPTA decisions are binary (meet the threshold or not) rather than judgment-based (Yukins 2010). The result is a perverse incentive: the system designed to ensure fairness drives agencies toward the evaluation method least likely to deliver value.

The US Court of Federal Claims and GAO together adjudicated over 2,500 protests in fiscal year 2023. By contrast, Canada's Procurement Ombudsman resolves disputes through investigation and mediation, and Chile's Observatory identifies problems systemically rather than case by case. The American protest system is adversarial, slow, and backward-looking. The systems examined in this paper are preventive, fast, and forward-looking.

### Workforce Shortages

The federal acquisition workforce has been under sustained stress for over two decades. The "procurement holiday" of the 1990s, when workforce reductions coincided with increased outsourcing, hollowed out institutional expertise that has never been fully restored (Kelman 2002). The average age of the federal contracting officer continues to rise. Mid-career attrition is high, driven by the private sector's willingness to pay procurement professionals 30-50% more than the government (NCMA 2023). The result is that the people making $700 billion in annual spending decisions are often overworked, undertrained, and operating under risk-averse incentives that prioritize compliance over outcomes.

Singapore trains its procurement officers systematically and compensates them competitively. The United Kingdom established Crown Commercial Service as a center of procurement excellence, investing in skills and professional development. The United States has no equivalent national investment in its acquisition workforce.

### Meanwhile, Other Countries Have Solved These Problems

The problems described above are real, but they are not inevitable. They are the product of specific policy choices, institutional designs, and historical path dependencies. Other countries -- many of them smaller, with fewer resources -- have built procurement systems that are faster, cheaper, more transparent, less corrupt, and better at delivering public value than the American system.

This paper examines twelve of those systems. Not because they are perfect, but because they are better. And because the specific ways in which they are better offer concrete, evidence-based lessons for American reform.

## 3. The World's Top Procurement Systems: What Works

### South Korea: KONEPS -- The Gold Standard in Digital Procurement

South Korea's Korea ON-line E-Procurement System (KONEPS) is widely regarded as the most advanced government e-procurement platform in the world. Launched in 2002 by the Public Procurement Service (PPS), KONEPS consolidated what had been a fragmented, paper-based system into a single digital platform handling the full procurement lifecycle -- from demand planning and solicitation through bidding, evaluation, contract award, payment, and post-award management.

**Scale and performance.** KONEPS processes approximately $160 billion in annual procurement volume, serving more than 64,000 public organizations and 430,000 registered suppliers (PPS 2023). All procurement transactions are conducted electronically. The system is 100% paperless.

**Measurable savings.** Lim (2009) estimated that KONEPS generates approximately $8 billion per year in direct savings through reduced processing costs, lower bid prices driven by increased competition, and elimination of paper-based inefficiencies. Bidding time, which previously averaged 30 hours under the manual system, was reduced to under 2 hours. The cost of participating in a bid dropped by approximately 80%.

**Artificial intelligence integration.** Since 2018, KONEPS has integrated artificial intelligence and big data analytics to detect bid rigging, predict market prices, identify anomalous bidding patterns, and support contracting officers in evaluation decisions. The AI system analyzes historical pricing data across millions of transactions to flag bids that deviate significantly from expected ranges -- a capability the US federal system lacks entirely.

**Anti-corruption by design.** KONEPS was built explicitly to reduce corruption in government contracting. By automating processes that previously required face-to-face interaction between officials and vendors, the system eliminated many of the opportunities for bribery and favoritism that had plagued Korean procurement. Transparency International has cited KONEPS as a model for anti-corruption through digitization (TI 2014).

**International export.** The Korean model has been adopted or adapted by Vietnam, Mongolia, Costa Rica, and Tunisia, among others. The PPS actively provides technical assistance to developing countries seeking to build e-procurement systems, and KONEPS has won the United Nations Public Service Award.

> **What the US can learn from Korea:** A single, unified, end-to-end digital procurement platform -- replacing the current patchwork of SAM.gov, FPDS, USASpending, eSRS, and CPARS -- could generate billions in annual savings while simultaneously improving transparency and reducing corruption risk.

### Chile: ChileCompra -- Transparency, SME Inclusion, and Real-Time Monitoring

Chile's public procurement system, managed through the platform ChileCompra (formally the Direccion ChileCompra), stands as Latin America's most advanced e-procurement framework and one of the most transparent procurement systems in the world. Established in 2003, ChileCompra has evolved from a basic electronic marketplace into a comprehensive ecosystem integrating transparency, analytics, anti-corruption monitoring, and inclusive economic policy.

**Scale and transparency.** ChileCompra processes approximately $16 billion in annual procurement volume across more than 850 public entities (ChileCompra 2023). All transactions above a low threshold are published in real time. Chile was among the first countries to adopt the Open Contracting Data Standard (OCDS), making its procurement data available in a standardized, machine-readable format that enables analysis by civil society, journalists, and researchers.

**The Observatory.** Chile established a dedicated procurement Observatory within ChileCompra that monitors transactions in real time for anomalies, conflicts of interest, and potential corruption. The Observatory uses data analytics to flag suspicious patterns -- such as repeated awards to the same vendor, unusually high prices, or patterns consistent with bid rigging -- and refers cases for investigation. Following a major reform in 2023, the Observatory's expanded mandate and new beneficial ownership transparency requirements produced a 67% reduction in detected conflicts of interest within the first year of implementation (ChileCompra 2024).

**Beneficial ownership transparency.** Chile's 2023 procurement reform included a requirement for beneficial ownership disclosure by companies bidding on government contracts. Within three months of implementation, 59% of companies in the procurement registry had disclosed their beneficial ownership structures -- a compliance rate that significantly exceeded initial expectations and provided the government with unprecedented visibility into who actually owns and controls its contractors (OECD 2024).

**SME inclusion.** Small and medium enterprises (SMEs) capture approximately 42% of all ChileCompra transactions -- roughly five times the national average for SME market share (ChileCompra 2023). This is not an accident. ChileCompra has deliberately designed its platform and processes to reduce barriers to entry for smaller firms, including simplified registration, lower bonding requirements for small procurements, and set-aside programs.

**International recognition.** The World Bank, OECD, and Inter-American Development Bank have all cited ChileCompra as a regional and global model. Chile's approach has been particularly influential in demonstrating that transparency, analytics, and anti-corruption monitoring can be integrated into a single procurement platform rather than bolted on as afterthoughts.

> **What the US can learn from Chile:** Real-time monitoring through an independent Observatory, combined with beneficial ownership transparency, can dramatically reduce conflicts of interest and corruption -- without slowing down the procurement process.

### United Kingdom: Crown Commercial Service and the Procurement Act 2023

The United Kingdom has undergone a fundamental transformation of its procurement framework, culminating in the Procurement Act 2023 -- the most significant reform of UK public procurement law in a generation. The legislation, which replaced the EU-derived procurement rules that governed UK purchasing for decades, explicitly moves the system from lowest-price logic toward a broader conception of public value.

**Crown Commercial Service (CCS).** CCS serves as the UK government's centralized procurement authority, managing framework agreements and commercial arrangements on behalf of government departments and the wider public sector. In 2023-24, CCS handled approximately GBP 30.3 billion in procurement spend and delivered GBP 4.9 billion in commercial benefits -- a savings rate of approximately 10.49% (CCS Annual Report 2024). This is not a theoretical estimate; it represents audited, verified savings against benchmark prices.

**The Social Value Act.** The Public Services (Social Value) Act 2012, as strengthened by subsequent policy, requires that all central government procurement above GBP 10 million apply a minimum 10% weighting to social value in evaluation. Social value encompasses environmental sustainability, community benefit, employment of disadvantaged groups, and support for local supply chains. The weighting is not optional, aspirational, or subject to contracting officer discretion -- it is mandatory and scored.

This represents a philosophical commitment that procurement is not merely a commercial transaction but a tool of public policy. The US federal system has no equivalent. While the FAR contains various socioeconomic provisions (small business set-asides, AbilityOne, HUBZone), these are implemented as compliance checkboxes rather than as scored evaluation criteria that affect who wins contracts.

**The Procurement Act 2023.** The Act replaced the EU concept of "Most Economically Advantageous Tender" (MEAT) with "Most Advantageous Tender" (MAT) -- a deliberate linguistic and substantive shift. By removing the word "economically," Parliament signaled that price and economic efficiency are important but not sufficient criteria for procurement decisions. MAT allows contracting authorities to consider a broader range of public benefits, including social value, innovation, resilience, and long-term sustainability.

The Act also introduced a new transparency framework requiring contracting authorities to publish notices at key stages of the procurement lifecycle, established a central digital platform for all procurement data, and created a new regime for managing underperforming contracts.

**SME participation.** Approximately 75% of suppliers on CCS frameworks are classified as SMEs (CCS 2024). The UK has achieved this not primarily through set-asides (which can create dependency and market distortion) but through process simplification, reduced bureaucratic burden, and active market engagement that helps smaller firms understand and access opportunities.

> **What the US can learn from the UK:** Mandatory, scored social value weighting in procurement evaluation -- not just socioeconomic compliance checkboxes -- can systematically direct public spending toward broader public benefit. The shift from "Most Economically Advantageous" to "Most Advantageous" is a model for redefining value in American procurement.

### Estonia: Digital Government and the X-Road Infrastructure

Estonia is frequently cited as the world's most advanced digital government, and its procurement system is built on the same infrastructure that powers every other government service: the X-Road data exchange platform.

**X-Road and interoperability.** X-Road is a secure, decentralized data exchange layer that connects 929 government institutions, enabling them to share data seamlessly without centralized databases. In 2023, X-Road processed 2.7 billion data queries (e-Estonia 2024). The "once-only" principle -- the idea that a citizen or business should never have to provide the same piece of information to the government twice -- is legally mandated and technically enforced through X-Road.

For procurement, this means that a company registering to bid on a government contract does not need to separately provide its tax compliance status, registration documents, financial statements, or insurance certificates. The procurement system pulls this information automatically from the relevant government databases. The result is a radical reduction in administrative burden for both bidders and contracting authorities.

**Digital savings.** The Estonian government estimates that its digital infrastructure saves approximately 2% of GDP annually -- a figure that encompasses all digital government services, not just procurement (e-Estonia 2024). For a small country, this is transformative. For the United States, 2% of GDP would represent savings of approximately $560 billion per year across all government operations.

**Innovation procurement.** Estonia ranks second in the European Union for innovation procurement -- the use of public purchasing to drive the development and adoption of innovative solutions (European Commission 2023). The country's small size and digital-first culture make it a natural testing ground for experimental procurement approaches, including challenge-based procurement and agile contracting.

**International export.** The X-Road platform has been deployed or adapted by more than 20 countries, including Finland (which shares a federated X-Road instance with Estonia), Japan, and several African nations. The Estonian government actively promotes its digital governance model through the e-Governance Academy, which provides training and technical assistance to governments worldwide.

> **What the US can learn from Estonia:** Interoperable data infrastructure -- a shared backbone connecting procurement, tax, financial, and regulatory systems -- can eliminate redundant data entry, accelerate processing, and enable the kind of AI-powered analytics that modern procurement requires. The "once-only" principle should be a design requirement for any US procurement platform reform.

### Singapore: GeBIZ and the Discipline of Value-for-Money

Singapore's Government Electronic Business (GeBIZ) portal has been the country's single procurement platform since 2000, making it one of the world's longest-running unified e-procurement systems. But Singapore's procurement excellence extends well beyond its technology platform. It is rooted in a deeply institutional commitment to value-for-money as a principle of governance.

**Explicit value-for-money doctrine.** Singapore's procurement guidelines state clearly: "Value for money does not mean lowest quote" (Ministry of Finance 2023). This is not buried in a footnote. It is a headline principle, understood and applied across the entire public service. Contracting officers are trained, expected, and incentivized to consider quality, reliability, total cost of ownership, and strategic fit -- not just initial price.

**Minimal corruption.** Transparency International's 2023 Corruption Perceptions Index ranks Singapore as one of the least corrupt countries in the world. In the procurement context, only 5% of companies report encountering procurement fraud in Singapore, compared to 29% globally (PwC Global Economic Crime Survey 2022). This is not simply a cultural outcome; it is the product of deliberate institutional design, including strict conflict-of-interest rules, regular audits, severe penalties for corruption, and a professional civil service with competitive compensation.

**Unified platform.** GeBIZ serves more than 120 government agencies and statutory boards, providing a single point of access for all government procurement opportunities, from small purchases to major capital projects. The platform handles the full procurement lifecycle and includes tools for vendor management, contract tracking, and performance evaluation.

**Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP).** Singapore employs the Analytic Hierarchy Process, a structured mathematical framework for multi-criteria decision-making, in complex procurement evaluations. AHP requires evaluators to decompose evaluation criteria into a hierarchy, assign relative weights through pairwise comparison, and score proposals against each criterion systematically. This reduces subjectivity, improves consistency, and produces evaluation decisions that are both defensible and transparent. The US federal system uses subjective evaluation narratives that vary enormously across agencies and contracting officers; Singapore uses a standardized, quantitative method.

> **What the US can learn from Singapore:** A clear, institutionally embedded doctrine that "value for money does not mean lowest price," combined with structured evaluation methodologies like AHP, can transform procurement from a compliance exercise into a value-delivery discipline.

### Georgia: How a Country Rebuilt Procurement from Scratch in Ten Months

Georgia's procurement transformation is perhaps the most dramatic in the world. In the early 2000s, Georgia was one of the most corrupt countries in the former Soviet Union. Government procurement was effectively a system of patronage and bribery. What happened next is a case study in how quickly a determined government can change.

**Speed of implementation.** In 2010, Georgia built and launched a comprehensive e-procurement system in approximately ten months, at a total cost of less than $1 million (World Bank 2012). The system was designed to be simple, transparent, and resistant to manipulation. All government tenders above a low threshold were required to be conducted through the electronic platform.

**Corruption reduction.** The Transparency International Global Corruption Barometer recorded that in 2004, 97% of Georgian citizens perceived the traffic police, customs, and related government services as corrupt. By 2006 -- following a comprehensive government reform program that included the procurement overhaul -- that figure had dropped to 3% (TI 2006). While the procurement reform was part of a broader anti-corruption effort, the e-procurement system was a central component, eliminating the face-to-face interactions that had enabled bribery.

**International validation.** Georgia's e-procurement system won the United Nations Public Service Award in 2012. The World Bank approved the system for use in Bank-financed projects -- a significant endorsement, as the Bank has strict requirements for procurement systems used in its operations. The system has been studied and, in some cases, adapted by other developing and transitional countries.

**Simplicity as a design principle.** Georgia's system succeeded in part because it was deliberately simple. Rather than attempting to digitize the full complexity of an advanced procurement framework (as larger countries often do), Georgia built a streamlined platform focused on the fundamentals: transparent publication, competitive bidding, electronic submission, and automated evaluation for straightforward procurements. This simplicity enabled rapid deployment, high adoption, and low maintenance costs.

> **What the US can learn from Georgia:** Procurement reform does not have to be expensive or slow. A focused, well-designed system can be built quickly and produce transformative results. Sometimes the most important design choice is what to leave out.

### Australia: Value for Money, Indigenous Inclusion, and Rules-Based Best Value

Australia's Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs) provide one of the clearest statutory frameworks in the world for ensuring that government procurement delivers value beyond the lowest price. Unlike the US system, where the choice between LPTA and best-value tradeoff is left largely to contracting officer judgment, Australia has codified value-for-money as the foundational principle of all procurement -- and defined it to include much more than price.

**Value-for-money framework.** The CPRs state that "price is not the sole factor" in procurement decisions and require agencies to consider the whole-of-life cost, fitness for purpose, quality, delivery, and the supplier's ability to perform (Department of Finance 2024). This is not guidance; it is a binding rule. Australian procurement officials do not have the option of defaulting to lowest-price evaluation in the way that US contracting officers routinely do.

**Indigenous Procurement Policy (IPP).** Australia's IPP, introduced in 2015, is one of the world's most ambitious programs for using procurement to advance economic opportunity for an indigenous population. The policy sets mandatory targets for the number and value of contracts awarded to Indigenous-owned businesses and requires large contractors to develop Indigenous participation plans.

The results are substantial. Since its inception, the IPP has generated $12.9 billion in opportunities for Indigenous businesses and supported more than 83,500 contracts (National Indigenous Australians Agency 2024). In 2023-24 alone, more than 1,300 Indigenous businesses received government contracts worth a combined $1.3 billion in new contract value. Indigenous-owned businesses have grown from fewer than 50 in the government supplier base to more than 4,000 since the policy was introduced.

The IPP works not through tokenistic set-asides but through mandatory minimum requirements integrated into mainstream procurement. Large contracts above certain thresholds must include Indigenous participation plans. Agencies must meet annual targets. Compliance is monitored and reported publicly.

**Implications for the US.** The United States has a patchwork of socioeconomic programs -- small business set-asides, 8(a), HUBZone, SDVOSB, WOSB -- but no comparable mandatory, monitored program for Native American, Alaska Native, or Native Hawaiian businesses that operates at the scale and with the accountability of Australia's IPP. The comparison is instructive.

> **What the US can learn from Australia:** Codifying "price is not the sole factor" as a binding procurement rule -- not just guidance -- changes institutional behavior. Mandatory indigenous/disadvantaged business participation targets, integrated into mainstream procurement with public accountability, produce measurable results.

### New Zealand: Public Value, Proportionality, and Cultural Inclusion

New Zealand's procurement framework is built on a concept the country calls "public value" -- a deliberate expansion of the traditional value-for-money concept to include broader economic, social, cultural, and environmental outcomes. New Zealand's approach is particularly notable for its emphasis on proportionality and its integration of Maori economic development goals.

**Public value framework.** New Zealand's Government Procurement Rules require agencies to consider broader outcomes in procurement decisions. A minimum 10% weighting for broader economic outcomes -- including social, environmental, and community benefits -- is required in all procurement evaluations above a specified threshold (New Zealand Government Procurement 2023). This is similar to the UK's Social Value Act but explicitly framed around the concept of "public value" rather than "social value," signaling an even broader conception of what procurement should deliver.

**Maori business participation.** New Zealand has established targets for procurement from Maori-owned businesses, with a goal of 8% of government procurement spend directed to Maori businesses. As of 2023, the actual figure was approximately 6%, representing roughly $930 million in contracts (Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment 2024). While the target has not yet been fully met, the trajectory is upward, and the government has been transparent about both the goal and the gap.

**Proportionality principle.** New Zealand's procurement framework includes an explicit proportionality requirement: the level of process, documentation, and compliance required in a procurement must be proportionate to the value, complexity, and risk of the procurement. Small, simple procurements have lighter requirements. Large, complex procurements have heavier requirements. This seems obvious, but it stands in contrast to the US system, where the FAR imposes substantial compliance requirements even for relatively modest procurements, creating barriers to participation for smaller firms and imposing unnecessary costs on agencies.

**Supplier relationship management.** New Zealand places unusual emphasis on the government's relationship with its supplier base. The government publishes regular surveys of supplier satisfaction, conducts debriefs with unsuccessful bidders, and maintains a commitment to treating suppliers as partners rather than adversaries. This relational approach is rare in government procurement and reflects a recognition that the quality of the government-supplier relationship affects the quality of the goods and services the government receives.

> **What the US can learn from New Zealand:** The proportionality principle -- right-sizing procurement requirements to the scale and risk of each purchase -- could significantly reduce burden and increase participation in the US system. Minimum weightings for broader economic outcomes, combined with transparent targets for disadvantaged-group participation, provide a replicable model.

### Brazil: Pregao and the Power of Reverse Auctions

Brazil's Pregao (reverse auction) system, introduced in 2000 for in-person auctions and expanded to electronic auctions (Pregao Eletronico) in 2005, represents one of the most successful process innovations in government procurement worldwide. The Pregao fundamentally reorders the traditional procurement sequence, producing dramatic improvements in speed and savings.

**The Pregao innovation.** In a traditional procurement (in Brazil and most countries, including the US), the process follows a familiar sequence: publish requirements, receive proposals, evaluate qualifications and technical capability, evaluate price, award contract. This process takes months and involves substantial administrative cost for both government and bidders.

The Pregao reverses the key steps. Bidders submit price proposals first, in a real-time electronic auction format. Only after the lowest-price bidder is identified does the government evaluate that bidder's qualifications and technical capability. If the lowest bidder is not qualified, the government moves to the next-lowest bidder, and so on.

**Speed.** The average Pregao procurement takes approximately 17 days from publication to award, compared to 90 or more days for conventional Brazilian procurement methods (Santana 2023). This represents a reduction of more than 80% in procurement cycle time.

**Anti-manipulation features.** The electronic Pregao includes a random closing mechanism: the auction does not end at a predetermined time but at a random point within a window, preventing the last-second "sniping" strategies that can distort outcomes in timed auctions. This design feature has been effective in preventing bidder collusion and strategic timing.

**Savings.** Brazil's Tribunal de Contas da Uniao (federal audit court) estimated savings of approximately $1.5 million (corrected for purchasing power) in the first two years of electronic Pregao implementation for a sample of federal agencies (TCU 2007). While this figure is modest by US standards, it represented a significant improvement for the agencies studied, and the savings have scaled substantially as Pregao became the default procurement method for common goods and services across the entire Brazilian federal government.

**Applicability to the US.** The US federal government uses reverse auctions in limited circumstances, primarily through GSA and the Department of Defense. However, reverse auctions are not a standard procurement tool and are not systematically employed for the commodity purchases where they are most effective. Brazil's experience suggests that making reverse auctions the default for standard goods and services -- while preserving best-value evaluation for complex procurements -- could yield meaningful savings and speed improvements.

> **What the US can learn from Brazil:** Reverse auctions, with qualification after bidding rather than before, can dramatically reduce procurement timelines for commodity purchases. Random auction closing times prevent manipulation. The key insight is to match the procurement method to the procurement type: reverse auctions for commodities, best-value evaluation for complex services.

### Canada: The Procurement Ombudsman and the Path to Best Value

Canada's procurement system is of particular interest to the United States because the two countries share a common-law legal tradition, similar governance structures, and a comparable (if smaller-scale) procurement challenge. Canada's most distinctive contribution to global procurement practice is its independent Procurement Ombudsman, an institution that has no equivalent in the US system.

**The Office of the Procurement Ombudsman (OPO).** Established in 2008, the OPO is an independent office with the authority to review procurement practices across the Canadian federal government, investigate complaints from suppliers, and make recommendations for systemic improvement. Unlike the US GAO's protest function -- which is reactive, adversarial, and case-specific -- the OPO operates proactively, conducting reviews of agency procurement practices, publishing annual reports on systemic issues, and recommending reforms.

The Ombudsman can investigate complaints even when they do not rise to the level of a formal protest. This is significant because many procurement problems -- chronic delays, unnecessarily restrictive requirements, poor communication with bidders, inconsistent evaluation practices -- affect suppliers and taxpayers but never generate formal protests. The GAO protest system captures only the tip of the iceberg; the Ombudsman model addresses the whole mass below the waterline.

**Moving toward broader best value.** Canada has been engaged in an ongoing effort to modernize its procurement framework, including proposals to adopt a broader definition of best value that incorporates social, economic, and environmental outcomes alongside price and technical merit. The Canadian government has also explored the establishment of a Federal Chief Procurement Officer -- a senior official with government-wide authority over procurement policy and practice -- to provide strategic leadership that the current decentralized system lacks.

**AI and modernization.** Canada's procurement modernization agenda includes proposals for integrating artificial intelligence into procurement decision-making, establishing unified procurement rules across the federal government (replacing the current patchwork of department-specific practices), and investing in procurement workforce development. While these reforms are still in progress, they reflect a recognition that the status quo is inadequate and a willingness to learn from international best practices.

**Dispute resolution.** The Canadian International Trade Tribunal (CITT) handles procurement disputes, but the OPO provides an alternative, less adversarial pathway for resolving complaints. This dual system -- a formal tribunal for serious cases and an ombudsman for systemic issues and supplier concerns -- offers a more nuanced approach to oversight than the US system, which relies almost entirely on the adversarial protest mechanism.

> **What the US can learn from Canada:** An independent Procurement Ombudsman with systemic review authority could address the root causes of procurement dysfunction, rather than the symptoms that emerge as individual protests. The Ombudsman model complements rather than replaces the existing protest system.

### Denmark and the Nordics: Innovation Procurement as a Strategic Discipline

Denmark, along with its Nordic neighbors, has emerged as a global leader in using public procurement as a tool for driving innovation. The Danish approach treats procurement not merely as a mechanism for buying goods and services but as an instrument for solving public problems and creating new markets.

**Pre-commercial procurement (PCP).** Denmark has been a pioneer in pre-commercial procurement -- a structured approach in which the government contracts with multiple firms to develop solutions to a defined problem, progressively narrowing the field through competitive phases before procuring the resulting innovation. A landmark example is the Danish hospital system's use of PCP to develop commercial robotic solutions for hospital logistics. The program identified a clinical need (automated delivery of supplies within hospitals), issued a PCP challenge, funded competing R&D efforts, and ultimately procured a commercially viable robotic system that has been deployed across Danish hospitals and subsequently sold internationally (Danish Ministry of Finance 2022).

This is not a small pilot program. It is a systematic methodology for using public procurement budgets to drive innovation that serves the public while creating commercial opportunities for firms.

**GovTech program.** Denmark's GovTech program is specifically designed to open public procurement to startups and innovative small firms that are typically excluded by the administrative burden and risk aversion of traditional government procurement. The program provides simplified procurement pathways, smaller contract sizes appropriate for startups, and structured support for scaling successful innovations across government.

**Nordic collaboration.** The Nordic countries (Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland, Iceland) collaborate on procurement innovation through shared research, joint pilot programs, and coordinated policy development. This collaboration allows each country to learn from the others' experiments and adopt proven approaches more quickly than if each worked in isolation.

**Scale of Nordic procurement.** Denmark alone spends approximately EUR 35 billion annually on public procurement, representing roughly 15% of GDP (European Commission 2023). The Nordic countries collectively represent a significant procurement market, and their willingness to use that market power to drive innovation has produced tangible results in healthcare, environmental technology, digital services, and infrastructure.

> **What the US can learn from Denmark and the Nordics:** Innovation procurement -- pre-commercial procurement, challenge-based acquisition, and startup-friendly pathways -- is a learnable discipline that can be systematically deployed. The US has experimented with similar approaches (SBIR/STTR, Other Transaction Authorities, challenge.gov) but has not integrated them into mainstream procurement practice the way Denmark has.

### The European Union: Setting the Standard for Sustainable and Innovative Procurement

The European Union's procurement directives govern approximately EUR 2 trillion in annual public spending across 27 member states -- roughly 14% of EU GDP (European Commission 2023). The EU framework is the world's largest harmonized procurement regime and has been enormously influential in shaping procurement practice globally. While individual EU member states implement the directives differently, the framework itself establishes principles and tools that offer important lessons for the United States.

**Most Economically Advantageous Tender (MEAT).** The EU procurement directives establish MEAT as the standard evaluation method, requiring contracting authorities to consider criteria beyond price, including quality, technical merit, environmental characteristics, innovation, after-sales service, delivery conditions, and life-cycle costs. While MEAT allows for a price-only evaluation in limited circumstances, the default expectation is multi-criteria assessment. This stands in contrast to the US system, where LPTA remains a readily available and widely used option.

**Life-cycle costing.** The EU directives explicitly authorize and encourage life-cycle costing -- evaluating the total cost of a product or service over its entire useful life, including acquisition, operating, maintenance, and disposal costs, as well as external environmental costs. This is a significant departure from the initial-price focus that dominates much of US procurement. The European Commission has published detailed guidance and tools for conducting life-cycle cost analyses, making it practical for contracting authorities to implement.

**Innovation partnerships.** The EU's innovation partnership procedure allows contracting authorities to establish long-term partnerships with firms for the development and subsequent purchase of innovative products or services. The procedure combines R&D and procurement in a single process, allowing the contracting authority to fund development and then purchase the resulting innovation without a separate procurement. This addresses a fundamental gap in traditional procurement: the inability to buy things that do not yet exist.

**Sustainable procurement.** Lithuania's experience illustrates what is possible when a member state commits to sustainable procurement. Lithuanian sustainable procurement increased from approximately 5% of tenders in 2018 to over 90% by 2021 -- a transformation achieved through mandatory requirements, training, and monitoring (European Commission 2022). This trajectory demonstrates that sustainable procurement is not a niche practice for wealthy or progressive countries; it is achievable at scale when there is political commitment and institutional support.

**Green Public Procurement (GPP) criteria.** The European Commission has developed detailed GPP criteria for 21 product and service groups, providing contracting authorities with ready-to-use technical specifications and award criteria that incorporate environmental considerations. These criteria are voluntary at the EU level but have been adopted as mandatory by several member states. The US has no equivalent systematic set of environmental procurement criteria.

> **What the US can learn from the EU:** Life-cycle costing, innovation partnerships, and systematic environmental criteria are mature tools that have been tested at enormous scale across 27 countries. The EU's experience demonstrates that these approaches are practical, not merely aspirational. Lithuania's transformation from 5% to 90%+ sustainable procurement in three years shows that rapid change is possible with political will.

## 4. Cross-Cutting Success Patterns

The twelve systems profiled in this paper differ enormously in scale, political context, legal tradition, and economic development level. South Korea's procurement system serves an industrialized economy of 52 million people. Georgia's was built for a transitional economy of 3.7 million. The EU framework governs 27 sovereign nations with different languages, legal systems, and administrative cultures. Yet across all of these systems, a set of common success patterns emerges. These patterns are not coincidental. They represent the institutional prerequisites for effective procurement.

### Pattern 1: A Single Unified Digital Platform

The most consistent feature of successful procurement systems is a single, unified digital platform that handles the full procurement lifecycle. South Korea (KONEPS), Singapore (GeBIZ), Chile (ChileCompra), Georgia, Brazil (ComprasNet/Pregao), and Estonia all operate through a single portal. Suppliers register once, find opportunities in one place, submit bids through one system, and track contracts through one interface.

The United States, by contrast, requires suppliers and agencies to navigate SAM.gov, FPDS, USASpending.gov, eSRS, CPARS, beta.SAM.gov, and various agency-specific portals. These systems were built at different times, by different agencies, with different technologies, and they are not fully interoperable. The result is friction, redundancy, error, and cost -- for both government and industry.

The evidence from every country that has unified its platform is consistent: a single digital portal reduces processing time, lowers participation costs for suppliers (especially SMEs), increases competition, improves data quality, and enables the analytics and AI capabilities that are prerequisites for continuous improvement.

### Pattern 2: Explicit "Not Lowest Price" Mandates

Every high-performing procurement system examined in this paper has, in some form, codified the principle that price alone is not a sufficient basis for procurement decisions. Australia's CPRs state that "price is not the sole factor." Singapore's guidelines declare that "value for money does not mean lowest quote." The UK's Procurement Act 2023 replaced "Most Economically Advantageous Tender" with "Most Advantageous Tender." New Zealand requires a minimum 10% weighting for broader outcomes. The EU's MEAT standard requires multi-criteria evaluation as the default.

The United States permits best-value evaluation but does not require it. LPTA remains a readily available, widely used, and often preferred option -- particularly because it is easier to defend against protest, faster to execute, and requires less evaluation skill. The result is that the path of least resistance for the US contracting officer leads to the evaluation method that is least likely to deliver value.

Codifying "not lowest price" as a default or mandate -- while preserving lowest-price evaluation for truly commodity purchases where quality does not vary -- would change the institutional incentives that drive evaluation method selection in the United States.

### Pattern 3: Transparency by Design and Anti-Corruption Architecture

Georgia, Chile, South Korea, and Estonia have all built their procurement systems with anti-corruption as a design requirement, not an afterthought. The common approach is to eliminate opportunities for corruption by automating processes that previously required human discretion or face-to-face interaction. Electronic bidding, automated evaluation for simple procurements, real-time publication of all transactions, and analytics-based monitoring for anomalies all contribute to systems where corruption is not merely punished after the fact but structurally prevented.

The US system relies primarily on after-the-fact enforcement -- inspector general investigations, Department of Justice prosecutions, suspension and debarment proceedings -- rather than on systemic prevention. While the US has more robust enforcement institutions than most countries, enforcement alone cannot compensate for a system that creates opportunities for corruption at every stage of the procurement lifecycle.

### Pattern 4: Social Value and Inclusion Requirements

The United Kingdom, New Zealand, Australia, and to a lesser extent Canada have moved beyond treating socioeconomic goals as compliance requirements and instead integrated them as scored evaluation criteria in procurement decisions. The UK's mandatory 10% social value weighting, New Zealand's 10% broader outcomes weighting, and Australia's Indigenous Procurement Policy all represent a recognition that procurement is not merely a commercial activity but a tool of public policy.

The US system has numerous socioeconomic programs, but they operate primarily through set-asides and goals rather than through integration into mainstream evaluation criteria. The result is that socioeconomic objectives are pursued through separate channels rather than being woven into the fabric of how the government makes every procurement decision. The international evidence suggests that integration is more effective than separation.

### Pattern 5: Independent Oversight Mechanisms

Canada's Procurement Ombudsman, Chile's Observatory, the UK's CCS, and Singapore's centralized oversight all represent different models of independent procurement oversight that go beyond the adversarial protest-based model used in the United States.

The US system relies primarily on GAO bid protests and Court of Federal Claims litigation -- reactive, adversarial, case-specific mechanisms that address individual grievances but do not systematically identify and correct institutional procurement problems. Canada's Ombudsman model, in particular, offers a complementary approach: proactive, systemic, and oriented toward continuous improvement rather than dispute resolution.

### Pattern 6: Speed and Simplification

Brazil's Pregao (17 days versus 90+), South Korea's KONEPS (under 2 hours of bidding time versus 30 hours), and Georgia's rapid platform deployment (10 months, under $1 million) all demonstrate that procurement can be dramatically faster without sacrificing quality or integrity.

The US procurement system is slow. Average cycle times for competitive procurements frequently exceed 200 days. Much of this time is consumed by administrative process rather than substantive evaluation. Requirements development takes months. Legal reviews take weeks. Clearance chains add layers of delay. The international evidence suggests that much of this time is eliminable through process redesign, digital platforms, and proportional requirements.

### Pattern 7: Innovation Procurement Pathways

Denmark's pre-commercial procurement, the EU's innovation partnerships, and Estonia's innovation-friendly procurement environment all represent structured pathways for using procurement to drive innovation. These are not ad hoc experiments; they are institutionalized procurement methods with defined rules, procedures, and support infrastructure.

The US has innovation-adjacent procurement mechanisms -- SBIR/STTR grants, Other Transaction Authorities (OTAs), challenge.gov -- but these exist outside the mainstream procurement system. A contracting officer who wants to procure an innovative solution using a standard FAR-based procurement faces significant barriers. Integrating innovation procurement pathways into the standard toolkit, as Denmark and the EU have done, would make innovation procurement accessible to the broader acquisition workforce.

### Pattern 8: Data-Driven Monitoring and Continuous Improvement

Chile's Observatory, Korea's AI-powered analytics, and the EU's data publication requirements all reflect a commitment to using procurement data not just for compliance reporting but for continuous improvement. These systems collect data on every transaction, analyze patterns, identify problems, and feed insights back into policy and practice.

The US collects enormous amounts of procurement data through FPDS and USASpending.gov, but this data is used primarily for after-the-fact transparency and reporting rather than for real-time monitoring, predictive analytics, or continuous improvement. The data infrastructure exists; the analytical superstructure does not.

### Pattern 9: Strong Acquisition Workforce Investment

Singapore's professional civil service, the UK's investment in CCS as a center of procurement excellence, and Australia's commitment to procurement capability development all reflect a recognition that procurement outcomes depend ultimately on the skills and judgment of the people making procurement decisions. Technology and rules matter, but they are not sufficient without a competent, well-trained, adequately compensated workforce to implement them.

The US federal acquisition workforce has been understaffed, undertrained, and undercompensated relative to its responsibilities for at least two decades. The Acquisition Workforce Development Fund, established in 2009, was a step in the right direction, but investment has not kept pace with the growing complexity and volume of federal procurement. No technology platform, however sophisticated, can compensate for contracting officers who lack the training, time, and institutional support to make good decisions.

### Pattern 10: International Knowledge Sharing and Export

South Korea, Estonia, Georgia, Chile, and Denmark all actively share their procurement innovations with other countries. Korea has exported KONEPS to four countries. Estonia's X-Road operates in 20+ countries. Georgia's system has been studied by developing countries worldwide. Chile's OCDS publication has been adopted by dozens of countries. Denmark's innovation procurement methodology has been replicated across the Nordics and the EU.

The United States, despite being the world's largest government buyer, has been remarkably insular in its approach to procurement reform. American procurement professionals rarely study or cite international examples. Congressional hearings on procurement reform rarely include testimony about what other countries are doing. The FAR revision process operates almost entirely within a domestic frame of reference. This insularity is a missed opportunity. The solutions to many of America's procurement problems already exist. They are operating, at scale, in other countries. The US does not need to invent them from scratch.

## 5. The Model: A Best-of-the-World Procurement Framework for the US

Based on the evidence presented in this paper, the following framework combines the most effective elements from the world's best procurement systems into a coherent model for US federal procurement reform. This is not a theoretical exercise. Every element of this model has been implemented, tested, and validated at scale in at least one of the countries profiled.

### Pillar 1: Unified Digital Infrastructure

**Source models:** South Korea (KONEPS) + Estonia (X-Road)

**Current US system:** SAM.gov, FPDS, USASpending.gov, eSRS, CPARS, and dozens of agency-specific systems, loosely connected and partially interoperable.

**Proposed model:** A single, end-to-end federal procurement platform that handles the full lifecycle from market research through contract closeout. The platform would incorporate:

- **Single registration.** Vendors register once and their information is available across all agencies, all procurement types, and all reporting systems. Registration data (tax status, financial health, insurance, past performance) is pulled automatically from authoritative government databases, following Estonia's "once-only" principle.
- **Single opportunity portal.** All federal procurement opportunities, regardless of agency, dollar value, or procurement type, are published in one place with standardized formatting, searchable by commodity, geography, set-aside type, and evaluation method.
- **Integrated evaluation tools.** The platform provides standardized tools for multi-criteria evaluation, including Singapore's Analytic Hierarchy Process methodology, enabling consistent, defensible evaluation decisions across agencies.
- **Real-time analytics.** Korea-style AI and big data analytics monitor all transactions for anomalies, bid rigging patterns, price deviations, and performance trends, providing contracting officers and oversight bodies with real-time intelligence.
- **Post-award management.** Contract performance, modifications, payments, and closeout are managed within the same platform, creating a complete lifecycle data trail for every procurement.

**Estimated impact:** Based on Korea's experience, a unified platform could generate savings of 1-3% of annual procurement volume, or $7-21 billion per year at current US procurement spending levels.

### Pillar 2: Value-Based Evaluation as the Default

**Source models:** Singapore + Australia + United Kingdom

**Current US system:** Contracting officers choose between LPTA and best-value tradeoff on a case-by-case basis, with institutional incentives favoring LPTA for simplicity and protest avoidance.

**Proposed model:**

- **Default to multi-criteria evaluation.** Best-value tradeoff (or the UK's "Most Advantageous Tender" concept) becomes the default evaluation method for all procurements above the simplified acquisition threshold. LPTA is restricted to genuinely commodity purchases where quality does not vary among offerors -- hardware to a defined specification, common commercial items with established performance records, and similar categories.
- **Mandatory price floor and ceiling.** Price must be weighted at a minimum of 30% (to ensure fiscal discipline) but no more than 60% (to ensure non-price factors receive meaningful consideration). The specific weighting within this range is determined by the contracting officer based on the nature of the procurement. Davidson's (2026) finding that evaluation method matters most for smaller contracts suggests that this requirement is especially important for procurements in the $250,000-$5 million range.
- **Structured evaluation methodology.** Agencies adopt a standardized multi-criteria evaluation framework (based on Singapore's AHP approach) that requires evaluators to define criteria, assign weights, and score proposals systematically -- replacing the narrative-based evaluation approach that currently produces inconsistent results across agencies.
- **Life-cycle costing.** Following the EU model, contracting authorities are required to consider total cost of ownership, not just acquisition price, for all procurements where operating, maintenance, or disposal costs are significant.

### Pillar 3: Transparency, Monitoring, and Anti-Corruption

**Source models:** Chile (Observatory) + South Korea (KONEPS analytics) + Georgia (design simplicity)

**Current US system:** After-the-fact transparency through FPDS and USASpending.gov; enforcement-based anti-corruption through IGs and DOJ; limited real-time monitoring.

**Proposed model:**

- **Independent Procurement Observatory.** A new, independent body (modeled on Chile's Observatory) monitors all federal procurement transactions in real time, using analytics to identify patterns indicative of waste, fraud, abuse, conflicts of interest, or systemic dysfunction. The Observatory publishes regular public reports and refers cases for investigation.
- **Beneficial ownership registry.** All companies receiving federal contracts above a defined threshold must disclose their beneficial ownership structures, following Chile's 2023 model. This information is verified against existing government databases and made available (with appropriate privacy protections) to contracting officers and oversight bodies.
- **Open Contracting Data Standard.** All federal procurement data is published in the Open Contracting Data Standard (OCDS), the international standard for procurement data publication adopted by Chile, the UK, and dozens of other countries. This enables analysis by civil society, journalists, researchers, and the public.
- **Anti-corruption by design.** Following Georgia and Korea's approach, the unified platform minimizes opportunities for corruption by automating processes that currently require discretionary human interaction -- particularly in areas like vendor qualification, bid evaluation for simple procurements, and payment processing.

### Pillar 4: Social Value Integration

**Source models:** United Kingdom (Social Value Act) + New Zealand (public value framework) + Australia (Indigenous Procurement Policy)

**Current US system:** Socioeconomic goals pursued through set-asides, goals, and compliance requirements that are separate from mainstream evaluation criteria.

**Proposed model:**

- **Mandatory social value weighting.** All procurements above the simplified acquisition threshold include a minimum 10% weighting for social value criteria, following the UK model. Social value criteria are defined to include environmental sustainability, community benefit, workforce development, and economic opportunity for disadvantaged groups.
- **Indigenous and disadvantaged business targets.** Following Australia's IPP model, mandatory targets are established for procurement from Native American, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, and other historically disadvantaged businesses. These targets are monitored, reported publicly, and enforced through agency accountability mechanisms.
- **Integration, not separation.** Social value is evaluated as part of the mainstream procurement evaluation, not through separate set-aside channels. This ensures that every procurement decision -- not just those designated for socioeconomic programs -- considers broader public value.

### Pillar 5: Independent Oversight

**Source models:** Canada (Procurement Ombudsman) + Chile (Observatory)

**Current US system:** GAO bid protests, Court of Federal Claims litigation, agency-level inspectors general.

**Proposed model:**

- **Federal Procurement Ombudsman.** A new, independent Procurement Ombudsman is established with authority to investigate systemic procurement issues, receive and resolve supplier complaints, conduct proactive reviews of agency procurement practices, and make public recommendations. The Ombudsman operates alongside -- not as a replacement for -- the existing GAO protest function. The Ombudsman focuses on systemic issues and continuous improvement; GAO continues to handle individual bid protests.
- **Streamlined protest process.** The existing protest process is reformed to reduce timelines, increase the use of alternative dispute resolution, and discourage strategic (non-meritorious) protests. Protest data is analyzed systematically to identify agencies, procurement types, and practices that generate disproportionate protest activity, enabling targeted intervention.

### Pillar 6: Speed and Simplification

**Source models:** Brazil (Pregao) + Georgia (rapid deployment) + New Zealand (proportionality)

**Current US system:** Average competitive procurement cycle times exceeding 200 days; uniform compliance requirements regardless of procurement size or complexity.

**Proposed model:**

- **Reverse auctions as default for commodities.** Following Brazil's Pregao model, reverse auctions with post-bid qualification become the default procurement method for standard goods and commodity services. This is expected to reduce cycle times for these procurements from months to weeks.
- **Proportionality principle.** Following New Zealand's model, procurement requirements are scaled to the value, complexity, and risk of each procurement. Small procurements (below $250,000) have radically simplified requirements. Mid-range procurements ($250,000-$10 million) follow standard procedures. Large and complex procurements (above $10 million) have enhanced requirements. This right-sizing eliminates unnecessary burden for smaller purchases while maintaining rigor for larger ones.
- **30-day standard timelines.** Target procurement cycle times of 30 days from solicitation to award for standard competitive procurements (excluding complex, high-value acquisitions). This is aggressive by current US standards but consistent with international best practice.

### Pillar 7: Innovation Procurement Pathways

**Source models:** Denmark (PCP) + European Union (innovation partnerships) + Estonia (innovation culture)

**Current US system:** SBIR/STTR, Other Transaction Authorities (limited to DoD and select agencies), challenge.gov, and pilot programs -- all outside the mainstream FAR procurement system.

**Proposed model:**

- **Pre-commercial procurement authority.** All federal agencies receive explicit statutory authority to conduct pre-commercial procurements, modeled on Denmark's PCP framework. PCP allows agencies to fund competitive R&D phases and subsequently procure the resulting innovation, within a single procurement process.
- **Innovation partnership contracts.** Following the EU model, a new contract type is established for long-term innovation partnerships that combine development and procurement phases.
- **Startup-friendly pathways.** Following Denmark's GovTech model, simplified procurement pathways are created for innovative startups, with smaller contract sizes, reduced compliance requirements, and structured support for scaling successful solutions.

## 6. Recommendations for US Policymakers

The following ten recommendations are presented in order of implementation priority, balancing impact, feasibility, and sequencing dependencies. Each recommendation identifies the responsible entity (Congress, the Executive Branch, or both) and a target implementation timeline.

### Recommendation 1: Establish a Unified Federal Procurement Platform

**Responsible entity:** Executive Branch (OMB/OFPP), with Congressional authorization and appropriations

**Timeline:** 3-5 years for full implementation; 12-18 months for initial consolidation of existing systems

**Action:** Direct the Office of Federal Procurement Policy (OFPP) to develop and implement a single, end-to-end federal procurement platform that consolidates the functions currently spread across SAM.gov, FPDS, USASpending.gov, eSRS, CPARS, and agency-specific systems. The platform should follow the architectural principles of Korea's KONEPS and Estonia's X-Road: single registration, once-only data submission, full lifecycle management, and real-time analytics.

**Rationale:** Every country profiled in this paper that has achieved significant procurement improvements has built or consolidated a unified digital platform. The US cannot achieve the other reforms recommended here without first fixing the fragmented data and process infrastructure that underlies the current system.

### Recommendation 2: Restrict LPTA and Default to Best-Value Evaluation

**Responsible entity:** Congress (legislation) + Executive Branch (FAR revision)

**Timeline:** 12-18 months for legislation and initial FAR revisions

**Action:** Amend the Federal Acquisition Regulation to make best-value tradeoff the default evaluation method for all procurements above the simplified acquisition threshold. Restrict LPTA to procurements for commodity goods and services where quality does not meaningfully vary among offerors. Require a written justification for the use of LPTA that explains why quality variation is not a factor.

Establish mandatory price weighting ranges: price must constitute at least 30% and no more than 60% of the total evaluation weight, with the specific weighting determined by the contracting officer based on the nature of the procurement.

**Rationale:** Australia, Singapore, New Zealand, and the UK have all codified that price is not the sole factor. Davidson (2026) provides empirical evidence that evaluation method affects cost outcomes, particularly for smaller contracts. The current system's default to LPTA is not based on evidence of LPTA's effectiveness; it is based on institutional convenience.

### Recommendation 3: Create a Federal Procurement Ombudsman

**Responsible entity:** Congress (legislation)

**Timeline:** 12-18 months for establishment

**Action:** Establish an independent Office of the Federal Procurement Ombudsman, modeled on Canada's OPO, with authority to investigate systemic procurement issues, receive and resolve supplier complaints, conduct proactive reviews of agency procurement practices, and publish annual reports with recommendations. The Ombudsman should operate independently of the Executive Branch agencies it oversees and report directly to Congress.

**Rationale:** The current US oversight system -- GAO protests and COFC litigation -- is reactive, adversarial, and addresses individual cases rather than systemic problems. Canada's Ombudsman model has demonstrated that proactive, systemic oversight can identify and correct problems before they generate protests, saving time and money for both government and industry.

### Recommendation 4: Mandate Social Value Weighting in Evaluation

**Responsible entity:** Congress (legislation) + Executive Branch (FAR revision)

**Timeline:** 18-24 months for legislation and implementation

**Action:** Require all federal procurements above the simplified acquisition threshold to include a minimum 10% weighting for social value criteria in evaluation. Social value criteria should encompass environmental sustainability, community benefit, workforce development, and economic opportunity for disadvantaged groups. Develop standardized social value evaluation criteria (similar to the EU's Green Public Procurement criteria) to ensure consistency across agencies.

**Rationale:** The UK's Social Value Act and New Zealand's broader outcomes framework have demonstrated that social value can be systematically and consistently evaluated in procurement without disrupting competition or increasing costs. The US socioeconomic procurement programs (small business set-asides, 8(a), HUBZone) operate through compliance channels separate from mainstream evaluation; integrating social value into evaluation criteria would mainstream these objectives.

### Recommendation 5: Implement a Beneficial Ownership Transparency Registry

**Responsible entity:** Congress (legislation) + Executive Branch (implementation)

**Timeline:** 12-18 months

**Action:** Require all companies receiving federal contracts above a defined threshold (e.g., $250,000) to disclose their beneficial ownership structures. Establish a central beneficial ownership registry for federal contractors, with appropriate privacy protections, that is accessible to contracting officers and oversight bodies. Chile's experience demonstrates that high compliance rates (59% within three months) are achievable.

**Rationale:** The US currently has limited visibility into who actually owns and controls its federal contractors. The Corporate Transparency Act (effective 2024) establishes a beneficial ownership reporting framework for the private sector; extending this to federal contractors would close a significant transparency gap and support anti-corruption objectives.

### Recommendation 6: Establish Reverse Auctions as the Default for Commodity Procurement

**Responsible entity:** Executive Branch (FAR revision)

**Timeline:** 12 months

**Action:** Make reverse auctions with post-bid qualification the default procurement method for standard goods and commodity services, following Brazil's Pregao model. Implement random closing times to prevent bid manipulation. This does not require legislation; it can be accomplished through FAR revision and OFPP guidance.

**Rationale:** Brazil's experience shows that reverse auctions reduce procurement cycle times from months to weeks for commodity purchases, with significant cost savings. The US already uses reverse auctions in limited contexts; making them the default for appropriate procurement types would extend these benefits across the federal government.

### Recommendation 7: Adopt the Proportionality Principle

**Responsible entity:** Executive Branch (FAR revision)

**Timeline:** 12-18 months

**Action:** Revise the FAR to implement a proportionality principle, following New Zealand's model. Procurement requirements -- documentation, compliance, evaluation procedures -- should be proportional to the value, complexity, and risk of each procurement. Develop tiered requirement sets:

- **Simplified tier** (below $250,000): Radically streamlined requirements, minimal documentation, rapid timelines.
- **Standard tier** ($250,000-$10 million): Standard requirements, 30-day target timeline, mandatory best-value evaluation.
- **Enhanced tier** (above $10 million): Full requirements, comprehensive evaluation, extended timelines as needed.

**Rationale:** The current FAR imposes substantial compliance requirements even for modest procurements, creating barriers to participation (especially for SMEs and startups) and imposing unnecessary costs on agencies. Proportionality would reduce burden where the risk does not justify it while maintaining rigor where it does.

### Recommendation 8: Create Innovation Procurement Authority for All Agencies

**Responsible entity:** Congress (legislation)

**Timeline:** 18-24 months

**Action:** Extend pre-commercial procurement authority and innovation partnership contract authority to all federal agencies, not just the Department of Defense. Establish a government-wide innovation procurement program, modeled on Denmark's GovTech program, that provides simplified procurement pathways for startups and innovative firms. Fund a government-wide innovation procurement support office that provides training, templates, and technical assistance to agencies seeking to use these tools.

**Rationale:** Other Transaction Authorities (OTAs) have demonstrated the value of flexible procurement for innovation, but they are available only to selected agencies. Denmark's experience shows that innovation procurement is a learnable discipline that can be systematically deployed across government. The US has the largest R&D ecosystem in the world; its procurement system should be able to tap that ecosystem effectively.

### Recommendation 9: Invest in the Acquisition Workforce

**Responsible entity:** Congress (appropriations) + Executive Branch (implementation)

**Timeline:** Ongoing, with initial investments in 12-18 months

**Action:** Significantly increase investment in the federal acquisition workforce through:

- **Competitive compensation.** Conduct a market-rate salary study and adjust procurement professional pay to be competitive with private-sector equivalents. Consider procurement-specific pay authorities similar to those available for IT and cybersecurity professionals.
- **Professional certification.** Establish or strengthen a professional procurement certification program, ensuring that all contracting officers meet defined competency standards.
- **Career pathways.** Create clear career pathways that incentivize procurement professionals to remain in government service, including rotation programs, advanced training opportunities, and leadership development.
- **Staffing levels.** Increase the size of the acquisition workforce to match the volume and complexity of the procurement workload. The current ratio of contracting officers to procurement dollars is unsustainable.

**Rationale:** Every successful procurement system profiled in this paper rests on a competent, professional workforce. Technology and rules matter, but they are implemented by people. The US cannot achieve the reforms recommended here without investing in the people who will implement them.

### Recommendation 10: Establish International Procurement Benchmarking

**Responsible entity:** Executive Branch (OFPP)

**Timeline:** 6-12 months for initial implementation

**Action:** Direct OFPP to establish a systematic international benchmarking program for federal procurement. This should include:

- **Annual benchmarking report.** OFPP produces an annual report comparing US procurement performance (cycle times, competition rates, cost growth, SME participation, protest rates) against a peer group of countries with advanced procurement systems.
- **International exchange programs.** Procurement professionals participate in structured exchange programs with peer countries (UK, Korea, Singapore, Australia, Canada) to observe and learn from their practices.
- **International best practice integration.** New FAR revisions and procurement policy changes include a mandatory consideration of international best practice, ensuring that the US is learning from the experiences of other countries rather than reforming in isolation.

**Rationale:** The US is the world's largest government buyer but among the most insular in its approach to procurement policy. Every other domain of government policy -- healthcare, education, defense, environmental regulation -- routinely incorporates international comparison. Procurement should do the same.

## 7. Conclusion

The United States federal government spends more than $700 billion each year buying goods and services from the private sector. The system it uses to make these purchases is fragmented, slow, overly focused on lowest price, and insufficiently oriented toward the public value that taxpayers have a right to expect. These are not new problems. They have been documented by the Government Accountability Office, the Congressional Research Service, the acquisition workforce itself, and academic researchers for decades.

What is new -- or at least newly compiled in this paper -- is the evidence that other countries have already solved many of these problems. South Korea built a single procurement platform that saves $8 billion per year. Chile created a monitoring Observatory that reduced conflicts of interest by 67%. Georgia rebuilt its entire procurement system in ten months for under $1 million and saw corruption virtually eliminated. Australia codified "price is not the sole factor" as a binding rule and directed $12.9 billion in procurement opportunities to Indigenous businesses. The United Kingdom mandated a 10% social value weighting in all major procurements. Brazil cut procurement timelines from 90 days to 17 days with reverse auctions. Denmark used public procurement to bring commercial robotics into hospitals. Estonia built a digital infrastructure that saves 2% of GDP annually.

These are not hypothetical reforms. They are operating systems, producing measurable results, in countries that face the same fundamental procurement challenges as the United States. The solutions exist. The evidence is available. The implementation models are documented.

What remains is political will.

The model framework proposed in this paper -- combining Korea's platform, Estonia's interoperability, Chile's transparency, Singapore's evaluation rigor, Australia's value-for-money rules, the UK's social value mandate, Canada's Ombudsman, Brazil's reverse auctions, New Zealand's proportionality, and Denmark's innovation procurement -- represents not an untested theory but a synthesis of proven practice. Every element has been implemented at scale. Every element has been evaluated and found effective. Every element is adaptable to the American context.

The question for American policymakers is not whether better procurement systems are possible. Other countries have demonstrated that they are. The question is whether the United States -- the world's largest government buyer, with the world's most dynamic private sector and the world's most capable technology industry -- will continue to operate a procurement system that lags behind countries a fraction of its size, or whether it will learn from the evidence and build something better.

The taxpayer deserves the latter.

## 9. Glossary

**Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP):** A structured decision-making methodology that decomposes complex evaluations into a hierarchy of criteria, assigns relative weights through pairwise comparisons, and scores alternatives systematically. Used by Singapore for procurement evaluation.

**Beneficial Ownership:** The natural person(s) who ultimately own or control a legal entity, as distinct from the nominal or registered owners. Beneficial ownership transparency in procurement reveals who actually profits from government contracts.

**Best-Value Tradeoff:** A procurement evaluation method that considers multiple factors -- technical merit, past performance, management approach, and other quality indicators -- alongside price. The government may award to a higher-priced offeror if the quality advantages justify the price premium.

**ChileCompra:** Chile's national e-procurement platform and the government agency that manages it. Processes approximately $16 billion in annual procurement volume.

**Crown Commercial Service (CCS):** The UK government's centralized procurement authority, which manages framework agreements and commercial arrangements on behalf of government departments.

**e-Procurement:** The use of electronic systems to conduct some or all stages of the procurement process, from solicitation through contract management and payment.

**FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation):** The principal set of rules governing procurement by US federal executive agencies. The FAR and its agency supplements run to thousands of pages.

**FPDS (Federal Procurement Data System):** The US government's database of record for federal procurement transactions.

**GeBIZ:** Singapore's Government Electronic Business portal, the country's single e-procurement platform since 2000.

**Innovation Partnership:** An EU procurement procedure that allows a contracting authority to establish a long-term partnership with one or more firms for the development and subsequent purchase of innovative products or services.

**KONEPS (Korea ON-line E-Procurement System):** South Korea's unified e-procurement platform, processing approximately $160 billion in annual procurement volume.

**Life-Cycle Costing:** An evaluation approach that considers the total cost of a product or service over its entire useful life, including acquisition, operating, maintenance, and disposal costs.

**LPTA (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable):** A procurement evaluation method in which the contract is awarded to the lowest-priced offeror whose proposal meets all minimum technical requirements. Quality above the minimum threshold is not considered.

**MEAT (Most Economically Advantageous Tender):** The EU standard for procurement evaluation, requiring consideration of criteria beyond price. Replaced in the UK by MAT (Most Advantageous Tender) under the Procurement Act 2023.

**OCDS (Open Contracting Data Standard):** An international data standard for publishing structured, machine-readable procurement data. Adopted by Chile, the UK, and dozens of other countries.

**Pre-Commercial Procurement (PCP):** A procurement approach in which the government contracts with multiple firms to develop solutions to a defined problem through competitive R&D phases, before procuring the resulting innovation.

**Pregao:** Brazil's reverse auction procurement system, in which vendors compete on price first and qualification is evaluated only for the winning bidder.

**Procurement Ombudsman:** An independent official with authority to investigate procurement complaints, review systemic procurement practices, and make recommendations. Canada's Office of the Procurement Ombudsman is the model discussed in this paper.

**Proportionality Principle:** The principle that the level of procurement process, documentation, and compliance required should be proportionate to the value, complexity, and risk of the procurement. Codified in New Zealand's procurement rules.

**Reverse Auction:** A procurement method in which sellers compete to offer the lowest price, with prices typically decreasing over the course of the auction. The opposite of a traditional auction, where buyers compete and prices increase.

**SAM.gov (System for Award Management):** The US government's official system for entity registration, opportunity posting, and other procurement functions. Consolidates several previously separate systems.

**Social Value:** The broader public benefits -- environmental, social, economic, and community -- that result from a procurement decision, beyond the direct goods or services being purchased. Mandated as an evaluation criterion in the UK and New Zealand.

**Value for Money:** The assessment of whether a procurement delivers the best available combination of quality and cost over the life of the goods or services. Distinguished from "lowest price" by the inclusion of quality, reliability, sustainability, and other non-price factors.

**X-Road:** Estonia's secure, decentralized data exchange platform connecting government institutions and enabling seamless data sharing. The technological backbone of Estonia's digital government.
