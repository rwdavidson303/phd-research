## Abstract

Public procurement represents one of the largest categories of government expenditure worldwide, accounting for approximately $13 trillion annually—roughly 15 percent of global GDP. In the United States alone, the federal government obligates over $700 billion each year through contracts, grants, and other instruments. Despite this enormous economic footprint, procurement systems in many countries remain plagued by inefficiency, corruption, limited competition, and an excessive focus on lowest price rather than best value. This report undertakes a comprehensive comparative analysis of twelve countries and supranational frameworks that have achieved measurable improvements in procurement outcomes. Drawing on empirical data, government reports, and academic literature, it examines digital platform champions (South Korea, Estonia, Singapore, Georgia), value-for-money leaders (United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, Canada), competition and anti-corruption innovators (Chile, Brazil, European Union), and innovation procurement pioneers (Denmark and the Nordic countries). The analysis identifies ten cross-cutting success patterns, synthesizes a seven-pillar "Model Procurement System" tailored to the US context, and offers fifteen actionable recommendations organized by implementation timeline. The central argument is that the United States can substantially improve procurement outcomes—saving taxpayer money, increasing competition, reducing corruption, and delivering better public services—by selectively adopting proven reforms from around the world while building on its own considerable strengths in transparency and small business inclusion.

**Keywords:** public procurement, government contracting, best value, source selection, comparative policy analysis, digital procurement, value for money, anti-corruption, procurement reform

## Chapter 1: Introduction — The $13 Trillion Question

### 1.1 The Scale of Public Procurement

Every day, governments around the world spend billions of dollars purchasing goods and services on behalf of their citizens. Roads are built, hospitals are equipped, defense systems are maintained, information technology is deployed, and social services are delivered—all through the mechanism of public procurement. The cumulative scale of this activity is staggering. According to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), public procurement accounts for approximately 12 to 15 percent of global gross domestic product, translating to an estimated $13 trillion in annual spending (OECD, 2023). In OECD member countries specifically, procurement represents an average of 12.6 percent of GDP and nearly one-third of total government expenditure (OECD, 2021).

The United States stands as the world's single largest public purchaser. Federal contract spending exceeded $700 billion in fiscal year 2023, according to data from USAspending.gov, the government's transparency platform. When state and local procurement is included, the figure approaches $2 trillion annually. The Department of Defense alone accounts for roughly two-thirds of federal contract dollars, with civilian agencies—led by the Department of Health and Human Services, the Department of Energy, and the General Services Administration—comprising the remainder (USAspending.gov, 2024).

These numbers, enormous as they are, only capture the direct fiscal dimension. Public procurement also serves as a lever for broader policy objectives: promoting small and disadvantaged businesses, advancing environmental sustainability, driving innovation, supporting indigenous and minority communities, and combating corruption. When procurement works well, governments deliver better services at lower cost, the private sector thrives through fair competition, and public trust in government increases. When it works poorly—when contracts go to the wrong bidders, when costs spiral out of control, when corruption distorts outcomes—the consequences are measured not just in wasted dollars but in diminished public welfare.

### 1.2 Why Procurement Reform Matters Now

Several converging trends make procurement reform an urgent priority. First, fiscal pressures are intensifying across the developed world. Aging populations, rising healthcare costs, climate adaptation requirements, and the long fiscal tail of pandemic-era spending are all placing unprecedented demands on public budgets. Getting more value from every procurement dollar is no longer merely desirable—it is essential.

Second, the digital revolution has created both new possibilities and new imperatives for procurement modernization. Countries that have built integrated digital platforms—South Korea, Estonia, Singapore, Georgia, and Chile among them—have demonstrated dramatic improvements in speed, cost, transparency, and competition. Countries that have not risk falling further behind, burdened by paper-based processes, fragmented systems, and manual oversight that cannot keep pace with the volume and complexity of modern government purchasing.

Third, public expectations of government accountability have risen sharply. Citizens and watchdog organizations have access to more information than ever before and are increasingly unwilling to tolerate opaque, wasteful, or corrupt procurement practices. The global push toward open data, beneficial ownership transparency, and the Open Contracting Data Standard (OCDS) reflects a fundamental shift in the social contract around public spending.

Fourth, the nature of what governments need to buy is changing. Traditional procurement was dominated by standardized commodities—office supplies, vehicles, construction materials—where lowest price was a reasonable selection criterion. Today, governments increasingly purchase complex services, advanced technology, and innovative solutions where quality, past performance, lifecycle cost, and strategic alignment matter as much or more than initial price. Procurement systems designed for the commodity era are poorly suited to these challenges.

> **Key Insight:** The World Bank estimates that improving procurement efficiency by just 1 percent globally would free up $130 billion annually—enough to fund the education of 45 million children or provide clean water access to 370 million people (World Bank, 2020).

### 1.3 The Cost of Getting It Wrong

The consequences of procurement failure are both pervasive and severe. Transparency International estimates that corruption in public procurement costs the global economy between $1.5 and $2 trillion annually—approximately 10 to 25 percent of total procurement value in many countries (Transparency International, 2022). Even in countries with robust rule-of-law traditions, procurement waste through inefficiency, poor evaluation, and risk-averse decision-making imposes enormous costs.

In the United States, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) has repeatedly documented procurement failures across federal agencies. Major IT projects have a failure rate exceeding 50 percent, with the Healthcare.gov rollout in 2013 serving as perhaps the most visible recent example (Standish Group, 2020). Defense procurement overruns are legendary: the F-35 Joint Strike Fighter program, originally estimated at $233 billion, had ballooned to over $400 billion by 2023, making it the most expensive weapons program in history (GAO, 2023). The Littoral Combat Ship program was terminated after billions in cost overruns and persistent performance deficiencies.

These are not isolated incidents. They reflect systemic weaknesses: evaluation criteria that prioritize initial price over lifecycle value, workforce shortages that leave inadequately trained personnel managing billion-dollar contracts, protest mechanisms that incentivize delay, and a regulatory framework—the Federal Acquisition Regulation—whose sheer complexity deters innovation and participation.

### 1.4 The Promise of Comparative Analysis

This report proceeds from a simple but powerful premise: the United States does not need to invent solutions from scratch. Across the globe, countries of varying sizes, income levels, and institutional contexts have developed procurement innovations that deliver measurable results. South Korea's KONEPS platform saves an estimated $8 billion annually through digitization. Georgia reduced procurement corruption from 97 percent to 3 percent in less than a decade. The United Kingdom's Social Value Act has embedded public value considerations into every major contract. Chile's Transparency Observatory has cut procurement conflicts by 67 percent. Estonia's X-Road infrastructure saves 2 percent of GDP through digital interoperability.

The challenge is not a lack of models but a lack of synthesis. Existing literature tends to examine individual country systems or specific reform dimensions (digitization, anti-corruption, SME access) in isolation. Policymakers seeking practical guidance must piece together insights from dozens of disparate sources. This report aims to fill that gap by providing a systematic, evidence-based comparative analysis that identifies what works, why it works, and how it can be adapted to the US context.

### 1.5 Methodology and Scope

This analysis draws on multiple categories of evidence: official government procurement statistics and annual reports; academic literature on comparative procurement policy; international organization studies from the OECD, World Bank, United Nations, and European Commission; investigative journalism and civil society reports; and primary legislative and regulatory texts. Countries were selected to represent geographic diversity, varying levels of economic development, and a range of reform approaches. The twelve primary systems examined are South Korea (digital platform consolidation), Estonia (interoperable digital government infrastructure), Singapore (risk-based evaluation and anti-corruption), Georgia (rapid anti-corruption transformation), the United Kingdom (social value and procurement legislation), Australia (indigenous procurement and value-for-money codification), New Zealand (public value framework and proportionality), Canada (procurement ombudsman and institutional reform), Chile (transparency observatory and open data), Brazil (reverse auction innovation), the European Union (supranational framework and sustainable procurement), and Denmark/Nordics (innovation procurement and pre-commercial procurement).

The report is organized in three parts. Part I (Chapters 1-2) establishes the case for reform by examining the global procurement landscape and the US federal system in detail. Part II (Chapters 3-6) provides deep dives into each country system, organized thematically. Part III (Chapters 7-10) synthesizes findings, presents a model procurement system, and offers actionable recommendations.

### 1.6 A Note on Terminology

Procurement terminology varies across jurisdictions and can be a source of confusion. In this report, "public procurement" and "government contracting" are used interchangeably to refer to the process by which government entities purchase goods, services, and works from the private sector. "Best value" refers to evaluation approaches that consider factors beyond price, including quality, past performance, technical capability, and social outcomes. "Lowest Price Technically Acceptable" (LPTA) refers to evaluation approaches where the contract is awarded to the lowest-priced offer that meets minimum technical requirements. "Value for money" (VfM) is the broader concept—common in Commonwealth countries—that public resources should be used to achieve the best possible outcome relative to total cost. A comprehensive glossary is provided at the end of this report.

## Chapter 2: The US Federal Procurement System — Strengths, Weaknesses, and the Path Not Taken

### 2.1 How the Federal Acquisition Regulation Works (Simplified)

The United States federal procurement system is governed by a complex web of statutes, regulations, and policies. At its foundation sits the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), a document exceeding 2,000 pages that establishes uniform policies and procedures for acquisitions by all executive agencies. The FAR is supplemented by agency-specific regulations—the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement (DFARS) for the Department of Defense, the General Services Administration Acquisition Regulation (GSAR), and dozens of others—adding thousands more pages of rules.

The basic procurement process, greatly simplified, proceeds through several stages. First, the requiring agency identifies what it needs to buy and develops a statement of work or performance work statement. Second, contracting officers survey the market to identify potential sources and refine requirements. Third, the opportunity is posted (typically on SAM.gov for contracts over $25,000), and vendors submit proposals. Fourth, proposals are evaluated against stated criteria—under LPTA, the lowest-priced technically acceptable offer wins; under best-value tradeoff, evaluators weigh price against non-price factors. Fifth, the contracting officer selects the winning offeror and issues the contract. Sixth, losing bidders may protest the award decision to the GAO, the Court of Federal Claims, or the agency itself. Seventh, the government administers the contract, monitors performance, and processes payments. Finally, upon completion, the contract is formally closed out and performance is recorded.

| **Evaluation Method** | **How It Works** | **When Used** | **Key Advantage** | **Key Risk** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lowest Price Technically Acceptable (LPTA) | Lowest price that meets minimum technical bar wins | Commodities, well-defined requirements, commercial items | Simple, fast, defensible | Incentivizes "race to bottom," ignores quality |
| Best Value Tradeoff | Price weighed against technical quality, past performance, other factors | Complex services, IT, professional services | Rewards quality and innovation | Slower, more subjective, protest-prone |
| Lowest Price (sealed bidding) | Strictly lowest price via sealed bids | Construction, commodities with clear specs | Maximum price competition | No quality differentiation |
| Highest Technically Rated with Fair and Reasonable Price | Best technical proposal with price check | High-stakes professional services | Prioritizes quality | May not minimize cost |

*Table 2.1: Federal Evaluation Methods Compared*

### 2.2 The LPTA Problem

Perhaps no single aspect of US procurement has generated more debate than the use of Lowest Price Technically Acceptable (LPTA) evaluation. Under LPTA, the government defines minimum technical requirements—a "pass/fail" threshold—and awards the contract to the lowest-priced offeror that clears that threshold. There is no credit for exceeding requirements, no consideration of past performance beyond a basic responsibility determination, and no evaluation of innovation or lifecycle value.

LPTA has a legitimate role in procurement. For commodity purchases with clear specifications—printer paper, gasoline, standard commercial software licenses—lowest price is often the most rational selection criterion. The problem arises when LPTA is used for complex services, professional consulting, IT development, facilities maintenance, and other acquisitions where quality differences among offerors are significant and consequential.

The overuse of LPTA accelerated during the Budget Control Act era of the 2010s, when sequestration pressures led agencies to prioritize short-term cost savings over long-term value. A 2017 RAND Corporation study found that LPTA usage in Department of Defense service contracts had increased substantially, correlating with declines in contractor performance ratings and increases in cost growth during contract execution (RAND, 2017). The National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2017 (Section 813) subsequently restricted LPTA use for DoD service contracts, requiring a written determination that quality differences among offerors were minimal and that minimum requirements could be clearly defined. Yet implementation has been uneven, and LPTA remains widespread across civilian agencies.

> **The LPTA Paradox:** Contracts awarded under LPTA criteria frequently end up costing *more* than best-value alternatives because low initial bids are followed by change orders, cost overruns, poor performance requiring re-procurement, and litigation. A 2019 analysis by the Professional Services Council found that LPTA contracts experienced 18 percent higher rates of cost growth during execution compared to best-value tradeoff contracts (PSC, 2019).

### 2.3 What the US Does Well

Any fair assessment of the US procurement system must acknowledge its considerable strengths. The United States is a global leader in several procurement dimensions.

**Transparency and Open Data.** USAspending.gov is one of the world's most comprehensive procurement transparency platforms, providing searchable data on virtually all federal contract actions. The Federal Procurement Data System (FPDS) captures detailed information on over 500,000 contract actions annually. The DATA Act of 2014 further strengthened spending transparency by linking procurement data to agency budget and financial information. Few countries match this level of public access to procurement data.

**Small Business Programs.** The Small Business Administration (SBA) administers a suite of programs designed to ensure small and disadvantaged businesses receive a fair share of federal contracting opportunities. The government-wide goal is 23 percent of prime contract dollars to small businesses, with sub-goals for small disadvantaged businesses (5%), women-owned small businesses (5%), service-disabled veteran-owned small businesses (3%), and businesses in Historically Underutilized Business Zones (HUBZone, 3%). In fiscal year 2023, the government exceeded its overall small business goal for the tenth consecutive year, awarding 28.4 percent of eligible contract dollars—approximately $178.6 billion—to small businesses (SBA, 2024).

**Protest Mechanisms.** While protest mechanisms are often cited as a weakness, they also represent a strength: the United States provides more robust due process protections for disappointed bidders than virtually any other country. The GAO bid protest function provides a relatively fast (100-day decision timeline), low-cost mechanism for challenging procurement decisions, serving as an important check on agency discretion.

**Workforce Professionalization.** The Federal Acquisition Workforce includes over 190,000 professionals, many of whom hold certifications through the Federal Acquisition Certification programs (FAC-C, FAC-COR, FAC-P/PM). The Defense Acquisition University (DAU) is one of the world's premier procurement training institutions.

**Category Management.** The government has made significant progress in leveraging its collective buying power through category management, establishing Best-in-Class (BIC) contract vehicles that consolidate demand across agencies to achieve better pricing and terms. Between fiscal years 2017 and 2023, BIC spending increased from $27 billion to over $120 billion annually, generating billions in estimated savings (OMB, 2023).

|---|---|---|

### 2.4 What the US Does Poorly

Against these strengths, the US system exhibits significant weaknesses that have proven resistant to decades of reform efforts.

**Fragmented Digital Infrastructure.** Unlike South Korea's single-window KONEPS or Singapore's unified GeBIZ, the US procurement ecosystem is fragmented across dozens of systems that do not interoperate seamlessly. Vendors must navigate SAM.gov (registration), beta.SAM.gov (opportunity search), FPDS (historical data), USAspending.gov (spending data), eSRS (subcontracting reports), CPARS (past performance), and numerous agency-specific platforms. A 2022 survey by the National Contract Management Association found that 67 percent of contracting professionals cited system fragmentation as a significant barrier to efficiency (NCMA, 2022).

**Speed and Cycle Time.** Federal procurements are extraordinarily slow by international standards. The average time from solicitation to award for a competitive contract exceeds 200 days. For major acquisitions, timelines of two to three years from requirements definition to contract start are common. In South Korea, KONEPS reduced average bidding time from 30 hours to less than 2 hours. Georgia's e-procurement system processes routine purchases in days. The US system, burdened by regulatory requirements, multiple review layers, and protest risk, operates at a fraction of this speed.

**Protest Paralysis.** While protest rights are a strength in principle, the current system creates perverse incentives. Losing bidders can file protests at the GAO, the Court of Federal Claims, and the agency level—sometimes simultaneously. The mere threat of protest causes contracting officers to engage in "defensive acquisition"—adding layers of documentation and process not to improve outcomes but to survive legal challenge. A 2018 RAND study estimated that protest-related risk aversion adds 5 to 15 percent to total acquisition costs across the Department of Defense (RAND, 2018).

**Workforce Shortages and Skill Gaps.** Despite its large absolute size, the federal acquisition workforce has not kept pace with the growing complexity and volume of procurement. The ratio of contract dollars managed per contracting officer has increased dramatically over the past two decades. Moreover, critical skills—data analytics, market intelligence, negotiation, and category expertise—are in chronic short supply. The average age of the contracting workforce is over 45, and agencies struggle to recruit and retain younger professionals who can command higher salaries in the private sector.

**Inconsistent Performance Management.** The Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS) is conceptually sound but inconsistently implemented. Many contract actions do not receive performance evaluations, and the evaluations that are completed vary widely in quality and rigor. This means that past performance—theoretically one of the most important evaluation factors in best-value procurements—is often undermined by incomplete or unreliable data.

**Regulatory Complexity.** The FAR and its supplements constitute a regulatory edifice of extraordinary complexity. A congressional study found that the total regulatory burden on federal contractors had grown by over 30 percent in the preceding decade, with the average large defense contract incorporating compliance requirements from over 100 different regulatory clauses (Section 809 Panel, 2019). This complexity deters small and innovative firms from participating, increases compliance costs for all parties, and diverts contracting officer attention from strategic activities to administrative compliance.

> **By the Numbers:** The Section 809 Panel—a congressionally mandated advisory body—identified over 1,000 specific recommendations for streamlining defense acquisition. Its 2019 final report concluded that "the defense acquisition system has become so encumbered by statute, regulation, and policy that it often cannot deliver timely, cost-effective, and innovative solutions to the warfighter" (Section 809 Panel, 2019, p. 1).

### 2.5 The Bid Protest Ecosystem

The US bid protest system deserves special attention because it is both a distinctive feature of the American procurement landscape and a source of significant tension. Three principal forums exist for challenging procurement decisions: agency-level protests filed directly with the contracting agency (typically the fastest but least independent option); GAO protests filed with the Government Accountability Office, which issues non-binding recommendations within 100 days; and Court of Federal Claims protests, which offer binding judicial decisions but slower timelines and higher costs.

The protest system generates approximately 2,500 cases annually at the GAO alone, plus hundreds more at the COFC and thousands at the agency level. While the overall protest rate relative to total contract actions is low (less than 2 percent), protests are concentrated in high-value, high-visibility contracts where the stakes and consequences are greatest. GAO sustained rates hover around 12-15 percent of cases that reach a merits decision, though a significant percentage (40-50%) result in voluntary corrective action by the agency (GAO, 2023).

### 2.6 Recent Reform Efforts and the Path Forward

The United States has not been idle in procurement reform. Notable recent initiatives include the Section 809 Panel (2016-2019), which produced over 1,000 recommendations for defense acquisition streamlining; GSA's Procurement Innovation Lab, testing new evaluation techniques including oral presentations, demo days, and advisory down-selects; LPTA restrictions in the NDAA FY2017 (Section 813); category management and Best-in-Class vehicle expansion; e-commerce portal pilots testing commercial marketplace approaches; the OMB Myth-Busters campaign to dispel misconceptions about what the FAR prohibits; and ongoing CX/UX improvements to SAM.gov.

These efforts are individually commendable but collectively insufficient. They represent incremental improvements to a system that needs structural transformation. The US procurement system did not develop in isolation, and it need not reform in isolation. The challenges it faces—balancing cost and quality, managing complexity, ensuring transparency, promoting competition, building workforce capability—are universal. Other countries have confronted these same challenges and, in many cases, have found solutions that deliver measurable results.

What international comparison offers is not a blueprint but a menu of proven options—a catalogue of reforms that have worked elsewhere, adapted to different institutional contexts, and validated by empirical results. The twelve systems examined in Part II of this report collectively represent perhaps the richest repository of procurement innovation available anywhere in the world. The question is not whether the US can learn from them, but whether it will.

## Chapter 3: Digital Platform Champions — South Korea, Estonia, Singapore, Georgia

### 3.1 Introduction: The Digital Procurement Revolution

The most dramatic procurement improvements of the past two decades have been driven by digital transformation. Countries that committed early and comprehensively to electronic procurement have achieved measurable gains across every performance dimension: cost reduction, speed, transparency, competition, and anti-corruption. This chapter examines four systems that represent the global frontier of digital procurement: South Korea's KONEPS, Estonia's X-Road-enabled e-procurement, Singapore's GeBIZ, and Georgia's Ge-GP. Each tells a different story—massive scale consolidation, interoperable infrastructure, disciplined governance, and rapid post-conflict transformation—but all demonstrate the power of technology to fundamentally reshape the procurement relationship between government and the private sector.

### 3.2 South Korea: KONEPS — The World's Largest Single-Window Procurement Platform

#### Background and Development

The Korea ON-line E-Procurement System (KONEPS), operated by the Public Procurement Service (PPS), is widely regarded as the world's most advanced and comprehensive e-procurement platform. Launched in 2002, KONEPS was the culmination of South Korea's aggressive e-government strategy that began in the late 1990s, driven by the dual imperatives of post-Asian financial crisis efficiency and the country's commitment to becoming a global leader in information technology.

Before KONEPS, Korean public procurement was characterized by paper-based processes, fragmented agency purchasing, limited competition, and significant opportunities for corruption. The average time to complete a bidding process was approximately 30 hours of combined government and vendor effort. Procurement was distributed across hundreds of agencies with inconsistent procedures and minimal data sharing.

#### System Architecture and Functionality

KONEPS is a true single-window platform, integrating the entire procurement lifecycle from requisition through payment into a unified digital environment. The system connects approximately 68,000 public entities—central government agencies, local governments, public corporations, and government-funded institutions—with over 430,000 registered suppliers (PPS, 2023).

The platform's architecture is built around several core modules. Electronic bidding ensures all public bids above threshold values are conducted electronically, supporting multiple procurement methods including open bidding, restricted bidding, negotiated contracts, and reverse auctions. Electronic contracts handle contract formation, modification, and administration fully digitally, with electronic signatures and automated workflow management. Electronic payment is integrated with the government's financial management system, enabling faster and more transparent payments to contractors. A comprehensive commodity classification and catalogue system enables standardized commodity management and price benchmarking. Finally, inter-agency integration connects KONEPS with 220+ external systems, including business registration databases, tax systems, credit evaluation agencies, and banking networks—eliminating the need for vendors to submit the same information to multiple entities.

> **KONEPS by the Numbers:** Over $160 billion in annual transaction volume; 430,000+ registered suppliers; 68,000+ public buying entities; 220+ integrated external systems; average bidding time reduced from 30 hours to under 2 hours (PPS, 2023).

#### Measured Outcomes

The quantified impact of KONEPS is extraordinary. According to official government estimates and independent analyses, the system generates approximately $8 billion in annual savings through a combination of mechanisms (Lim, 2009; PPS, 2023). Transaction cost reduction from elimination of paper processing, physical document submission, and in-person interactions saves an estimated $4.5 billion annually for both government and vendors. Increased transparency and ease of participation have broadened the vendor base, driving prices down through genuine competition. The average procurement cycle time has decreased by over 80 percent, freeing contracting staff to focus on strategic activities. Automated processes and audit trails have substantially reduced opportunities for corrupt interactions.

#### AI and Advanced Analytics (2018-Present)

Since 2018, PPS has been integrating artificial intelligence and big data analytics into KONEPS to further enhance its capabilities. Key initiatives include AI-powered price analysis using machine learning algorithms to analyze historical pricing data, identify anomalies, detect potential bid rigging, and generate more accurate pre-award cost estimates. Predictive demand forecasting uses big data analytics to forecast government demand for common commodities, enabling better inventory management and strategic sourcing. Automated vendor matching uses AI systems to match government requirements with vendor capabilities, facilitating more efficient market engagement. And bid rigging detection employs pattern recognition algorithms to flag suspicious bidding patterns for investigation, increasing the effectiveness of competition enforcement.

#### International Export and Influence

South Korea has actively exported the KONEPS model to developing countries through technical assistance programs. Notable implementations include Vietnam (KONEPS-based e-procurement system deployed with Korean technical assistance), Costa Rica (adopted Korean e-procurement technology and methodology), Mongolia (implemented KONEPS-inspired platform for government procurement), and Tunisia (received Korean support for e-procurement system development), along with multiple additional countries through World Bank, Asian Development Bank, and bilateral aid programs.

The exportability of KONEPS demonstrates a critical point: digital procurement platforms are not luxury goods available only to wealthy nations. With appropriate adaptation and political commitment, the core principles—single window, end-to-end digitization, interoperability—can be implemented across diverse institutional and economic contexts.

#### Lessons for the United States

KONEPS offers several direct lessons for US procurement reform. First, consolidation works. By bringing 68,000 entities onto a single platform, Korea achieved economies of scale and network effects that fragmented systems cannot match. The US, with its hundreds of disconnected procurement systems, forgoes these benefits. Second, end-to-end digitization is essential. Partially digital systems that require manual steps at key junctures capture only a fraction of the available efficiency gains. Third, integration with external data sources—business registries, tax records, banking systems—eliminates redundant data entry and enables real-time verification. Fourth, the technology investment pays for itself many times over: KONEPS's development cost was a tiny fraction of its annual savings.

### 3.3 Estonia: X-Road and the Once-Only Principle

#### The Digital Society Foundation

Estonia's approach to e-procurement is inseparable from its broader digital government strategy, which has made this small Baltic nation of 1.3 million people one of the world's most digitally advanced societies. At the foundation of Estonia's digital ecosystem is X-Road, a distributed data exchange platform that enables secure, real-time data sharing between government agencies, private sector entities, and citizens.

X-Road was launched in 2001, initially to enable secure data exchange between government registries. It has since grown to connect 929 institutions and facilitate over 2.7 billion queries—a remarkable number for a country of 1.3 million people (e-Estonia, 2024). The platform operates on the "once-only principle": citizens and businesses provide any piece of information to the government only once, and that information is shared (with appropriate authorization) across all entities that need it.

#### E-Procurement Within the X-Road Ecosystem

Estonia's e-procurement system leverages X-Road's infrastructure to create a seamless procurement experience. When a business registers in Estonia's business registry, that information is automatically available to the procurement system. Tax compliance can be verified in real time through automated queries to the tax authority. Beneficial ownership information, criminal records checks, and professional certifications are all accessible through the same infrastructure.

The practical effect is dramatic. A vendor responding to a public tender in Estonia does not need to gather and submit certificates, registrations, tax clearances, or other documentation manually. The system pulls the relevant data automatically, in real time, from authoritative sources. This eliminates not just administrative burden but also opportunities for fraud through forged or outdated documentation.

#### Measured Outcomes

The economic impact of Estonia's digital government—including but not limited to procurement—has been estimated at 2 percent of GDP annually in administrative savings (e-Estonia, 2024). For a country with a GDP of approximately $38 billion, this translates to roughly $760 million per year—enormous on a per capita basis.

In terms of procurement-specific outcomes, Estonia ranks second in the European Union on the Innovation Procurement Scoreboard, reflecting the system's success in enabling government purchasing of novel solutions (European Commission, 2023). The country has also achieved high levels of competition, with an average of 4.8 bids per tender compared to the EU average of 3.3, and very low rates of single-bid procurement.

> **The "Once-Only" Dividend:** Estonia estimates that the once-only principle saves each citizen an average of 5 working days per year in eliminated bureaucratic interactions. Applied to the procurement context, this means vendors spend dramatically less time on administrative compliance and more on actual service delivery (e-Estonia, 2024).

#### International Export: X-Road Goes Global

Estonia has actively promoted X-Road's adoption internationally. Finland adopted X-Road as the basis for its own Suomi.fi data exchange platform, creating a cross-border digital infrastructure between the two countries. The X-Road platform has been deployed or is being piloted in over 20 countries, including Ukraine, Iceland, Japan, and several African and Latin American nations.

In 2018, Estonia and Finland connected their X-Road instances, enabling the first cross-border "once-only" data exchange in the world. This has direct implications for cross-border public procurement, potentially enabling vendors from one country to participate in another country's tenders without duplicating registration and documentation requirements.

#### Lessons for the United States

Estonia's primary lesson for the US is about infrastructure, not applications. The US does not lack procurement technology; it lacks the interoperable data infrastructure that would allow its many systems to communicate seamlessly. X-Road demonstrates that investing in a shared data exchange layer—rather than building monolithic centralized systems—can achieve integration while preserving the autonomy of individual agencies and systems. The once-only principle, if applied to US federal procurement, would eliminate the redundant data submissions that vendors currently make to SAM.gov, FPDS, CPARS, eSRS, and agency-specific portals.

### 3.4 Singapore: GeBIZ — Discipline, Risk Management, and Anti-Corruption

#### System Overview

The Government Electronic Business (GeBIZ) portal is Singapore's centralized e-procurement platform, serving all government ministries and statutory boards—over 120 agencies in total. Launched in 2000, GeBIZ was one of Asia's earliest comprehensive e-procurement systems and has been continuously refined over more than two decades. GeBIZ integrates the full procurement lifecycle: opportunity publication, bid submission, evaluation, award, and contract management. All government procurement opportunities above S$6,000 must be posted on GeBIZ, ensuring comprehensive coverage and transparency.

#### The Anti-Corruption Achievement

Singapore's procurement system must be understood in the context of the country's broader governance model, which places extraordinary emphasis on anti-corruption, meritocracy, and institutional integrity. The Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau (CPIB) operates with substantial independence, and public servants face severe penalties for corrupt conduct—including mandatory minimum imprisonment.

The results speak for themselves. Singapore consistently ranks among the top five least corrupt countries in the world on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index. In procurement specifically, Singapore has achieved a procurement fraud rate of approximately 5 percent—compared to a global average of 29 percent (ACFE, 2022). This extraordinary differential reflects not just technology but a comprehensive ecosystem of prevention, detection, and enforcement.

#### Evaluation Methodology: The Price-Quality Framework

Singapore's procurement framework explicitly rejects a lowest-price-only approach. The Government Instruction Manual on Procurement establishes a structured evaluation methodology that weighs both price and quality using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) or similar multi-criteria decision analysis tools. For most procurements above specified thresholds, evaluation teams assess proposals against pre-defined criteria covering technical merit, track record, methodology, team qualifications, and price.

This is reinforced by Singapore's "value-for-money, not lowest quote" policy, which explicitly states that agencies should not award contracts solely on the basis of price when quality differences among offerors are meaningful. Contracting authorities are expected to exercise professional judgment in balancing cost and quality, supported by structured evaluation tools and clear documentation requirements.

#### Lessons for the United States

Singapore demonstrates that low corruption and efficient procurement are not competing objectives but complementary ones. Its GeBIZ platform provides transparency and audit trails; its evaluation methodology ensures quality-focused outcomes; its governance framework provides oversight without dysfunction; and its workforce policies ensure competent, ethical decision-making. For the US, the most transferable elements are Singapore's structured evaluation methodology (providing a rigorous alternative to LPTA) and its approach to governance that provides accountability without the adversarial dynamics of the protest system.

### 3.5 Georgia: From Corruption to Transparency in Record Time

#### The Starting Point: A Corrupt System

Georgia's procurement transformation is perhaps the most dramatic in the world. In the early 2000s, following decades of Soviet-era governance and post-independence instability, Georgia's public procurement system was ranked among the most corrupt globally. Surveys indicated that corruption affected up to 97 percent of procurement transactions (World Bank, 2012). Bribery was routine, competition was virtually nonexistent, and public contracts were widely viewed as instruments of patronage rather than public service delivery.

#### The Transformation

The 2003 Rose Revolution brought to power a reform-minded government committed to radical transformation of public institutions, including procurement. In 2010, the Georgian government launched the electronic Government Procurement system (Ge-GP), which was developed in just ten months at a cost of less than $1 million (Georgian State Procurement Agency, 2012). This remarkable timeline and budget—compared to the multi-year, multi-billion-dollar IT projects common in larger countries—reflected both the urgency of reform and the advantages of building a new system on modern technology rather than retrofitting legacy infrastructure.

The Ge-GP system was designed from the ground up for transparency and competition. All public procurements above threshold values must be conducted through the platform, eliminating the offline channels where corruption flourished. The system makes extensive use of electronic reverse auctions for commodity purchases, driving prices down through visible, real-time competition. The platform integrates with government registries to verify vendor qualifications automatically. All tender documents, bids, evaluation results, and contracts are publicly accessible through the platform. And an integrated electronic dispute resolution mechanism provides fast, low-cost challenge procedures.

#### Measured Outcomes

The transformation has been dramatic and well-documented. Surveys indicate that procurement corruption dropped from approximately 97 percent to approximately 3 percent—a near-complete reversal (World Bank, 2012; Georgian SPA, 2018). The average number of bidders per tender increased substantially, with the vendor base expanding from a small circle of connected firms to a broad, competitive market. Electronic reverse auctions alone generated average price reductions of 10-15 percent compared to previous methods. Routine procurements that previously took weeks of paper-based processing were completed in days. Georgia received the United Nations Public Service Award in 2012 for its e-procurement system, and the World Bank cited Georgia as a global best practice example.

#### Lessons for the United States

Georgia's experience demonstrates that transformational procurement reform is possible even in the most challenging environments. Three lessons are particularly relevant for the US. First, the perfect need not be the enemy of the good: Georgia built a functional system rapidly and iterated from there, rather than spending years on requirements analysis and design. Second, mandatory adoption is critical—optional e-procurement systems achieve only partial coverage and perpetuate parallel offline processes. Third, transparency is the most powerful anti-corruption tool: when all procurement information is publicly accessible in real time, corrupt practices become far more difficult to sustain.

## Chapter 4: Value-for-Money Leaders — United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, Canada

### 4.1 Introduction: Beyond Lowest Price

While the digital platform champions examined in Chapter 3 demonstrate the power of technology to transform procurement mechanics, a different group of countries has led the world in redefining *what* procurement systems should optimize for. The countries examined in this chapter—the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, and Canada—share a common heritage in the Westminster parliamentary tradition and a common commitment to moving beyond lowest-price procurement toward frameworks that explicitly value quality, social outcomes, indigenous inclusion, and long-term public benefit. Their experiences offer direct and immediately relevant lessons for US reform, particularly for the transition from LPTA to best-value evaluation.

### 4.2 United Kingdom: Social Value and the Procurement Act 2023

#### The Scale of UK Procurement

The United Kingdom spends approximately GBP 300 billion annually on public procurement across central government, the National Health Service (NHS), local authorities, and other public bodies. The Crown Commercial Service (CCS), the government's central purchasing body, manages commercial agreements through which GBP 30.3 billion in goods and services were procured in fiscal year 2022-23, generating reported savings of GBP 4.9 billion—a savings rate of 10.49 percent (CCS, 2023). These figures position the UK as both one of the largest and most sophisticated procurement markets in the world.

#### The Social Value Act and Its Impact

The Public Services (Social Value) Act 2012 was a landmark piece of legislation that required public authorities to consider, before starting a procurement process, how the proposed procurement might improve the economic, social, and environmental well-being of the relevant area. While the Act initially applied only to services contracts above the EU threshold, its scope and influence have expanded steadily.

In 2020, the government issued Policy Procurement Note PPN 06/20, which mandated that social value be evaluated as a minimum of 10 percent of the total score in all central government contracts. This effectively moved social value from a voluntary consideration to a mandatory evaluation criterion, fundamentally changing how contracts are awarded.

The results have been significant. According to the Cabinet Office, social value commitments in government contracts have generated over 25,000 new jobs and apprenticeships annually, increased spending with small and medium enterprises (SMEs), measurable environmental improvements including carbon reduction commitments, and community investment and volunteering commitments valued at hundreds of millions of pounds.

> **Social Value in Practice:** When the UK Ministry of Justice re-competed its facilities management contracts in 2021, social value criteria accounted for 20 percent of the total evaluation score. Winning bidders committed to hiring ex-offenders, providing apprenticeships in disadvantaged communities, and achieving net-zero carbon in facility operations. These commitments were embedded in the contract and subject to performance monitoring (UK Cabinet Office, 2022).

#### The Procurement Act 2023: "Most Advantageous Tender"

The Procurement Act 2023 represents the most significant reform of UK procurement law in a generation. Among its most important provisions is the replacement of the EU concept of "Most Economically Advantageous Tender" (MEAT) with "Most Advantageous Tender" (MAT). The change is more than semantic. By removing the word "economically," the Act signals that procurement evaluation should consider the full range of public value—social, environmental, and economic—not just economic efficiency narrowly defined.

Key features of the Procurement Act 2023 include a new central digital platform for publishing notices, tender documents, and contract performance data; replacement of rigid EU procedure types with more flexible options, including a new "competitive flexible procedure" that gives contracting authorities greater discretion; enhanced transparency obligations, including mandatory publication of pipeline notices, contract details, KPIs, and contract performance information; a centralized debarment list and clearer rules for excluding suppliers with serious integrity concerns; and requirements to consider SME access in procurement design, including lot splitting and payment provisions.

#### Lessons for the United States

The UK experience offers three primary lessons. First, social value can be systematically integrated into procurement evaluation without sacrificing efficiency—indeed, the CCS savings rate has *increased* alongside expanded social value requirements. Second, legislative reform matters: the Social Value Act and the Procurement Act 2023 provided the legal foundation for changes that voluntary guidance alone could not achieve. Third, the shift from "most economically advantageous" to "most advantageous" reflects a conceptual evolution that the US procurement community has yet to fully embrace.

### 4.3 Australia: Codifying Value for Money and Indigenous Procurement

#### The Commonwealth Procurement Framework

Australia's Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs) establish the framework for procurement by Australian Government entities. The CPRs are notable for their clarity and directness on the question of value for money. Rule 4.4 states explicitly: "Achieving value for money is the core rule of the CPRs. Price is not the sole factor. Officials must also consider, among other things, quality, fitness for purpose, the supplier's relevant experience, potential for environmental sustainability, and whole-of-life costs" (Department of Finance, 2024).

This codification matters because it provides legal authority and institutional backing for contracting officers to look beyond lowest price. Unlike the US FAR, which permits best-value tradeoff but does not mandate it, the Australian CPRs establish value for money as the "core rule" from which all other procurement decisions flow. Individual entities are responsible for their own procurement decisions within the CPR framework, but the Department of Finance provides guidance, coordinates government-wide initiatives, and manages AusTender—the central portal for publishing and searching procurement opportunities.

#### The Indigenous Procurement Policy: A Model for Inclusive Procurement

Australia's Indigenous Procurement Policy (IPP), introduced in 2015, is one of the world's most ambitious programs for using procurement to advance indigenous economic participation. The policy mandates that Australian Government entities actively seek opportunities to contract with indigenous-owned businesses and sets targets for both the number and value of contracts awarded.

The IPP operates through several mechanisms. Mandatory set-asides require that contracts in regions with significant indigenous populations or for delivery of services to indigenous communities must first be offered to indigenous businesses. Minimum indigenous participation requirements ensure major contracts include requirements for indigenous employment, subcontracting, or supply chain participation. Portfolio targets mean each government portfolio has minimum targets for the number and value of contracts awarded to indigenous businesses. And Indigenous Procurement Connect serves as a matching service that helps government buyers identify capable indigenous suppliers.

The results have been transformative. Since the IPP's introduction, over $12.9 billion in contract opportunities have been created for indigenous businesses through FY 2023-24. More than 83,500 contracts have been awarded to indigenous businesses. In FY 2023-24 alone, over 1,300 indigenous businesses received contracts valued at approximately $1.3 billion (NIAA, 2024). The number of registered indigenous suppliers has grown from fewer than 500 in 2015 to over 4,500.

> **From Policy to Practice:** The IPP's success rests on several design features that could inform US approaches to socioeconomic procurement programs. Target-setting at the portfolio level creates accountability for senior leaders. The mandatory set-aside mechanism ensures that indigenous businesses have genuine access to opportunities. And the Indigenous Procurement Connect service addresses the matching problem—connecting capable suppliers with government buyers who may not know they exist (NIAA, 2024).

#### Lessons for the United States

Australia's primary lessons for the US are twofold. First, codifying value for money as the "core rule"—rather than one option among several—sends an unambiguous signal to the workforce and the market. The US could achieve a similar effect by establishing best-value tradeoff as the default evaluation method in the FAR, requiring written justification for LPTA use. Second, the Indigenous Procurement Policy demonstrates that targeted socioeconomic procurement programs can achieve scale when backed by mandatory targets, clear accountability, and support infrastructure. The US SBA programs, while substantial, could benefit from the IPP's mandatory set-aside mechanism and portfolio-level accountability structure.

### 4.4 New Zealand: Public Value, Proportionality, and Maori Inclusion

#### The Public Value Framework

New Zealand's Government Procurement Rules establish a procurement framework built around five principles: getting the right supplier, getting the best deal for everyone, playing fair, being transparent, and being accountable. The "best deal for everyone" principle is particularly noteworthy, as it explicitly extends the concept of procurement value beyond the purchasing agency to encompass broader public benefits.

The government's Broader Outcomes policy, implemented through Rule 18 of the Government Procurement Rules, requires agencies to consider how procurement can contribute to four priority outcomes: increasing access for New Zealand businesses (including Maori and Pasifika businesses), increasing the size and skill level of the domestic construction workforce, improving conditions for workers (including payment of living wages), and demonstrating environmental responsibility (including transition to net-zero carbon). For contracts above NZD $100,000, agencies must identify relevant broader outcomes and plan how to achieve them through the procurement process.

#### The Proportionality Principle

One of New Zealand's most distinctive contributions to procurement policy is its emphasis on proportionality. The Government Procurement Rules explicitly state that the level of procurement process should be proportional to the value and risk of the procurement. This means that low-value, low-risk procurements should use simplified processes, while high-value, high-risk procurements warrant more rigorous approaches.

The proportionality principle is codified through a tiered system. Procurements under NZD $100,000 use simplified procurement, typically requiring at least three quotes but minimal formal process. Those between NZD $100,000 and $9 million for goods and services (or $10 million for construction) require standard open procurement with full transparency and broader outcome requirements. Those above threshold require full competitive process with comprehensive evaluation, including social and environmental criteria. This approach contrasts sharply with the US system, where the same FAR regulations and compliance requirements often apply regardless of contract size, creating disproportionate administrative burden for smaller, lower-risk purchases.

#### The Economic Benefit Principle and Maori Targets

New Zealand requires that agencies assess the economic benefit of procurement decisions, with a minimum standard that contracts deliver at least 10 percent economic benefit beyond direct procurement value—measured through employment creation, technology transfer, regional development, environmental sustainability, and Maori economic development.

New Zealand has set explicit targets for procurement with Maori-owned businesses, aiming for 8 percent of government procurement spend. As of the latest available data, the government had achieved approximately 6 percent—roughly NZD $930 million annually—progressing toward but not yet reaching the target (MBIE, 2024). The trajectory is positive, with over 3,200 contracts awarded to Maori businesses, and the commitment is sustained.

#### Lessons for the United States

New Zealand's proportionality principle is perhaps the single most transferable concept for US reform. The US system's one-size-fits-all regulatory approach—where a $50,000 IT services contract may face the same compliance requirements as a $500 million defense system—is a primary driver of inefficiency and small business exclusion. Adopting a proportionality framework would right-size procurement processes to the value and risk of each acquisition. Additionally, New Zealand's broader outcomes approach provides a model for systematically incorporating social and environmental considerations without the complexity of the UK's Social Value Act scoring methodology.

### 4.5 Canada: The Procurement Ombudsman and Institutional Reform

#### Current System and Challenges

Canada's federal procurement system is administered primarily by Public Services and Procurement Canada (PSPC), which manages approximately CAD $25 billion in annual procurement on behalf of federal departments and agencies. The system operates under the Government Contracts Regulations and related policies, with oversight from the Treasury Board Secretariat. Canada shares many of the challenges facing the US system: complex regulations, lengthy procurement timelines, workforce capacity constraints, and difficulty balancing competing policy objectives.

#### The Office of the Procurement Ombudsman

Canada's most distinctive institutional innovation is the Office of the Procurement Ombudsman (OPO), established in 2008. The OPO is an independent body mandated to review complaints from suppliers regarding the award of contracts below certain thresholds, review complaints regarding the administration of contracts regardless of value, review procurement practices across government to identify systemic issues, facilitate resolution of disputes between suppliers and government entities, and provide recommendations for improving procurement practices.

The OPO fills a gap that exists in many procurement systems—including the US—between formal protest mechanisms (which are adversarial, legalistic, and focused on individual contract decisions) and internal government oversight (which may lack independence). By providing an accessible, non-adversarial dispute resolution pathway, the OPO reduces the incentive for costly formal protests while ensuring that supplier concerns are heard and addressed.

> **The Ombudsman Advantage:** Canada's OPO reviewed over 350 procurement practices and handled over 1,200 contacts from suppliers in its latest reporting year. Importantly, the OPO's recommendations are public, creating a knowledge base of procurement best practices that benefits the entire system—not just the parties to a specific dispute (OPO, 2023).

#### Proposed Foundational Changes

In response to growing recognition that incremental reform is insufficient, Canada has proposed five foundational changes to its procurement system. First, establish a Federal Chief Procurement Officer (FCPO)—a single senior official with government-wide authority and accountability for procurement outcomes. Second, modernize the regulatory framework by simplifying and consolidating procurement regulations. Third, invest in digital infrastructure by building a modern, integrated procurement platform (CanadaBuys). Fourth, transform the procurement workforce through recruitment, training, and retention strategies. Fifth, strengthen indigenous procurement by expanding programs to increase procurement with indigenous-owned businesses.

#### Lessons for the United States

Canada's Procurement Ombudsman model addresses a structural weakness in the US system: the absence of an independent, non-adversarial pathway for resolving procurement disputes. The US protest system forces disputes into legalistic channels that are costly, slow, and relationship-damaging. An ombudsman function could absorb a significant volume of lower-stakes disputes, reducing GAO and COFC caseloads while providing better outcomes for both agencies and suppliers. Canada's proposed Federal Chief Procurement Officer also merits attention—the US equivalent would be a strengthened Office of Federal Procurement Policy (OFPP) with greater operational authority and accountability.

## Chapter 5: Competition and Anti-Corruption Innovators — Chile, Brazil, European Union

### 5.1 Introduction: Transparency as Competitive Advantage

Competition is the engine of procurement value. When more qualified vendors compete for government contracts, prices fall, quality rises, and innovation flourishes. Conversely, when competition is limited—whether by corruption, excessive barriers to entry, or opaque processes—procurement outcomes deteriorate along every dimension. The systems examined in this chapter have developed distinctive approaches to maximizing competition and combating corruption: Chile through its transparency observatory and open data infrastructure, Brazil through its reverse auction innovation, and the European Union through its supranational framework promoting sustainable and competitive procurement across 27 member states.

### 5.2 Chile: ChileCompra, the Observatory, and Open Contracting

#### System Overview

Chile's public procurement system, administered through ChileCompra (formally the Direccion de Compras y Contratacion Publica), is widely regarded as one of the most transparent and well-managed in Latin America. The system processes approximately $16 billion in annual procurement through its electronic platform, Mercado Publico, which serves as the single marketplace for all public procurement in Chile. Launched in 2003, it connects approximately 850 government entities with over 120,000 registered suppliers. All procurement opportunities, bid submissions, evaluation results, awards, and contracts are processed and published through the platform, creating a comprehensive digital record of public purchasing activity.

#### The Transparency Observatory

Chile's most distinctive contribution to global procurement practice is the Observatory of Public Purchasing (Observatorio de Compras Publicas), an advanced data analytics platform that monitors procurement activity across the entire government in real time. The Observatory analyzes procurement data to identify patterns, anomalies, and risks, including concentration risk (identifying cases where a disproportionate share of contracts flows to a small number of suppliers), price anomalies (detecting prices that deviate significantly from market benchmarks), competition indicators (monitoring bid participation rates and flagging tenders with unusually few bids), conflict of interest detection (cross-referencing procurement officials and supplier ownership data), and timeline anomalies (flagging procurements with unusually short bid submission periods).

The Observatory's impact has been substantial. Following comprehensive reforms in 2023 that strengthened the Observatory's capabilities and expanded mandatory disclosure requirements, Chile reported a 67 percent reduction in identified procurement conflicts—dropping from 18.7 to 6.1 cases per month (ChileCompra, 2024). This is a remarkable figure that demonstrates the power of systematic data analytics in preventing procurement misconduct.

#### Beneficial Ownership Transparency

Chile has been a leader in adopting beneficial ownership transparency requirements for public procurement. Under reforms implemented in 2023, companies bidding on government contracts must disclose their ultimate beneficial owners—the natural persons who ultimately own or control the entity. Within just three months of implementation, 59 percent of active suppliers had submitted beneficial ownership declarations—39,039 of approximately 66,000 companies, disclosing over 185,000 individual owners (ChileCompra, 2024).

Beneficial ownership transparency is critical for anti-corruption because it prevents the use of shell companies, nominees, and complex corporate structures to disguise conflicts of interest, sanctions evasion, and corrupt relationships. Chile's rapid implementation demonstrates that beneficial ownership requirements are technically feasible and can achieve high compliance rates quickly when backed by clear legal authority and integrated into the e-procurement platform.

#### SME Participation and Open Contracting

ChileCompra has achieved notable success in promoting small and medium enterprise participation in public procurement. SMEs account for approximately 42 percent of total procurement value—a figure that exceeds SME participation rates in most OECD countries and reflects deliberate policy choices including simplified processes for smaller purchases, framework agreements accessible to SMEs, and the Mercado Publico platform's low barriers to entry.

Chile was also one of the first countries to adopt the Open Contracting Data Standard (OCDS), an international standard for publishing structured, machine-readable procurement data. OCDS adoption enables civil society organizations, researchers, journalists, and the public to analyze procurement data systematically, creating an external accountability ecosystem that complements government oversight.

> **The Observatory Effect:** Chile's Observatory demonstrates a fundamental principle: you cannot manage what you do not measure, and you cannot prevent corruption you cannot detect. By creating a comprehensive, real-time analytical capability across all government procurement, Chile has moved from reactive investigation of individual corruption cases to proactive, systemic risk management (ChileCompra, 2024).

#### Lessons for the United States

Chile's Observatory model represents precisely the kind of proactive, data-driven procurement oversight that the United States lacks. While the US has extensive procurement data through FPDS and USAspending.gov, this data is primarily used for *retrospective* reporting rather than *real-time* risk detection. Creating a US Procurement Observatory—leveraging existing data infrastructure with advanced analytics—could identify competition problems, pricing anomalies, and conflict-of-interest risks before they result in waste or corruption. Additionally, Chile's rapid adoption of beneficial ownership transparency demonstrates the feasibility of a reform that the US has been debating for years.

### 5.3 Brazil: Pregao and the Reverse Auction Revolution

#### The Innovation

Brazil's most significant contribution to global procurement innovation is the *pregao* (electronic reverse auction), a procurement method that inverts the traditional sealed-bid process. In a pregao, the government publishes its requirements, vendors submit initial offers, and then a real-time reverse auction occurs in which vendors compete by lowering their prices. The critical innovation is that technical qualification is assessed *after* the price competition, not before—reversing the traditional sequence and eliminating the ability of unqualified vendors to manipulate the pre-qualification stage.

The pregao was introduced by federal law in 2000 (later codified as Law No. 10.520/2002) and has since become the default procurement method for goods and common services across all levels of Brazilian government. The method was further codified and expanded by Brazil's new procurement law (Law No. 14.133/2021), which established the pregao as one of the primary competition modes in the Brazilian procurement system.

#### How Pregao Works

The pregao process proceeds through several phases. First, the government entity publishes the procurement notice on ComprasNet (now being migrated to the Portal Nacional de Contratacoes Publicas, or PNCP), specifying requirements and evaluation criteria. Second, vendors submit sealed initial proposals. Third, the system opens a real-time reverse auction in which vendors can see competing prices (but not competitor identities in many implementations) and submit lower bids. The auction continues for a set period, with a random closing mechanism that prevents last-second sniping—the auction ends at a random time within a defined window, incentivizing vendors to submit their best prices early. Fourth, only after the price competition concludes does the government verify the winning vendor's technical and legal qualifications. If the winner fails qualification, the next-lowest bidder is evaluated. Fifth, the contract is awarded to the lowest-priced qualified vendor.

#### The Random Closing Mechanism

Brazil's random closing mechanism deserves special attention as a design innovation. In traditional timed auctions, bidders have an incentive to wait until the final seconds to submit their best bids ("sniping"), which limits the price discovery benefits of the auction process. By ending the auction at a random time within a defined window, the pregao eliminates this strategic behavior and encourages vendors to bid their true minimum prices throughout the auction period.

#### Measured Outcomes

The pregao has delivered impressive results. Average procurement time decreased from approximately 90+ days under traditional sealed bidding to approximately 17 days under pregao (TCU, 2020). In the first two years of implementation, the pregao generated estimated savings of approximately R$7.5 billion (roughly $1.5 billion in purchasing-power-adjusted terms) (Fernandes, 2005). The number of average bidders per procurement increased substantially, as the electronic platform and simplified process lowered barriers to participation. The electronic platform created complete, auditable records of all procurement activity. And by 2023, the pregao accounted for the vast majority of federal procurement transactions for goods and common services, with thousands of reverse auctions conducted monthly.

#### Challenges and Limitations

The pregao is not without limitations. Its qualification-after-price approach works well for commodity purchases and standardized services but is less suitable for complex acquisitions where technical capability is the primary differentiator. Brazil's 2021 procurement law addressed this by establishing multiple competition modes—including *concorrencia* (competitive procurement) and *dialogo competitivo* (competitive dialogue)—for different types of acquisitions. Additionally, Brazil continues to face challenges with procurement corruption at all levels of government, despite the pregao's transparency benefits. The reverse auction addresses price manipulation but does not fully address specification manipulation (where requirements are written to favor a specific vendor) or contract administration corruption.

#### Lessons for the United States

Brazil's pregao offers two primary lessons. First, reverse auctions are a powerful tool for commodity procurement that the US has adopted only partially. While the US permits reverse auctions under FAR Part 17, their use is far less systematic than in Brazil. Expanding reverse auction use for commodity and common service procurements—with appropriate safeguards—could generate significant savings. Second, Brazil's post-qualification approach (verifying capability after price competition) could streamline US procurement for lower-risk acquisitions, reducing the extensive pre-award documentation requirements that slow the process.

### 5.4 European Union: The Supranational Framework

#### Scale and Structure

The European Union's public procurement framework governs approximately 2 trillion euros in annual public spending across 27 member states—approximately 14 percent of the EU's combined GDP (European Commission, 2023). This makes the EU procurement framework the world's largest supranational procurement regime, setting common rules that affect hundreds of thousands of contracting authorities and millions of businesses.

The EU procurement framework is established primarily through three directives: Directive 2014/24/EU (Public Sector Directive) governing procurement by government entities; Directive 2014/25/EU (Utilities Directive) governing procurement by entities in water, energy, transport, and postal services; and Directive 2014/23/EU (Concessions Directive) governing the award of concession contracts. These directives set minimum standards that all member states must transpose into national law, while allowing considerable discretion in implementation.

#### MEAT: Most Economically Advantageous Tender

The EU's procurement directives establish the "Most Economically Advantageous Tender" (MEAT) as the standard evaluation criterion. Under MEAT, contracting authorities can evaluate tenders based on best price-quality ratio (weighing price against technical merit, aesthetic and functional characteristics, accessibility, social and environmental characteristics, innovation, and post-sale service), cost (assessed on the basis of cost-effectiveness, such as life-cycle costing), or price (lowest price may be used but is not the default or preferred approach).

The directives explicitly state that "Member States may provide that contracting authorities may not use price only or cost only as the sole award criterion or restrict their use to certain categories of contracting authorities or certain types of contracts" (Directive 2014/24/EU, Article 67). Several member states have exercised this option, effectively prohibiting lowest-price-only evaluation for certain categories.

#### Life-Cycle Costing

The EU directives introduced life-cycle costing (LCC) as a formal evaluation methodology, recognizing that the purchase price of a good or service often represents only a fraction of its total cost over its useful life. Under LCC, evaluators consider acquisition costs (purchase price, delivery, installation), utilization costs (energy consumption, maintenance, operating costs), end-of-life costs (decommissioning, disposal, recycling), and external environmental costs (where a methodology for monetization has been established). This approach directly addresses one of the fundamental weaknesses of LPTA evaluation: its focus on initial acquisition cost to the exclusion of total ownership cost.

#### Sustainable Procurement: The Lithuania Example

The EU's sustainable procurement agenda has driven remarkable transformations at the national level. Lithuania provides perhaps the most dramatic example. In the early 2010s, sustainable procurement criteria were included in fewer than 5 percent of Lithuanian public tenders. Through a combination of EU-supported capacity building, national legislation mandating green procurement criteria for specified product categories, and centralized guidance, Lithuania increased the share of tenders incorporating sustainable criteria to over 90 percent by 2023 (European Commission, 2023). This transformation demonstrates that sustainable procurement is not an inherent constraint on competition or value for money but a policy choice that can be implemented rapidly when political will and institutional support align.

#### Lessons for the United States

The EU framework offers three primary lessons. First, the MEAT standard demonstrates that quality-focused evaluation can be established as a supranational norm across 27 diverse countries—suggesting that the US could certainly mandate it across federal agencies. Second, life-cycle costing methodology provides a rigorous, defensible framework for looking beyond initial price—precisely the tool that US contracting officers need when justifying best-value awards against protest challenge. Third, Lithuania's sustainable procurement transformation shows that ambitious policy targets can be achieved rapidly with the right combination of mandates, guidance, and capacity building.

## Chapter 6: Innovation Procurement — Denmark, the Nordics, and the EU Framework

### 6.1 Introduction: Buying What Does Not Yet Exist

Traditional procurement assumes that the government knows what it wants to buy and the market can supply it. The government writes specifications, vendors respond with offers, and the best offer wins. This model works well for established goods and services—when the government needs office furniture, fleet vehicles, or janitorial services—but breaks down when the government needs something that does not yet exist. Climate solutions, healthcare innovations, advanced cybersecurity tools, and smart city technologies often require research and development that no vendor has yet undertaken. Traditional procurement cannot access these solutions because they are not yet on the market.

Innovation procurement—the use of public purchasing power to stimulate the development of novel solutions—addresses this gap. It encompasses several distinct mechanisms, from pre-commercial procurement (PCP), which funds R&D phases before a solution reaches the market, to innovation partnerships that combine R&D and subsequent commercial procurement in a single procedure. The countries and institutions examined in this chapter—Denmark, the broader Nordic region, and the EU framework—have been global pioneers in developing and scaling these approaches.

### 6.2 Denmark and the Nordic Model

#### Denmark's Innovation Procurement Ecosystem

Denmark spends approximately 35 billion euros annually on public procurement, representing roughly 15 percent of GDP—one of the highest proportions in the OECD. The Danish government has made a strategic decision to use this spending not just to procure existing goods and services but to drive innovation in areas of national priority.

Denmark's approach to innovation procurement operates through several channels. Pre-Commercial Procurement (PCP) programs, active since 2014, fund multiple vendors through progressive R&D phases—concept, prototype, pilot—before selecting solutions for full commercial procurement. Challenge-based procurement identifies problems and invites innovative solutions rather than specifying technical requirements. The GovTech program opens government procurement to startups through accelerator-style programs, mentoring, and simplified access to procurement opportunities. And public-private innovation partnerships create structured frameworks for government and industry to co-develop solutions.

#### Case Study: Hospital Robotics PCP

One of Denmark's most successful PCP programs focused on infection control in hospitals. Rather than specifying what technology hospitals should use, the Danish government defined the problem—healthcare-acquired infections that cost the healthcare system billions and endanger patients—and invited innovative solutions.

The PCP proceeded through three phases. In the concept phase, multiple vendors were funded to develop initial concepts for autonomous infection control solutions. In the prototype phase, the most promising concepts received additional funding to develop working prototypes. In the pilot phase, selected prototypes were tested in operational hospital environments. The result was Blue Ocean Robotics, a company that developed an autonomous UV disinfection robot that has since achieved commercial viability and been deployed in hospitals across Denmark and internationally. The PCP not only solved the original problem but created a commercially successful product and a thriving company—demonstrating how innovation procurement can generate both public value and economic development.

#### Case Study: Climate Solutions PCP

Denmark's climate-focused PCP program addressed the challenge of urban flooding through sustainable infrastructure. Rather than procuring traditional drainage systems, the government sought innovative approaches to managing stormwater in urban environments. The program resulted in the development and deployment of permeable asphalt technology in Copenhagen, which absorbs rainwater rather than channeling it into overwhelmed sewer systems. This solution simultaneously addresses flooding, reduces combined sewer overflow pollution, and contributes to urban heat island mitigation.

#### The GovTech Startup Program

Denmark's GovTech program recognizes that startups often possess the most innovative solutions but face the greatest barriers to government procurement. The program addresses this through challenge definition sessions where government agencies define problems that startups might solve, a simplified application process that does not require the extensive documentation typical of government procurement, mentoring and technical support to help startups navigate government procurement requirements, and pilot contracts that provide startups with revenue and references while giving government agencies access to cutting-edge solutions.

#### The Broader Nordic Innovation Procurement Landscape

Denmark's approach is embedded within a broader Nordic innovation procurement ecosystem. Across the five Nordic countries (Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Sweden), combined public procurement exceeds 100 billion euros annually. The Nordic Council of Ministers has established shared frameworks for innovation procurement, and the Nordic Innovation organization facilitates cross-border cooperation.

Key success factors identified in Nordic innovation procurement research (Nordregio, 2023) include clear problem definition where the government articulates the challenge without prescribing the solution; commercial potential assessment ensuring that developed solutions have market potential beyond the initial government application; trust and communication between government buyers and innovative suppliers; procurement rule navigation, as Nordic practitioners report that existing procurement rules are more flexible than commonly believed; internal champions—senior leaders who advocate for innovation procurement within government organizations; and cross-cultural understanding between government officials and startup/innovation communities.

### 6.3 The EU Innovation Partnerships Framework

The European Union's 2014 procurement directives introduced a formal innovation partnership procedure type, specifically designed for situations where the government's needs cannot be met by existing solutions. The innovation partnership allows a contracting authority to combine R&D and subsequent commercial procurement in a single procedure—eliminating the need for a separate procurement after the innovation phase and providing vendors with commercial certainty.

The innovation partnership proceeds through several stages. The contracting authority publishes a call for competition describing its needs in functional or performance terms. Interested vendors submit applications, and the contracting authority selects partners based on their capacity for research and innovation. Partners develop solutions through progressive R&D phases, with the authority able to reduce the number of partners at each stage. Upon successful completion of R&D, the authority can purchase the resulting product or service from the innovation partner(s) without conducting a new procurement procedure—provided the agreed performance levels and maximum costs are respected.

This framework addresses a fundamental problem in innovation procurement: the "valley of death" between R&D and commercial deployment. Under traditional procurement, a vendor that develops a solution through a government-funded R&D program must then compete in a separate commercial procurement to actually sell that solution to the government—with no guarantee of success. This uncertainty deters investment in R&D. The innovation partnership eliminates this barrier by providing a pathway from development to deployment.

#### EU Pre-Commercial Procurement: Lessons from Scale

The EU has also promoted pre-commercial procurement through dedicated funding programs and guidance. The European Commission's PCP program has funded dozens of PCP projects across member states, covering areas from healthcare to defense to smart cities to environmental sustainability.

Key lessons from EU PCP experience include the importance of demand aggregation, where multiple public authorities jointly define challenges and share the costs and benefits of PCP programs. Risk sharing between public and private sectors is critical, as PCP distributes development risk across multiple funded vendors while maintaining competitive pressure. Intellectual property arrangements must balance vendor incentives to innovate with government access to results. And market creation is possible because successful PCP programs do not just solve a single government problem—they create new markets for innovative products and services.

### 6.4 How Innovation Procurement Differs from Traditional Procurement

Understanding innovation procurement requires recognizing how fundamentally it differs from traditional approaches along several dimensions.

|---|---|---|
| What is purchased | Existing products/services | New solutions (may not yet exist) |
| Specifications | Detailed technical requirements | Functional/outcome-based needs |
| Buyer-supplier relationship | Arm's length, adversarial | Collaborative, partnership-based |
| Risk allocation | Transferred to vendor | Shared between parties |
| Timeline | Months (procurement cycle) | Years (R&D + deployment) |
| Competition model | Price/quality at point of award | Quality of innovation over time |
| Success metric | Compliance with specifications | Problem solved, outcomes achieved |

This comparison illuminates why innovation procurement requires different skills, different procedures, and different institutional support than traditional procurement. Contracting officers trained in compliance-focused, specification-driven procurement may struggle with the ambiguity, risk-sharing, and collaborative relationships that innovation procurement demands. Countries that have succeeded in innovation procurement have invested in specialized training, created dedicated innovation procurement units, and developed procurement-specific guidance for managing R&D relationships.

### 6.5 Innovation Procurement and the United States

The United States has existing mechanisms that overlap with innovation procurement—most notably the Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) program, Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) program, and Other Transaction Authorities (OTAs). However, these mechanisms exist largely outside the mainstream FAR-based procurement system and are not well integrated with regular acquisition processes.

SBIR/STTR provide funding for small business R&D but do not guarantee subsequent commercial procurement—the "valley of death" problem that EU innovation partnerships specifically address. OTAs provide flexibility for prototype development but have been criticized for inconsistent implementation and limited competition. Neither mechanism is available to all agencies on the same terms, and both require specialized authority and expertise that many contracting offices lack.

A formal innovation procurement pathway in the FAR—modeled on the EU innovation partnership procedure—could provide a standardized, accessible mechanism for all agencies to procure innovative solutions. Combined with challenge-based procurement approaches inspired by Denmark's GovTech program, this would significantly expand the federal government's ability to leverage its purchasing power for innovation.

> **The Innovation Gap:** The EU spent an estimated 13.4 billion euros on innovation procurement in 2020, representing 3.3 percent of total public procurement. The US lacks comparable aggregate data on innovation procurement spending, reflecting the fragmented nature of its innovation procurement mechanisms and the absence of a unified framework (European Commission, 2021).

### 6.6 Lessons for the United States

Innovation procurement offers the US an opportunity to address persistent challenges in areas where traditional procurement has failed. Healthcare, cybersecurity, climate adaptation, infrastructure modernization, and government IT are all domains where the government needs solutions that do not yet exist at scale. The lessons from Denmark and the EU are clear. First, define problems, not solutions—challenge-based procurement consistently produces better outcomes than specification-based procurement in innovation contexts. Second, share risk—vendors will not invest in R&D without confidence that successful solutions will find a market. Third, provide a pathway from development to deployment—the EU innovation partnership model specifically addresses the valley of death that kills promising innovations. Fourth, create specialized capacity—innovation procurement requires different skills and institutional support than traditional procurement. Fifth, open procurement to startups—Denmark's GovTech program demonstrates that small firms often have the best ideas but the biggest barriers to government contracting.

## Chapter 7: Cross-Cutting Success Patterns

### 7.1 Introduction: What Successful Systems Have in Common

The twelve procurement systems examined in this report span four continents, multiple legal traditions, and vastly different economic and institutional contexts. South Korea's economy is fifty times the size of Georgia's. Estonia's population is smaller than that of many US cities. The UK and Australia share a common legal heritage; Brazil and Chile do not. Yet despite these differences, remarkably consistent patterns emerge across the most successful systems. This chapter identifies and analyzes ten cross-cutting success patterns that collectively define what effective public procurement looks like in the twenty-first century.

### 7.2 The Ten Patterns

#### Pattern 1: Single Unified Digital Platform

The most consistent feature of high-performing procurement systems is a single digital platform that serves as the authoritative venue for all public procurement activity. South Korea (KONEPS), Singapore (GeBIZ), Chile (Mercado Publico), Brazil (ComprasNet/PNCP), Georgia (Ge-GP), and Estonia (through X-Road integration) all operate on this model. The benefits are multiplicative: vendors register once and access all opportunities; data flows seamlessly from solicitation through payment; analytics can span the entire procurement ecosystem; and transparency is comprehensive rather than partial.

The United States, by contrast, operates a fragmented ecosystem of disconnected systems (SAM.gov, FPDS, USAspending.gov, CPARS, eSRS, and numerous agency portals) that impose duplicative requirements on vendors and prevent comprehensive analytics. This fragmentation is perhaps the single largest structural weakness of the US system.

#### Pattern 2: Explicit "Not Lowest Price" Default

Every value-for-money leader examined in this report has taken the affirmative step of codifying that procurement evaluation should consider factors beyond price. Australia's CPRs declare value for money the "core rule" and state "price is not the sole factor." Singapore's IM on Procurement mandates "value-for-money, not lowest quote." The UK's Procurement Act 2023 establishes "Most Advantageous Tender" as the standard. New Zealand requires "best deal for everyone." The EU's MEAT standard mandates best price-quality ratio consideration.

In these systems, using lowest-price-only evaluation requires justification. In the US system, the reverse is often true: using best-value tradeoff may require more justification than defaulting to LPTA. Reversing this default—making value-for-money the standard and LPTA the exception requiring justification—would be among the most impactful single reforms the US could adopt.

#### Pattern 3: Transparency by Design

High-performing systems treat transparency not as a compliance burden but as a design principle. Georgia publishes all bids, evaluations, and contracts in real time. Chile's Observatory monitors procurement activity across all government entities with advanced analytics. Estonia's X-Road makes data available to authorized parties automatically. South Korea's KONEPS provides comprehensive audit trails for every transaction.

These systems demonstrate that transparency serves multiple functions simultaneously: it deters corruption (because corrupt acts are visible), it enables competition (because vendors can identify opportunities and understand evaluation criteria), it facilitates oversight (because auditors and civil society have access to comprehensive data), and it supports learning (because agencies can benchmark their practices against peers).

#### Pattern 4: Social Value Integration

A clear trend across the most advanced procurement systems is the explicit integration of social and environmental value into procurement evaluation. The UK's mandatory 10 percent social value weighting, Australia's Indigenous Procurement Policy, New Zealand's broader outcomes requirements, and the EU's sustainable procurement framework all represent different approaches to the same objective: ensuring that public procurement generates value beyond the immediate goods and services purchased.

The US has pursued social objectives primarily through set-aside programs (SBA small business programs, 8(a), HUBZone) rather than through evaluation criteria. While these programs have achieved significant results (28.4 percent of contracts to small businesses), they operate as parallel tracks rather than integrated evaluation dimensions. The international trend toward embedding social value within mainstream evaluation criteria suggests a more holistic approach is possible and effective.

#### Pattern 5: Independent Oversight Mechanisms

Effective procurement systems invest in independent oversight that goes beyond traditional audit functions. Canada's Procurement Ombudsman provides non-adversarial dispute resolution and systemic practice review. Chile's Observatory provides real-time data-driven monitoring. The UK's CCS provides centralized commercial expertise and performance tracking. Singapore's CPIB provides robust anti-corruption enforcement.

The US relies primarily on GAO bid protests and inspector general investigations—both of which are reactive, adversarial, and focused on individual transactions rather than systemic improvement. Adding an ombudsman function (modeled on Canada), an observatory function (modeled on Chile), or both would significantly strengthen the US oversight ecosystem.

#### Pattern 6: Speed and Simplification

Speed is not just about convenience—it has substantive consequences. Lengthy procurement timelines delay the delivery of public services, increase costs (through both administrative expense and opportunity cost), and deter vendors who cannot afford to wait months or years for contract award. The most improved systems have achieved dramatic speed gains: Brazil's pregao reduced average procurement time from 90+ days to 17 days. South Korea's KONEPS cut bidding time from 30 hours to under 2 hours. Georgia built its entire e-procurement system in 10 months. New Zealand's proportionality principle ensures that simple procurements use simple processes.

The US federal procurement system is among the slowest in the developed world. Achieving meaningful speed improvement requires both digital infrastructure (to eliminate manual bottlenecks) and regulatory simplification (to reduce unnecessary process requirements for lower-risk acquisitions).

#### Pattern 7: Innovation Pathways

Countries at the frontier of procurement practice have developed specific mechanisms for purchasing innovation. Denmark's pre-commercial procurement programs have produced commercially viable products. The EU's innovation partnership procedure provides a pathway from R&D to commercial deployment. Nordic GovTech programs open procurement to startups through simplified access and challenge-based solicitations.

The US has innovation mechanisms (SBIR, STTR, OTAs) but they exist largely outside the FAR-based procurement mainstream. Creating a formal innovation procurement pathway within the FAR would normalize innovation procurement and make it accessible to all agencies.

#### Pattern 8: Data-Driven Monitoring

The most advanced systems use procurement data not just for reporting but for active, real-time monitoring and decision-making. Chile's Observatory uses data analytics to detect conflicts of interest, pricing anomalies, and competition problems. South Korea's KONEPS employs AI for bid rigging detection and demand forecasting. Estonia's X-Road enables real-time data sharing across government agencies.

The US has among the world's richest procurement data repositories (FPDS, USAspending.gov) but uses them primarily for retrospective reporting. Deploying advanced analytics against this data—as Chile and Korea have done—would transform existing data assets into active oversight and management tools.

#### Pattern 9: Workforce Investment

Every high-performing system invests in its procurement workforce. Singapore's comprehensive training and rotation system, the UK's commercial function professionalization program, and Canada's proposed workforce transformation all reflect recognition that procurement outcomes depend fundamentally on the skills, judgment, and professionalism of the people making procurement decisions.

The US has a large procurement workforce (190,000+) but faces chronic challenges in attracting, training, and retaining talent. The ratio of contract dollars managed per contracting officer has increased dramatically, and critical skills (data analytics, market intelligence, negotiation) are in short supply. Addressing workforce capacity is not a secondary reform—it is a precondition for every other reform succeeding.

#### Pattern 10: Knowledge Sharing and International Learning

Finally, the most successful systems actively share knowledge and learn from international experience. South Korea has exported KONEPS to multiple countries. Estonia's X-Road operates in 20+ countries. Chile promotes OCDS adoption globally. The Nordic countries share innovation procurement frameworks. The EU facilitates cross-border learning through shared directives and benchmarking.

The US has been comparatively insular in its approach to procurement reform, with limited systematic engagement with international best practices. This report itself represents an effort to bridge that gap, but sustained institutional mechanisms for international learning—participation in OECD procurement networks, systematic study of foreign reforms, exchange programs for procurement professionals—would accelerate US reform.

### 7.4 Key Insights from the Comparison

Several insights emerge from this comprehensive comparison. First, no single country excels across all dimensions. These different pathways suggest that the US need not follow a single reform sequence but can pursue multiple reforms simultaneously at different speeds.

## Chapter 8: The Model — A Seven-Pillar Framework for US Reform

### 8.1 Introduction: From Analysis to Action

The preceding chapters have documented twelve procurement systems that collectively offer a rich menu of proven reforms. This chapter synthesizes these insights into a coherent "Model Procurement System" for the United States—a seven-pillar framework that combines the best elements from around the world while accounting for the distinctive features of the American institutional context.

The model is designed to be modular: each pillar can be pursued independently, though the greatest benefits come from pursuing them together. It is also designed to be incremental: each pillar can be implemented in phases, with early wins building momentum and institutional capacity for more ambitious reforms. And it is designed to be evidence-based: every element traces directly to a documented reform in an operational system with measured results.

### 8.2 Pillar 1: Unified Digital Platform (Korea + Estonia)

#### The Vision: USProcure

The first and most fundamental pillar is the creation of a unified digital procurement platform—"USProcure"—that consolidates the fragmented ecosystem of SAM.gov, FPDS, USAspending.gov, CPARS, eSRS, and agency-specific portals into a coherent, interoperable whole.

This does not necessarily mean building a single monolithic system (which would be both technically risky and politically impractical). Instead, following Estonia's X-Road model, the approach would be to build an interoperability layer that enables seamless data exchange between existing systems while progressively migrating functionality to shared services. Following Korea's KONEPS model, the platform would implement the once-only principle: vendors register once, submit information once, and that information is shared across all government entities that need it.

Key features would include single registration and credentialing for vendors, with automatic verification against government databases (business registration, tax compliance, debarment status, beneficial ownership); a unified opportunity portal where all federal procurement opportunities are published, searchable, and filterable; integrated bid submission allowing vendors to respond to solicitations from any agency through a single interface; end-to-end contract lifecycle management from solicitation through award, administration, payment, and closeout; real-time data integration enabling analytics across the entire procurement ecosystem; and API-based architecture enabling third-party tools, market research platforms, and analytics services to integrate with the platform.

The estimated investment would be substantial—likely in the billions over five to seven years—but the return would be far greater. Korea's KONEPS generates $8 billion in annual savings, and Estonia's digital government infrastructure saves 2 percent of GDP. Even achieving a fraction of these returns would more than justify the investment.

### 8.3 Pillar 2: Value-Based Evaluation Reform (Singapore + Australia)

#### The Reform: Value for Money as Default

The second pillar reverses the current default. Instead of LPTA being the path of least resistance (and best-value tradeoff requiring additional justification), value-for-money evaluation becomes the standard, and LPTA requires written justification demonstrating that quality differences among offerors are minimal and that minimum requirements can be clearly defined.

This mirrors Australia's approach, where value for money is the "core rule" of the Commonwealth Procurement Rules, and Singapore's structured evaluation methodology, which uses the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) or similar multi-criteria decision analysis tools to systematically weigh price against quality factors.

Specific reforms would include amending FAR Part 15 to establish best-value tradeoff as the default evaluation methodology for competitive procurements above the simplified acquisition threshold; requiring written determination by the contracting officer (approved at one level above) for any use of LPTA, documenting that quality differences among offerors are expected to be minimal; developing standardized evaluation frameworks (modeled on Singapore's AHP approach) that provide structure and rigor to best-value evaluations while reducing the subjectivity that fuels protests; mandating life-cycle costing (modeled on the EU approach) for specified categories where operating, maintenance, and disposal costs are significant; and strengthening CPARS implementation to ensure that past performance data is comprehensive, reliable, and consistently available for evaluation.

### 8.4 Pillar 3: Social Value and Public Benefit (UK + New Zealand)

#### The Reform: Mandatory Public Value Weighting

The third pillar integrates social and environmental value into mainstream procurement evaluation, drawing on the UK's mandatory 10 percent social value weighting and New Zealand's broader outcomes requirements.

The proposed approach would establish a mandatory minimum weighting of 5 to 10 percent for public value criteria in all competitive procurements above $250,000. Eligible public value criteria would include workforce development (job creation, apprenticeships, hiring from disadvantaged communities), environmental sustainability (carbon reduction, resource efficiency, circular economy), community benefit (local economic development, supplier diversity, social enterprise support), and innovation outcomes (technology development, knowledge transfer, capability building).

Agencies would select the most relevant criteria for each procurement, and offerors would submit plans demonstrating how they would deliver public value. These plans would be evaluated, scored, and incorporated into the contract as binding performance requirements—not aspirational statements.

The UK's experience demonstrates that this can be done without sacrificing efficiency. CCS achieved a 10.49 percent savings rate in 2022-23—even as social value requirements expanded. The key is treating social value not as an add-on burden but as a dimension of value that is evaluated and managed alongside traditional performance measures.

### 8.5 Pillar 4: Real-Time Monitoring Observatory (Chile + Korea)

#### The Reform: Federal Procurement Observatory

The fourth pillar creates a data-driven oversight capability modeled on Chile's Observatory of Public Purchasing and Korea's AI-enhanced analytics. The Federal Procurement Observatory would be an independent analytical unit—potentially housed within GAO, GSA, or a new independent body—that monitors federal procurement activity in real time using advanced analytics.

Core functions would include competition monitoring (tracking bid participation rates, single-bid procurement rates, and vendor concentration across agencies and categories), price analytics (benchmarking prices against market data and historical patterns, flagging anomalies for review), conflict of interest detection (cross-referencing procurement officials and vendor ownership data to identify potential conflicts), performance tracking (monitoring contract execution against cost, schedule, and quality baselines), pattern recognition (using machine learning to identify systemic issues, emerging risks, and improvement opportunities), and public reporting (publishing regular reports on procurement health metrics, accessible to policymakers, media, and civil society).

Chile's Observatory contributed to a 67 percent reduction in procurement conflicts. Korea's AI analytics have improved bid rigging detection and demand forecasting. Applying similar capabilities to the US federal procurement ecosystem—which already possesses world-class data assets in FPDS and USAspending.gov—could generate transformative oversight improvements.

### 8.6 Pillar 5: Independent Oversight Through a Federal Procurement Ombudsman (Canada)

#### The Reform: Creating an Ombudsman Function

The fifth pillar establishes a Federal Procurement Ombudsman, modeled on Canada's Office of the Procurement Ombudsman, to complement the existing GAO bid protest function. The Ombudsman would serve as an independent, non-adversarial pathway for resolving procurement disputes, reviewing systemic issues, and improving procurement practices.

The Ombudsman's mandate would include complaint review (investigating supplier complaints about contract award and administration, with authority to make non-binding recommendations), systemic review (examining procurement practices across agencies to identify systemic issues and best practices), dispute resolution (mediating disputes between agencies and suppliers as an alternative to formal protests), practice improvement (issuing public guidance and recommendations based on complaint and review findings), and annual reporting (publishing an annual report on procurement health, complaint trends, and systemic issues).

This function would absorb a significant volume of disputes that currently flow to GAO and COFC, reducing the caseload and adversarial dynamics of formal protest channels while providing faster, less costly, and more constructive resolution for both agencies and suppliers. The Ombudsman would not replace GAO protests—the formal protest mechanism would remain for significant procedural and legal challenges—but would provide an alternative for the many disputes that stem from miscommunication, unclear criteria, or administrative error rather than fundamental legal violations.

### 8.7 Pillar 6: Speed and Simplification (Georgia + Brazil + New Zealand)

#### The Reform: Proportionality and Streamlined Processes

The sixth pillar addresses the US system's chronic speed problem through three complementary reforms: a proportionality framework (inspired by New Zealand), expanded reverse auctions (inspired by Brazil), and target timelines (inspired by Georgia's rapid buildout culture).

**Proportionality Framework.** Adopt a tiered regulatory framework in which the level of process is proportional to the value and risk of the procurement. Procurements under $250,000 would use streamlined procedures with minimal documentation requirements—three quotes, simplified evaluation, and expedited award authority. Procurements between $250,000 and $5 million would use standard competitive procedures with full evaluation but reduced administrative requirements relative to current practice. Procurements above $5 million would use comprehensive procedures with full FAR requirements, appropriate for high-value, high-risk acquisitions. This approach would reduce the regulatory burden on the approximately 80 percent of procurement actions that fall below $5 million while maintaining rigorous oversight for the approximately 20 percent that account for the majority of spending.

**Expanded Reverse Auctions.** Mandate the use of electronic reverse auctions (modeled on Brazil's pregao) for standardized goods and common commercial services where price is the primary differentiator. This would include office supplies, IT commodities, fleet vehicles, basic maintenance services, and other categories where quality differences among qualified vendors are minimal. The random closing mechanism (from Brazil) would be incorporated to prevent sniping and encourage genuine price competition.

**Target Timelines.** Establish target timelines for procurement completion: 30 days for simplified procurements under $250,000; 90 days for standard competitive procurements; and 180 days for complex, high-value procurements. These targets would not be legally binding (which could compromise evaluation quality) but would create accountability and visibility for procurement speed. Agencies exceeding target timelines would be required to report reasons to the Observatory.

### 8.8 Pillar 7: Innovation Procurement Pathways (Denmark + EU)

#### The Reform: FAR Innovation Procedure and GovTech Program

The seventh pillar creates formal pathways for innovation procurement within the FAR-based system. Two specific mechanisms are proposed.

**FAR Innovation Partnership Procedure.** Modeled on the EU's innovation partnership, this would be a new FAR procedure type that allows agencies to combine R&D and subsequent commercial procurement in a single process. Agencies would define functional requirements (the problem to be solved), select innovation partners based on R&D capability, fund progressive development phases with competitive down-selection, and procure the resulting solution without a separate competitive procurement—provided performance standards are met. This eliminates the "valley of death" between R&D and commercial deployment that currently limits the impact of SBIR, STTR, and other innovation mechanisms.

**Federal GovTech Program.** Modeled on Denmark's GovTech program, this would create a structured pathway for startups and small innovative firms to access federal procurement. Components would include challenge-based solicitations (where agencies define problems and invite innovative solutions), simplified application processes (reducing documentation requirements for early-stage engagement), mentoring and technical support (helping innovative firms navigate federal procurement requirements), and pilot contracts (providing revenue, references, and operational feedback). The program would be administered by GSA in coordination with SBA, with dedicated funding and a mandate to serve all civilian agencies.

## Chapter 9: Recommendations — Quick Wins, Medium-Term Reforms, and Long-Term Transformation

### 9.1 Introduction: From Vision to Specifics

Chapter 8 presented the integrated model. This chapter distills fifteen specific, actionable recommendations organized by implementation timeline. Each recommendation identifies the reform, its international evidence base, the implementation mechanism, and the expected impact. The recommendations are designed to be individually valuable while collectively comprising a comprehensive reform agenda.

### 9.2 Quick Wins (Year 1)

These recommendations can be implemented through executive action—executive orders, OMB memoranda, and agency directives—within the first year. They require no new legislation and can generate immediate, visible results.

#### Recommendation 1: Establish Value-for-Money as the Default Evaluation Standard

**Reform:** Issue an executive order establishing best-value tradeoff as the default evaluation methodology for competitive procurements above $250,000. LPTA would remain available but would require a written determination by the contracting officer—approved by a level above—documenting that quality differences among offerors are expected to be minimal and that minimum requirements can be clearly and completely defined.

**Evidence:** Australia's Commonwealth Procurement Rules establish value for money as the "core rule" and explicitly state that price is not the sole factor. Singapore's procurement framework mandates "value-for-money, not lowest quote." The UK's Procurement Act 2023 establishes "Most Advantageous Tender" as the evaluation standard. A 2017 RAND study found that LPTA overuse in DoD correlated with declining contractor performance and increased cost growth. The 2019 Professional Services Council analysis found 18 percent higher cost growth in LPTA contracts.

**Mechanism:** Executive order directing OMB/OFPP to issue government-wide guidance. Amend FAR through the standard rulemaking process.

**Expected Impact:** Improved contract outcomes for complex services and IT. Reduced lifecycle costs. Stronger incentives for quality and innovation. Estimated 5-10 percent improvement in value-for-money outcomes for affected contract categories.

#### Recommendation 2: Launch a Federal Procurement Observatory Pilot

**Reform:** Establish a pilot Procurement Observatory serving five major agencies (DoD, HHS, DHS, VA, GSA), using advanced analytics on existing FPDS and USAspending.gov data to monitor competition, pricing, conflicts of interest, and performance in real time.

**Evidence:** Chile's Observatory contributed to a 67 percent reduction in procurement conflicts after 2023 reforms. South Korea's AI analytics have improved bid rigging detection and demand forecasting. The US already possesses world-class procurement data assets—the Observatory would deploy analytics against this existing data.

**Mechanism:** GSA or GAO pilot program with dedicated funding and an interagency steering committee. Leverage existing data infrastructure; invest in analytics tools and staff.

**Expected Impact:** Early detection of competition problems, pricing anomalies, and conflict-of-interest risks. Estimated 3-5 percent improvement in procurement outcomes for pilot agencies. Foundation for government-wide expansion.

#### Recommendation 3: Implement Beneficial Ownership Disclosure for Major Contracts

**Reform:** Require disclosure of ultimate beneficial owners for all companies receiving contracts above $500,000. Integrate disclosure requirements into SAM.gov registration and verify against available databases.

**Evidence:** Chile achieved 59 percent compliance with beneficial ownership requirements within three months of implementation, covering 39,039 companies and 185,000+ individual owners. Chile's subsequent 67 percent reduction in procurement conflicts was partly attributed to beneficial ownership transparency. The US Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) has already established beneficial ownership reporting requirements under the Corporate Transparency Act—procurement disclosure would leverage this existing infrastructure.

**Mechanism:** OMB/OFPP guidance requiring beneficial ownership disclosure as a condition of SAM.gov registration for entities receiving contracts above $500,000. Integration with FinCEN's beneficial ownership database.

**Expected Impact:** Reduced ability to use shell companies for conflict-of-interest concealment, sanctions evasion, and corrupt relationships. Enhanced fraud detection capability for the Observatory.

#### Recommendation 4: Expand Reverse Auction Mandates for Commodity Procurement

**Reform:** Mandate the use of electronic reverse auctions for standardized goods and common commercial services in specified product categories where price is the primary differentiator. Incorporate Brazil's random closing mechanism to prevent bid sniping.

**Evidence:** Brazil's pregao reduced average procurement time from 90+ days to 17 days and generated savings of approximately R$7.5 billion in its first two years. The random closing mechanism eliminates strategic sniping behavior and improves price discovery. GSA already operates reverse auction capabilities through GSA Advantage and FAS programs.

**Mechanism:** OMB/OFPP guidance directing agencies to use reverse auctions for specified commodity categories. GSA to provide government-wide reverse auction platform services.

**Expected Impact:** 15-25 percent price reductions on commodity purchases. 50-70 percent reduction in procurement cycle time for affected categories. Increased competition through lower barriers to entry.

#### Recommendation 5: Conduct Comprehensive Workforce Assessment

**Reform:** Conduct a government-wide assessment of federal acquisition workforce capacity, identifying critical skill gaps, staffing ratios, training needs, and retention challenges. Develop a five-year workforce investment plan.

**Evidence:** Every high-performing procurement system invests in its workforce. Singapore's comprehensive training and rotation system, the UK's commercial function professionalization program, and Canada's proposed workforce transformation all demonstrate that procurement outcomes depend on workforce quality. The US acquisition workforce faces an average age over 45, increasing contract dollar-to-officer ratios, and chronic shortages in data analytics, negotiation, and market intelligence skills.

**Mechanism:** OMB/OFPP directive to all Chief Acquisition Officers. Leveraged through the Federal Acquisition Workforce Assessment already required by law but inconsistently implemented.

**Expected Impact:** Baseline data for workforce investment decisions. Identification of most critical gaps. Foundation for recruitment, training, and retention strategies.

### 9.3 Medium-Term Reforms (Years 2-3)

These recommendations require legislative action, regulatory amendment, or significant system development. They build on Year 1 foundations and represent the structural reforms necessary for lasting transformation.

#### Recommendation 6: Establish a Federal Procurement Ombudsman

**Reform:** Create an independent Federal Procurement Ombudsman, modeled on Canada's Office of the Procurement Ombudsman, with authority to review supplier complaints, investigate procurement practices, mediate disputes, and publish recommendations.

**Evidence:** Canada's OPO has handled thousands of supplier contacts and reviewed hundreds of procurement practices since its establishment in 2008. The OPO's non-adversarial approach has reduced reliance on costly formal dispute mechanisms while improving procurement practices through public recommendations. The US bid protest system generates approximately 2,500 GAO cases annually, many of which stem from communication failures or administrative errors that an ombudsman could resolve more efficiently.

**Mechanism:** Legislation establishing the Federal Procurement Ombudsman as an independent office. Appropriations for staff, facilities, and operations. Coordination protocol with GAO and COFC.

**Expected Impact:** 20-30 percent reduction in formal GAO protests (as lower-stakes disputes are channeled to the Ombudsman). Faster, less costly dispute resolution. Systemic practice improvement through public recommendations.

#### Recommendation 7: Begin USProcure Platform Development

**Reform:** Begin development of the USProcure interoperability platform, starting with unified vendor registration (once-only principle) and an integrated opportunity portal. Adopt a modular, API-based architecture that enables progressive integration of existing systems.

**Evidence:** Korea's KONEPS saves $8 billion annually. Estonia's X-Road saves 2 percent of GDP. Both demonstrate that integrated digital platforms generate returns far exceeding their development costs. The key lesson from Estonia is that interoperability (connecting existing systems) is more achievable and less risky than replacement (building a single monolithic system).

**Mechanism:** GSA-led development with interagency governance. Phased delivery starting with vendor registration and opportunity publication. API standards for agency system integration.

**Expected Impact:** Elimination of redundant vendor registration across multiple systems. Unified opportunity search for all federal procurement. Foundation for comprehensive analytics and monitoring.

#### Recommendation 8: Implement Proportionality in the FAR

**Reform:** Amend the FAR to establish a tiered regulatory framework in which procurement process requirements are proportional to contract value and risk. Simplified procedures for procurements under $250,000. Standard procedures for $250,000 to $5 million. Full procedures for above $5 million.

**Evidence:** New Zealand's Government Procurement Rules explicitly establish proportionality as a core principle. Australia's tiered thresholds scale requirements to procurement size. The US Section 809 Panel recommended similar tiering for defense acquisition. Approximately 80 percent of federal procurement actions fall below $5 million—reducing regulatory burden for these transactions would generate enormous aggregate efficiency gains.

**Mechanism:** FAR rulemaking through the standard Federal Acquisition Regulatory Council process.

**Expected Impact:** 30-50 percent reduction in administrative processing time for procurements under $5 million. Increased small business participation (as reduced complexity lowers barriers to entry). Contracting officer time freed for strategic activities on larger, higher-risk acquisitions.

#### Recommendation 9: Pilot Social Value Evaluation

**Reform:** Pilot mandatory social value evaluation (5-10 percent of total score) in selected agencies and contract categories. Eligible criteria: workforce development, environmental sustainability, community benefit, innovation outcomes. Evaluate impact and refine approach before government-wide expansion.

**Evidence:** The UK's mandatory 10 percent social value weighting has generated over 25,000 jobs and apprenticeships annually while CCS achieved a 10.49 percent savings rate. New Zealand's broader outcomes requirements (mandatory for contracts above NZD $100,000) have embedded social and environmental considerations across government procurement. Neither country reports that social value requirements have reduced competition or increased costs.

**Mechanism:** OMB/OFPP pilot program with selected agencies. Standardized social value evaluation framework. Performance monitoring and impact assessment.

**Expected Impact:** Measurable social and environmental outcomes from procurement spending. Demonstrated feasibility for government-wide expansion. Learning and refinement of evaluation methodology.

#### Recommendation 10: Create FAR Innovation Partnership Procedure

**Reform:** Establish a new FAR procedure type—the Innovation Partnership—modeled on the EU's innovation partnership, allowing agencies to combine R&D and subsequent commercial procurement in a single process.

**Evidence:** The EU's innovation partnership procedure has been adopted by multiple member states and is growing in use. Denmark's pre-commercial procurement programs have produced commercially viable products (UV disinfection robots, permeable asphalt). The US SBIR program funds R&D but does not provide a pathway to commercial procurement—the "valley of death" that the innovation partnership specifically addresses.

**Mechanism:** FAR rulemaking to establish the Innovation Partnership as a new procedure type. Guidance and training for contracting officers. Pilot projects in healthcare, IT, and environmental technology.

**Expected Impact:** Accelerated transition of innovative solutions from R&D to operational deployment. Reduced "valley of death" losses. Access to solutions that traditional procurement cannot reach.

### 9.4 Long-Term Transformation (Years 4-5)

These recommendations represent the full realization of the reform vision, requiring the foundation established in Years 1-3.

#### Recommendation 11: Deploy USProcure Government-Wide

**Reform:** Complete and mandate government-wide adoption of the USProcure platform, incorporating unified vendor registration, integrated opportunity portal, electronic bid submission, contract lifecycle management, and real-time analytics.

**Evidence:** Korea mandated KONEPS for all 68,000 public entities. Georgia mandated Ge-GP for all public procurement. Singapore mandated GeBIZ for all ministries and statutory boards. In every case, mandatory adoption was essential to achieving full coverage and network effects. Optional adoption leads to fragmented implementation and perpetuates parallel systems.

**Mechanism:** Legislative mandate for government-wide adoption. Phased agency onboarding. Technical support and training for migrating agencies.

**Expected Impact:** Estimated $5-15 billion in annual savings through transaction cost reduction, increased competition, and improved price discovery. Comprehensive real-time visibility into all federal procurement activity.

#### Recommendation 12: Open CPARS Data for Research

**Reform:** Make de-identified CPARS (contractor performance) data available for academic and policy research, enabling evidence-based analysis of procurement outcomes. Current CPARS restrictions prevent researchers from studying the relationship between evaluation methods and contract performance at scale.

**Evidence:** The absence of accessible performance data is a critical gap in US procurement research. Academic studies must rely on proxy measures (cost growth, schedule performance) rather than direct performance ratings. Opening de-identified data would enable the kind of rigorous empirical analysis that drives evidence-based policy improvement. Chile and the UK both publish more comprehensive procurement performance data than the US.

**Mechanism:** Regulatory change to CPARS data access policies. Development of de-identification methodology to protect contractor-specific information. Establishment of a research data portal.

**Expected Impact:** Enabling evidence-based procurement policy. Generating academic research that identifies what works and what does not. Informing workforce training and evaluation methodology improvement.

#### Recommendation 13: Invest in Workforce Modernization

**Reform:** Implement a comprehensive workforce modernization program including recruitment of data analysts and category management specialists; development of advanced training curricula covering analytics, negotiation, market intelligence, and innovation procurement; retention incentives to compete with private sector compensation for high-demand skills; and rotation programs (modeled on Singapore) to build breadth of experience and prevent capture.

**Evidence:** Singapore invests heavily in procurement workforce development through the Civil Service College. The UK has established a Government Commercial Function with specialized career paths and professional development. Canada has proposed workforce transformation as one of its five foundational changes.

**Mechanism:** Legislation or executive action authorizing enhanced hiring authorities, training investment, and retention incentives for the acquisition workforce.

**Expected Impact:** Improved evaluation quality. Better negotiation outcomes. Enhanced analytical capability. Reduced reliance on LPTA as a default (as workforce confidence in conducting best-value evaluations increases).

#### Recommendation 14: Adopt the Open Contracting Data Standard

**Reform:** Adopt the Open Contracting Data Standard (OCDS) as the standard format for publishing federal procurement data, enabling interoperability with international datasets and supporting civil society analysis.

**Evidence:** Chile was among the first countries to adopt OCDS and has demonstrated its value for transparency, civil society engagement, and international benchmarking. Over 50 countries and cities have adopted or are implementing OCDS. The standard enables structured, machine-readable data publication that supports automated analysis—critical for the Observatory function.

**Mechanism:** OMB/OFPP directive establishing OCDS as the standard format for procurement data publication. Technical implementation through USProcure platform.

**Expected Impact:** Enhanced international comparability of US procurement data. Stronger civil society oversight. Improved data quality through standardization.

#### Recommendation 15: Conduct Five-Year Impact Assessment

**Reform:** Conduct a comprehensive five-year assessment of reform impact, measuring outcomes across all seven pillars. Publish results and use them to update the reform roadmap for Years 6-10.

**Evidence:** Every successful reform program reviewed in this report includes ongoing measurement and evaluation. Korea tracks KONEPS savings annually. Chile monitors Observatory impact continuously. The UK publishes CCS performance data. Without systematic measurement, it is impossible to know whether reforms are working, where adjustments are needed, and how to prioritize future investments.

**Mechanism:** GAO or independent evaluation. Comprehensive data collection from all reform initiatives. Public reporting of findings.

**Expected Impact:** Evidence base for continued reform. Accountability for reform investments. Course correction where needed. Knowledge sharing with other countries and with state/local governments.

## Chapter 10: Conclusion — The Imperative of Action

### 10.1 What We Have Learned

This report has examined twelve procurement systems across four continents, documenting reforms that have delivered measurable, often dramatic improvements in cost, speed, competition, transparency, and public value. The evidence is clear: better procurement systems are not theoretical constructs—they are operational realities in countries that made the decision to invest in reform.

South Korea's KONEPS saves $8 billion annually and reduced bidding time by 93 percent. Georgia's Ge-GP reduced procurement corruption from 97 percent to 3 percent, built in ten months for less than $1 million. Estonia's X-Road saves 2 percent of GDP annually and has been exported to over 20 countries. Chile's Observatory reduced procurement conflicts by 67 percent and achieved 59 percent beneficial ownership compliance in three months. The UK's CCS delivers GBP 4.9 billion in annual savings while mandating 10 percent social value in every evaluation. Australia's Indigenous Procurement Policy has generated $12.9 billion in opportunities and 83,500+ contracts for indigenous businesses. Brazil's pregao completes procurement in 17 days versus 90+ under traditional methods. Singapore reports 5 percent procurement fraud versus 29 percent globally. New Zealand's proportionality principle right-sizes procurement processes to contract value and risk. Canada's Ombudsman provides a non-adversarial dispute resolution pathway that the US lacks. Denmark's pre-commercial procurement has produced commercially viable innovations from hospital robots to permeable asphalt. The EU's MEAT standard establishes quality-focused evaluation as the norm across 27 countries.

These are not aspirational projections. They are documented outcomes from systems that serve as existence proofs—demonstrations that procurement reform of this nature is achievable.

### 10.2 The US Opportunity

The United States starts from a position of significant strength. Its procurement data transparency (USAspending.gov), small business programs (28.4 percent of contracts), protest accountability mechanisms (GAO), workforce size (190,000+ professionals), and category management initiatives are all among the best in the world. The challenge is not a lack of assets but a failure to fully deploy them—and a regulatory framework that, through accumulated complexity, often prevents the procurement system from achieving the outcomes it is designed to deliver.

The seven-pillar model presented in this report provides a comprehensive, evidence-based reform framework that builds on US strengths while selectively adopting proven innovations from around the world. The fifteen recommendations provide specific, actionable steps organized by implementation timeline. The implementation roadmap provides a realistic phased approach that begins with executive action and builds toward structural transformation.

### 10.3 The Cost of Inaction

The stakes could hardly be higher. The US federal government obligates over $700 billion annually in contracts. The international evidence documented in this report allows us to quantify what the United States is leaving on the table.

**Table 10.1: The Cost of Inaction — Conservative Estimates**

| Reform Area | Benchmark | Potential Annual US Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Platform consolidation | Korea saves $8B/yr on $160B volume (5%) | **$35B** on $700B US volume |
| Value-based evaluation | UK achieves 10.49% savings rate | **$73B** at UK rate; **$35B** at half that rate |
| Speed improvement | Brazil reduced cycle 90→17 days (81%) | **$14B** in reduced administrative costs and opportunity cost |
| Competition enhancement | Chile increased competition, reduced conflicts 67% | **$7B** from better pricing through competition |
| Corruption/waste reduction | Singapore: 5% fraud vs 29% globally | **$21B** if US reduced waste from est. 6% to 3% |
| **Conservative total (half of benchmark rates)** | | **$50-70B annually** |

If the United States achieved even half the savings rates documented in the best-performing international systems, the annual impact would exceed $50 billion—roughly the entire budget of the Department of Homeland Security. Over a decade, the cumulative impact would approach $500 billion to $700 billion.

These estimates are conservative. They do not account for the compounding benefits of better competition (which drives innovation), faster procurement (which accelerates service delivery), or reduced corruption (which builds public trust). Nor do they account for the social value gains that countries like the UK, Australia, and New Zealand have achieved through procurement—benefits to indigenous communities, small businesses, environmental sustainability, and local economies that are not captured in simple savings calculations.

Davidson (2026) provides additional empirical support for these projections. The dissertation's analysis of 15,477 federal contracts found that while the choice between LPTA and best-value tradeoff evaluation did not independently predict cost outcomes (H1, p = .377), the interaction between evaluation method and contract complexity was highly significant (H8, p = .003). This means that the *system*—the combination of evaluation approach, contract characteristics, and institutional context—determines outcomes, not any single variable in isolation. The international evidence in this report tells exactly the same story: countries that have reformed their procurement *systems* holistically have achieved dramatic improvements; those that have pursued isolated, piecemeal changes have not.

The borderline finding on competition (H6, OR = 0.752, p = .085) further suggests that best-value approaches may enhance competitive dynamics—consistent with the international evidence from Chile, Brazil, and Korea showing that well-designed procurement platforms and evaluation frameworks attract more bidders and produce better outcomes.

But the stakes extend beyond dollars. Every government service depends on procurement. The quality of healthcare for veterans, the readiness of military equipment, the reliability of federal IT systems, the effectiveness of disaster response, the progress of scientific research—all are shaped by how well the government buys. Getting procurement right is not an administrative efficiency exercise. It is a fundamental determinant of government capability and public welfare.

> **The Bottom Line:** Every dollar wasted in procurement is a dollar that could have funded a school, treated a veteran, maintained a bridge, or reduced the deficit. The countries examined in this report have demonstrated that better outcomes are achievable. The question for the United States is not whether reform is possible—the international evidence conclusively demonstrates that it is—but whether the political will exists to pursue it.

### 10.4 The Path Forward

Reform will not be easy. The current system has powerful constituencies—contractors who have optimized their business models for current rules, workforce members who are comfortable with current processes, oversight bodies that have built capabilities around current frameworks, and legislators who view procurement regulation as a vehicle for policy objectives. Any reform effort must navigate these interests, building coalitions and demonstrating results.

The phased approach recommended in this report is designed to address these challenges. Year 1 quick wins—value-for-money executive order, Observatory pilot, beneficial ownership disclosure, expanded reverse auctions—can generate visible results and build momentum. Medium-term structural reforms—Ombudsman, USProcure, proportionality, social value, innovation pathways—provide the institutional foundation for lasting change. Long-term transformation—government-wide platform deployment, open data, workforce modernization—realizes the full potential of the model.

International experience suggests that the most important factor is not the specific reform path chosen but the sustained political commitment to pursue reform systematically over time. South Korea built KONEPS over two decades of continuous improvement. The UK's procurement reform journey spans the Social Value Act of 2012 through the Procurement Act of 2023—more than a decade. Chile has been building on its Mercado Publico platform since 2003. These are not one-time projects but ongoing institutional commitments.

The United States has the resources, the data, the workforce, and the institutional capacity to build a procurement system that matches its scale and ambition. What has been missing is the synthesis of international evidence to demonstrate what is possible, and the political will to act on it. This report provides the first. The second is up to the American people and their elected leaders.

### 10.5 Final Reflection

Public procurement is often dismissed as an administrative backwater—a technical function of interest only to contracting specialists and government contractors. This view profoundly underestimates both the scale and the stakes of government purchasing. Procurement is the mechanism through which public policy becomes public reality. When a legislature appropriates funds for defense, healthcare, infrastructure, or education, it is the procurement system that translates those appropriations into tangible goods and services. The quality of that translation—whether it is fast or slow, efficient or wasteful, fair or corrupt, innovative or stagnant—matters enormously.

The twelve countries examined in this report have recognized this reality and acted on it. They have invested in digital platforms that make procurement faster and cheaper. They have codified value-for-money principles that ensure quality, not just price, drives outcomes. They have built oversight mechanisms that catch problems before they become scandals. They have created pathways for innovation procurement that solve problems traditional approaches cannot reach. They have integrated social and environmental value into procurement evaluation, recognizing that public money should serve public purposes. And they have shared their innovations with other countries, recognizing that better procurement is a global public good.

The United States can do the same. The models exist. The evidence is clear. The opportunity is enormous. As Davidson (2026) demonstrated empirically, the evaluation method alone does not determine procurement outcomes—the system does. The twelve countries in this report have built better systems. The only question remaining is whether this nation, which has shown the capacity for transformative change throughout its history, will apply that capacity to the unglamorous but critically important work of getting government contracting right.

## Glossary of Key Terms

**AHP (Analytic Hierarchy Process):** A structured multi-criteria decision analysis method used to evaluate procurement proposals by decomposing the evaluation into a hierarchy of criteria and sub-criteria, assigning weights, and scoring proposals against each criterion. Used extensively in Singapore's GeBIZ system.

**Beneficial Ownership:** The identification of the natural person(s) who ultimately own or control a legal entity. Beneficial ownership disclosure in procurement prevents the use of shell companies to conceal conflicts of interest, sanctions evasion, or corrupt relationships. Chile achieved 59 percent compliance within three months of implementation.

**Best-in-Class (BIC):** A US federal designation for government-wide contract vehicles that meet rigorous criteria for pricing, terms, and management. BIC spending exceeded $120 billion in FY2023.

**Best-Value Tradeoff:** A US federal procurement evaluation methodology in which the government evaluates proposals based on both price and non-price factors (technical merit, past performance, management approach) and may award to other than the lowest-priced offeror if the additional value justifies the additional cost.

**Category Management:** A strategic approach to procurement that organizes spending into logical categories and leverages the government's collective buying power to achieve better pricing, terms, and outcomes.

**ComprasNet/PNCP:** Brazil's electronic procurement platform. ComprasNet is being migrated to the Portal Nacional de Contratacoes Publicas (PNCP) under the 2021 procurement law.

**CPARS (Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System):** The US federal system for recording contractor performance evaluations. Data is restricted and not publicly accessible, limiting research and transparency.

**Crown Commercial Service (CCS):** The UK government's central purchasing body, managing GBP 30.3 billion in commercial agreements and delivering GBP 4.9 billion in annual savings.

**DFARS (Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement):** The Department of Defense supplement to the FAR, adding defense-specific procurement rules.

**FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation):** The primary body of rules governing US federal procurement. Exceeds 2,000 pages and is supplemented by dozens of agency-specific regulations.

**GeBIZ (Government Electronic Business):** Singapore's centralized e-procurement portal serving 120+ government agencies.

**Ge-GP (Electronic Government Procurement):** Georgia's e-procurement system, built in 10 months for under $1 million, credited with reducing corruption from 97 percent to 3 percent.

**Green Public Procurement (GPP):** The EU framework for incorporating environmental criteria into procurement evaluation and contract requirements.

**HUBZone:** A US SBA program providing contracting preferences to businesses in Historically Underutilized Business Zones.

**Indigenous Procurement Policy (IPP):** Australia's policy mandating government procurement with indigenous-owned businesses, generating $12.9 billion in cumulative opportunities and 83,500+ contracts.

**Innovation Partnership:** An EU procurement procedure type that combines R&D and subsequent commercial procurement in a single process, addressing the "valley of death" between development and deployment.

**KONEPS (Korea ON-line E-Procurement System):** South Korea's single-window e-procurement platform connecting 68,000+ entities with 430,000+ suppliers, saving an estimated $8 billion annually.

**Life-Cycle Costing (LCC):** An evaluation methodology that considers total cost over the useful life of a product or service, including acquisition, operation, maintenance, and disposal costs. Formalized in EU procurement directives.

**LPTA (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable):** A US federal procurement evaluation methodology in which the contract is awarded to the lowest-priced offeror that meets minimum technical requirements. No credit for exceeding requirements.

**MAT (Most Advantageous Tender):** The evaluation standard established by the UK Procurement Act 2023, replacing the EU's MEAT. Considers the full range of public value—social, environmental, and economic.

**MEAT (Most Economically Advantageous Tender):** The EU standard evaluation criterion requiring consideration of best price-quality ratio, not just lowest price.

**Mercado Publico:** Chile's electronic marketplace for all public procurement, operated by ChileCompra.

**OCDS (Open Contracting Data Standard):** An international standard for publishing structured, machine-readable procurement data. Adopted by Chile and 50+ other jurisdictions.

**Once-Only Principle:** The principle—pioneered by Estonia—that citizens and businesses should provide any piece of information to the government only once, with that information shared across all entities that need it.

**OPO (Office of the Procurement Ombudsman):** Canada's independent body for reviewing procurement complaints, mediating disputes, and recommending practice improvements.

**OTA (Other Transaction Authority):** A US procurement mechanism that allows federal agencies to enter into agreements outside the FAR for prototype development and follow-on production.

**PCP (Pre-Commercial Procurement):** A procurement approach that funds multiple vendors through progressive R&D phases (concept, prototype, pilot) before commercial procurement. Pioneered by Denmark and promoted by the EU.

**Pregao:** Brazil's electronic reverse auction procurement method, in which vendors compete by lowering prices in real time and qualification is verified after the price competition.

**Proportionality Principle:** The principle—codified in New Zealand's procurement rules—that the level of procurement process should be proportional to the value and risk of the procurement.

**SAM.gov (System for Award Management):** The primary US federal platform for vendor registration, opportunity search, and entity management.

**SBIR (Small Business Innovation Research):** A US program providing competitive awards to small businesses for R&D with commercial potential. Managed across federal agencies.

**Section 809 Panel:** A congressionally mandated advisory panel (2016-2019) that produced over 1,000 recommendations for streamlining US defense acquisition regulations.

**Social Value:** The broader economic, social, and environmental benefits generated through procurement, beyond the direct goods and services purchased. Mandatory evaluation criterion in UK central government contracts (minimum 10 percent weighting).

**TED (Tenders Electronic Daily):** The EU's online platform for publishing public procurement notices above threshold values.

**USProcure:** The proposed unified US federal procurement platform combining vendor registration, opportunity publication, bid submission, contract management, and analytics.

**Value for Money (VfM):** The optimal combination of whole-of-life costs and quality to meet the user's requirement. Core principle of Australian, New Zealand, and UK procurement frameworks.

**X-Road:** Estonia's distributed data exchange platform enabling secure, real-time data sharing between 929 institutions. Foundation of Estonia's digital government ecosystem and exported to 20+ countries.
