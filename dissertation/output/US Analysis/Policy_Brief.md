# Does How We Pick Government Contractors Actually Matter?

## What 15,000+ Federal Contracts Tell Us

**A Policy Brief for Lawmakers, Government Leaders, and Taxpayers**

**Richard W. Davidson, DBA Candidate**
**Indiana University -- Kelley School of Business**

---

> ### Key Takeaways
>
> - The federal government spends over **$700 billion per year** on contracts. How it picks contractors should be guided by evidence, not ideology.
> - We studied **15,477 competitive federal contracts** from fiscal year 2024 to test whether the method used to choose a contractor -- cheapest bid versus weighing quality alongside price -- actually affects outcomes.
> - **Going with the cheapest bid did not lead to significantly more cost overruns overall.** This surprised us.
> - **But contract size changes the story.** For smaller contracts ($250K to $5M), weighing quality alongside price *did* produce lower cost growth. For bigger contracts, the advantage disappeared.
> - **No single evaluation method is a silver bullet.** Better contract management, a more skilled acquisition workforce, and improved data may matter more than mandating one approach over the other.

---

## The Problem in Plain English

Every year, the federal government signs hundreds of thousands of contracts -- for everything from IT systems and cybersecurity to building maintenance and consulting services. In fiscal year 2024 alone, the government obligated more than **$700 billion** through contracts.

When a federal agency needs to hire a contractor, it has to decide *how* to pick one. There are two main approaches:

**Lowest Price, Technically Acceptable (LPTA):** The agency sets minimum requirements. Every company that meets those requirements is eligible. The contract goes to whoever offers the lowest price. Think of it like buying a commodity -- you know exactly what you want, and you just want the cheapest option that checks every box.

**Best Value (Tradeoff):** The agency considers multiple factors -- the company's technical approach, past performance, management plan, and staffing -- alongside price. A company that costs a bit more but has a stronger plan or better track record can still win. Think of it like hiring an employee -- you don't just pick whoever asks for the lowest salary.

### Why Does This Matter?

Congress, government watchdogs, and industry groups have debated this choice for years. The concern is straightforward:

> If you always go with the cheapest bid, do you end up paying more in the long run -- through cost overruns, shoddy work, change orders, and failed projects?

It sounds intuitive. "You get what you pay for," as the saying goes. And Congress has acted on this intuition. The **National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA)** has repeatedly restricted the Department of Defense's use of the lowest-price method for certain types of contracts. Several government reports have recommended limiting its use.

But until now, **no large-scale empirical study had tested whether these concerns hold up in the data.** Most of the debate has been based on anecdotes, case studies, or opinion surveys.

We decided to find out.

This question isn't just academic. Real consequences flow from how the government picks its contractors. When a contract goes wrong -- when software doesn't work, when a building project stalls, when a service provider delivers poor results -- taxpayers pay twice: once for the failed work and again to fix it. If there's a better way to pick contractors that reduces these failures, the public deserves to know. And if the current debate is based on faulty assumptions, the public deserves to know that too.

## What We Did

We analyzed **15,477 competitive federal service and IT contracts** valued at over $250,000, all awarded during fiscal year 2024. The data came from **USAspending.gov**, a publicly available federal database.

We focused on three outcomes that taxpayers and policymakers care about:

1. **Cost overruns** -- Did the final cost exceed the original contract price?
2. **Contract modifications** -- How often did the contract need to be changed after it was signed?
3. **Competition** -- Did only one company bid, or did multiple companies compete for the work?

### Making Fair Comparisons

A simple comparison of lowest-price versus best-value contracts would be misleading. Agencies don't pick their evaluation method at random. They tend to use the lowest-price method for simpler, more routine work and the best-value method for complex, high-stakes projects. Comparing the two directly would be like comparing outcomes for patients who take aspirin versus those who undergo surgery -- the patients are different to begin with.

To address this, we used **statistical matching techniques** -- methods that create apples-to-apples comparisons by pairing contracts that are similar in size, type, and complexity but differ in the evaluation method used. This is the same general approach used in medical research and program evaluation when a randomized experiment isn't possible.

---

## What We Found

### Finding 1: The Cheapest Bid Didn't Lead to Significantly More Cost Overruns

This was our most surprising result.

Across the full sample, **the evaluation method alone did not drive post-award cost growth.** Contracts awarded using the lowest-price method did not experience statistically higher cost overruns than those awarded using the best-value method.

> **By the numbers:** The difference in cost growth between the two methods was not statistically significant (p = 0.377). In plain terms, there is roughly a 38% chance we would see a difference this large even if the evaluation method had no real effect -- far too high to draw any firm conclusion.

This finding held up across every subgroup we tested -- different agency types, different service categories, different contract structures.

### Finding 2: No Difference in How Often Contracts Needed Changes

We also looked at whether lowest-price contracts required more modifications -- additions, corrections, or scope changes after the award. Again, **the rates were similar regardless of which method was used** (p = 0.449).

This matters because contract modifications are often cited as a hidden cost of going cheap. Our data did not support that claim at the overall level.

### Finding 3: Best-Value May Attract More Competition

Here we found an interesting signal. Contracts using the best-value method showed roughly **25% lower odds of receiving only a single bid** compared to lowest-price contracts. In other words, more companies appeared willing to compete when they knew the government would consider quality, not just price.

> **A note on certainty:** This finding was suggestive but not conclusive (p = 0.085). In research terms, it fell short of the conventional threshold for statistical significance. We consider it a pattern worth watching and studying further, not a proven fact.

Why might this happen? When the government says "we'll pick the cheapest option that meets our minimum requirements," some companies may decide it's not worth the effort to compete. They know they can't win on price alone, so they don't bother bidding. When quality counts, more firms may see a path to winning.

This matters for taxpayers. More competition generally means better prices and better solutions. If the way we structure a solicitation discourages companies from bidding, we may be leaving money -- and innovation -- on the table. A government that receives only one bid has no negotiating leverage and no way to compare alternatives.

### Finding 4: Size Matters -- The Study's Strongest Result

This is where the data told the most compelling story.

**For smaller contracts ($250,000 to $5 million), best-value evaluation produced meaningfully lower cost growth.** The relationship was statistically significant and practically important (p = 0.003 for the interaction effect).

For larger contracts, however, **the advantage of best-value disappeared -- and in some cases reversed.**

> **What does this mean in practice?** For a $1 million IT services contract, choosing to evaluate proposals on quality alongside price -- rather than just picking the cheapest -- was associated with lower cost growth after the award. But for a $50 million systems integration contract, the evaluation method didn't make a clear difference.

Why might this be? We have several hypotheses:

- **Smaller contracts may benefit most from quality screening** because they often lack the oversight infrastructure (dedicated program managers, extensive reporting requirements) that larger contracts have.
- **Larger contracts tend to receive more scrutiny regardless** -- more reviews, more oversight, more senior attention -- which may compensate for any weaknesses in the initial evaluation method.
- **Larger contracts are also more complex**, and their cost growth may be driven by factors (changing requirements, technology shifts, political priorities) that no evaluation method can fully address.

---

## What We Did Not Find

It is just as important to note what the data did *not* show:

- **No evidence that the lowest-price method is broadly harmful.** Despite years of criticism, the data does not support a blanket ban on this approach.
- **No evidence that best-value evaluation is a cure-all.** Simply weighing quality alongside price did not eliminate cost overruns or reduce modifications across the board.
- **No evidence that either method is universally superior.** Context matters -- especially contract size.

## What This Means for Policy

Based on these findings, we offer six recommendations for policymakers:

### 1. Don't Mandate One Method Over the Other

The data does not support requiring agencies to use either lowest-price or best-value evaluation for all contracts. A one-size-fits-all mandate -- in either direction -- would be inconsistent with what the evidence shows.

> **For lawmakers:** Rather than legislating which evaluation method agencies must use, focus on ensuring contracting officers have the training, resources, and authority to choose the right method for each situation.

### 2. Pay Special Attention to Smaller Contracts

For contracts in the $250,000 to $5 million range -- which represent a large share of federal contracting activity -- the data suggests that best-value evaluation produces better cost outcomes. Agencies should default to considering quality alongside price for these contracts, unless there is a clear reason to do otherwise.

> **Practical suggestion:** Agency procurement guidance could establish a presumption in favor of best-value evaluation for contracts under $5 million in services and IT, with a documented justification required for choosing lowest-price instead.

### 3. Invest in the Acquisition Workforce

If the evaluation method itself doesn't explain most of the variation in contract outcomes, then *something else does*. Our research points to a likely candidate: **the people managing the process.**

A skilled contracting officer who writes clear requirements, evaluates proposals thoughtfully, and manages contractor performance actively will likely achieve good outcomes regardless of the evaluation method used. An undertrained or overworked contracting officer may struggle with either approach.

The federal acquisition workforce has faced years of hiring freezes, retirements, and growing workloads. The average contracting officer manages dozens of active contracts simultaneously, often with limited support staff. Many agencies report difficulty recruiting and retaining experienced procurement professionals, particularly when the private sector offers higher salaries for the same skill set.

Investing in recruitment, training, and retention of acquisition professionals may yield a higher return than any regulatory change to evaluation procedures. Concrete steps could include expanding the use of the Acquisition Workforce Development Fund, creating fellowship and rotation programs, and reducing administrative burdens so contracting officers can spend more time on judgment-intensive tasks like evaluation and oversight.

### 4. Fix the Data

One of the most frustrating aspects of this research was what we *couldn't* study. The federal government maintains a database of **contractor performance ratings (CPARS)**, but this data is not available to researchers or the public. Without it, we cannot measure the most important outcome of all: **Did the contractor actually do a good job?**

> **For lawmakers and agency leaders:** Making anonymized contractor performance data available for research -- with appropriate privacy protections -- would dramatically improve our ability to answer the questions that matter most. Cost overruns are an imperfect proxy for quality. We need to measure quality directly.

### 5. Recognize the Limits of Pre-Award Decisions

This study focused on one decision point: how the government evaluates proposals before making an award. But a contract's success or failure depends on many decisions that come *after* the award:

- How well does the government manage the contractor's performance?
- Are requirements clear and stable, or do they shift constantly?
- Does the agency have enough qualified staff to provide oversight?
- Are problems identified and addressed early, or allowed to fester?

**Post-award contract management may matter more than pre-award evaluation method.** Policymakers should invest as much attention in contract oversight as they do in procurement reform.

### 6. The NDAA Restrictions Were Directionally Right -- but Not Sufficient

Congress has restricted the use of lowest-price evaluation for certain Defense Department contracts through the National Defense Authorization Act. Our findings suggest this instinct was **partially supported by the evidence** -- particularly for smaller contracts where best-value evaluation does appear to help.

However, restricting one evaluation method without addressing the underlying challenges -- workforce capacity, data availability, and contract management -- is unlikely to solve the problem on its own. Our data suggests that a more targeted approach -- focusing LPTA restrictions on smaller, moderately complex service contracts while preserving agency discretion for larger or more routine acquisitions -- would be more consistent with the evidence.

## A Note on What This Study Can and Cannot Tell Us

Every study has limitations, and we want to be transparent about ours:

- **We studied one year of data (FY2024).** Patterns may differ across years or economic conditions.
- **We measured cost growth and modifications, not quality.** A contract that comes in on budget is not necessarily a successful contract. We could not measure whether the work itself was done well because performance data is not publicly available.
- **We used statistical matching, not a randomized experiment.** While this is the best available approach, it cannot prove causation with the same certainty as a controlled experiment. There may be unmeasured factors influencing both the choice of method and contract outcomes.
- **Our sample covers contracts over $250,000.** Results may differ for smaller purchases.

Despite these limitations, this is -- to our knowledge -- **the largest empirical study of federal evaluation method effects conducted to date.** The findings are based on real contract data, not surveys or opinions, and the results were tested using multiple statistical approaches to ensure robustness.

---

## The Bottom Line

> The method used to pick a contractor is just one piece of the puzzle. **Better requirements, better contract management, and a skilled acquisition workforce may do more to save taxpayer money than mandating any particular evaluation approach.**
>
> But the data does tell us one clear thing: **for smaller contracts, choosing to weigh quality alongside price -- rather than just picking the cheapest bid -- appears to produce better cost outcomes.** That finding is worth acting on.

The federal government's $700+ billion annual contracting spend demands evidence-based policy. This study provides some of that evidence. The answer isn't as simple as "always pick the cheapest" or "never pick the cheapest." It's more nuanced -- and better policy will come from embracing that nuance rather than reaching for easy answers.

For too long, the debate over how to pick government contractors has been driven by assumptions and anecdotes. We now have data from more than 15,000 real contracts that can inform this discussion. The findings won't satisfy those looking for a simple answer. But they should reassure taxpayers that careful, evidence-based decision-making -- not sweeping mandates -- is the best path forward for responsible stewardship of public funds.

## Glossary of Terms

**Best Value (Tradeoff):** A method of evaluating contractor proposals that considers technical quality, past performance, and other factors alongside price. The government can choose a higher-priced proposal if it offers meaningfully better quality.

**CPARS (Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System):** A federal database where agencies rate contractor performance. Currently restricted to government users and not available for independent research.

**Cost Growth:** The difference between a contract's original value and its final value. Often reflects change orders, scope changes, or overruns.

**LPTA (Lowest Price, Technically Acceptable):** A method of evaluating contractor proposals where the government sets minimum technical requirements and awards the contract to the lowest-priced bidder that meets them.

**Modification:** A formal change to a contract after it has been awarded. Can include changes in scope, price, schedule, or terms.

**NDAA (National Defense Authorization Act):** Annual legislation that sets policies and budgets for the Department of Defense. Has included provisions restricting the use of LPTA evaluation in recent years.

**p-value:** A statistical measure of how likely it is that an observed result occurred by chance. Lower values suggest the result is more likely to be real. By convention, a p-value below 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

**Statistical matching:** A research technique that creates fair comparisons by pairing similar observations that differ on one key variable. Used when randomized experiments are not feasible.

**USAspending.gov:** A publicly accessible federal website that tracks government spending, including detailed information on contracts, grants, and other awards.
