## 1. Executive Summary

The federal government spends more than $700 billion each year on contracts with private companies. When an agency needs to hire a contractor, it must decide *how* to pick one. The two main approaches are: go with the cheapest bid that meets minimum requirements, or weigh quality alongside price and pick the best overall deal.

This question has been debated for decades in Congress, in agency procurement offices, and in industry boardrooms. Despite all the debate, surprisingly little rigorous evidence has existed to settle it. This study analyzed more than 15,000 real federal contracts to find out what actually happens.

**Key findings:**

- **The evaluation method alone did not drive cost overruns.** Across the full sample of 15,477 contracts, there was no statistically significant difference in cost growth between the two approaches. This was the study's most robust finding -- it held up in every subgroup and every statistical test.

- **Contract modifications were similar under both methods.** The cheapest-bid approach did not produce more post-award changes than the quality-weighing approach.

- **Weighing quality may attract more competition.** Contracts that considered quality alongside price showed roughly 25% lower odds of receiving only a single bid. This finding was suggestive but fell just short of the conventional threshold for statistical certainty.

- **Contract size changes the equation -- and this is the study's strongest finding.** For smaller contracts ($250,000 to about $5 million), weighing quality alongside price was associated with meaningfully lower cost growth (p = 0.003). For larger contracts, this advantage disappeared. This finding was statistically significant and practically important.

**What this means for policy:**

- Do not mandate a single evaluation method for all contracts. The evidence does not support a one-size-fits-all approach.
- Focus quality-weighing requirements on smaller and mid-sized service and IT contracts, where the data shows the most benefit.
- Invest in the acquisition workforce. The skill of the people managing the procurement process may matter more than the formal rules they follow.
- Improve post-award contract management. What happens after the contract is signed may be more important than how the contractor was chosen.
- Make contractor performance data available for research, so the quality question can actually be answered.
- Strengthen requirements development -- the clarity of what the government asks for may matter more than how it evaluates the answers.

## 2. The Problem: Why This Matters to Taxpayers

### The Scale of Federal Contracting

Every year, the United States federal government signs contracts worth more than **$700 billion**. To put that in perspective, that is roughly the entire gross domestic product of Switzerland. The government is the single largest buyer of goods and services on the planet.

These are not abstract budget numbers. Federal contracts pay for the information systems that process veterans' disability claims. They fund the cybersecurity teams that protect critical infrastructure. They support the cloud computing platforms that host census data. They cover everything from building maintenance at federal courthouses to advanced satellite systems for national defense.

When these contracts go well, the public gets the services it needs at a fair price. When they go wrong -- when software systems fail to work, when construction projects stall, when consulting firms deliver poor advice -- taxpayers pay twice: once for the work that failed, and again to fix it or start over.

### Two Ways to Pick a Contractor

At the heart of every competitive federal contract is a deceptively simple decision: *How should we choose which company gets the work?*

The federal government has two main approaches:

**The cheapest-bid approach (formally known as "Lowest Price Technically Acceptable" or LPTA).** The agency sets minimum requirements. Any company that clears the bar is eligible. The contract goes to whoever offers the lowest price. Full stop.

**The quality-weighing approach (formally known as "Best-Value Tradeoff").** The agency evaluates companies on multiple factors -- their technical plan, their track record, their management approach, and their staffing -- alongside price. A company that costs a bit more but has a stronger proposal can still win.

Think about it in everyday terms:

> **The cheapest-bid approach is like buying a car based solely on sticker price.** You decide you need a four-door sedan with at least 30 miles per gallon. Any car that meets those minimums qualifies. You buy the cheapest one on the lot. You do not consider reliability ratings, safety features, resale value, or warranty coverage -- just the price tag.

> **The quality-weighing approach is like actually shopping for a car.** You compare fuel efficiency, safety ratings, maintenance costs, warranty terms, and the dealer's reputation -- and then you weigh all of that against the price. You might pay a bit more for a car with better reliability ratings because you know it will cost less to own over five years.

Or think of it like hiring a contractor to renovate your kitchen:

> Under the cheapest-bid approach, you would describe your kitchen renovation, collect bids from licensed contractors, and hire whoever quotes the lowest number. Under the quality-weighing approach, you would also look at each contractor's past work, their proposed timeline, their crew's experience, and their references -- and then decide which bid offers the best overall value for your money.

Most homeowners instinctively use the quality-weighing approach for significant purchases. The question is whether the federal government should do the same for its most important contracts.

### The Real-World Consequences

The debate over these two methods is not theoretical. Real consequences flow from the choice:

**Cost overruns.** When a contractor wins by bidding the lowest price on a complex project, that contractor may have achieved the low price by underestimating the work, planning to use less experienced staff, or assuming that ambiguities in the contract will break in their favor. When reality hits, the costs climb. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) has documented numerous cases where the initial "savings" from lowest-price awards were dwarfed by subsequent cost increases.

**Change orders and modifications.** Federal contracts are routinely modified after they are signed -- sometimes dozens of times. Each modification requires government staff time to negotiate, review, and approve. When a contract was awarded based on an unrealistically low price, modifications become the mechanism through which costs climb back to reality.

**Quality problems.** When the only criterion is price, companies have every incentive to minimize costs during performance. That can mean less experienced staff, thinner quality controls, and cutting corners where the contract language permits it. The government may not discover the quality problems until the damage is done.

**Reduced competition.** Ironically, the cheapest-bid approach -- which is intended to promote competition -- may actually reduce it. When companies know the government will pick the lowest price regardless of quality, firms that invest in experienced personnel, innovative approaches, and robust management systems may decide it is not worth competing. They know they cannot win a price war against a competitor willing to cut every corner.

### Congressional Concern

Congress has noticed. Over the past decade, lawmakers on both sides of the aisle have raised concerns about agencies defaulting to the cheapest-bid approach for complex contracts where quality matters.

The **National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA)** for Fiscal Year 2017 included Section 813, which directed the Department of Defense to limit its use of the cheapest-bid method for knowledge-based professional services, cybersecurity, and acquisitions with significant life-cycle costs. The NDAA for Fiscal Year 2018 went further, requiring Defense Department contracting officers to avoid the cheapest-bid method when it would prevent them from considering a company's past performance.

These legislative actions reflected a bipartisan judgment that something was wrong. But they were based largely on anecdotes, case studies, and professional intuition -- not on the kind of large-scale empirical evidence that should inform major policy decisions.

This study was designed to fill that gap.

## 3. How Government Picks Contractors Today

### The Two Methods, Explained

To understand the study's findings, it helps to understand exactly how each method works in practice.

**Lowest Price Technically Acceptable (LPTA)**

When an agency uses LPTA, the process works like this:

1. The agency writes a solicitation describing what it needs and setting minimum technical requirements.
2. Companies submit proposals that include both a technical volume and a price volume.
3. Government evaluators review each technical proposal against the minimum requirements. The evaluation is pass/fail -- either the proposal meets the requirements or it does not. There is no credit for exceeding the minimums.
4. Among the proposals that pass, the government awards the contract to the company that offered the lowest price.

The key feature of LPTA is that **quality above the minimum threshold does not matter**. A company that proposes an innovative approach with a team of industry experts and a detailed management plan will lose to a company that barely clears the technical bar but offers a lower price.

**Best-Value Tradeoff**

When an agency uses the tradeoff approach, the process is more involved:

1. The agency writes a solicitation describing what it needs, identifies the evaluation factors (such as technical approach, past performance, management plan, staffing, and price), and explains how much weight each factor carries.
2. Companies submit proposals addressing all evaluation factors.
3. Government evaluators assess each proposal on every factor, assigning ratings or scores. Unlike LPTA, there is a comparative evaluation -- proposals are ranked against each other, not just against a minimum bar.
4. The government makes a tradeoff decision: it weighs the strengths and weaknesses of each proposal across all factors and selects the one that offers the best overall value. This means the government can select a higher-priced proposal if the quality advantages justify the premium.

The key feature of the tradeoff approach is that **quality counts**. A company with a stronger technical approach, better past performance, and a more experienced team can win even if it is not the cheapest bidder.

### The Rules: The Federal Acquisition Regulation

Both methods are governed by the **Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR)**, the massive body of rules that controls how the federal government buys things. Part 15 of the FAR lays out the ground rules for competitive proposals.

The FAR does not say one method is always better than the other. Instead, it provides guidance on when each is appropriate:

- **LPTA is appropriate** when the requirement is well-defined, when there is minimal risk of unsuccessful performance, and when paying more for higher quality would not meaningfully benefit the government.

- **Tradeoff is appropriate** when the requirement is complex, when quality differences among companies are likely and consequential, and when the government values technical or managerial excellence beyond minimum requirements.

In practice, the FAR gives contracting officers -- the government professionals who manage the procurement process -- considerable discretion in choosing between the two methods.

### Why Agencies Default to the Cheapest Bid

Despite the FAR's guidance, many agencies default to LPTA even for complex contracts. Several institutional pressures push in this direction:

**Fear of protests.** When a losing company challenges an award decision (called a "bid protest"), the agency must defend its choice. LPTA awards are perceived as easier to defend because the decision criteria are straightforward: the company met the requirements and offered the lowest price. Tradeoff decisions, which involve subjective judgments about the relative value of technical approaches, are perceived as more vulnerable to challenge. Whether this perception is accurate is debatable, but it influences behavior.

**Administrative burden.** Tradeoff evaluations require more work. The agency must assemble a source selection team, develop detailed evaluation criteria, train evaluators, conduct comparative assessments, document the tradeoff rationale, and often conduct discussions with offerors. LPTA evaluations are simpler: check whether proposals meet the requirements, then compare prices. For overworked procurement offices, the simpler path is tempting.

**Workforce shortages.** The federal acquisition workforce -- the professionals who plan, manage, and oversee contracts -- has faced years of hiring freezes, retirements, and growing workloads. Conducting a rigorous tradeoff evaluation requires significant skill and experience. When the workforce is stretched thin, the simpler LPTA approach becomes a practical necessity even when it is not the ideal choice.

**Political and organizational pressure.** In an environment that emphasizes cost discipline and fiscal responsibility, agencies may feel pressure to demonstrate that they are getting the lowest possible price. Awarding a contract to a company that was not the cheapest bidder -- even if it offered the best overall value -- can invite second-guessing from oversight bodies, inspectors general, and congressional appropriators.

### The Case for Weighing Quality

The theoretical case for considering quality alongside price is straightforward and draws on several well-established economic principles:

**Better screening.** When the government evaluates quality, it can distinguish between companies that merely clear a minimum bar and those that have genuine capability, relevant experience, and sound approaches. This screening function is especially valuable for complex work where the difference between a good and a mediocre contractor can mean the difference between project success and failure.

**Less underbidding.** When price is the only selection criterion, companies have an incentive to bid as low as possible -- sometimes unrealistically low -- and then recover costs through post-award change orders and modifications. When quality also counts, companies can compete on the strength of their approach rather than engaging in a pure price war, reducing the incentive to lowball.

**Broader competition.** Companies that invest in quality, innovation, and experienced personnel may be discouraged from competing when only price matters, because they know their higher costs will disqualify them. When quality is evaluated, these firms have a meaningful chance of winning, which can broaden the competitive field and give the government access to more and better options.

**Better lifecycle value.** A contractor selected for quality as well as price may deliver work that requires fewer corrections, less oversight, and fewer modifications over the life of the contract. The slightly higher initial price may be more than offset by lower costs downstream.

### How Other Countries Handle This

The United States is not alone in grappling with this question. The European Union's procurement directives explicitly provide for "most economically advantageous tender" (MEAT) evaluation -- the European equivalent of best-value tradeoff. Several EU member states have moved to restrict lowest-price-only awards for complex services.

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has identified "value for money" -- not lowest price -- as a core principle of public procurement. The OECD framework emphasizes that procurement decisions should consider quality, lifecycle costs, and risk alongside initial price.

The Asian Development Bank and the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development have developed detailed methodologies for assessing value for money that incorporate risk adjustment, recognizing that a low initial price with a high probability of overruns may actually represent poor value compared to a higher but more predictable price.

The international trend is clearly toward more sophisticated evaluation methods that consider quality alongside price. But here, as in the United States, the empirical evidence on whether these methods actually produce better results has been thin.

## 4. What We Studied and How

### The Dataset

This study analyzed **15,477 competitive federal service and IT contracts** valued at more than $250,000 each, all awarded during the second quarter of fiscal year 2024 (January through March 2024). The data came from **USAspending.gov**, the federal government's official public database of spending information.

The contracts in the study covered a wide range of work:

- Professional, scientific, and technical services (management consulting, computer systems design, engineering, research and development)
- Computing infrastructure, data processing, and web hosting
- Software development and publishing
- Administrative and support services (facilities support, staffing, document preparation)

These categories were chosen because they represent the types of work where the evaluation method debate is most active and most consequential. For commodity purchases -- say, buying office supplies or standard equipment -- the cheapest-bid approach makes intuitive sense because the products are standardized. But for complex services and IT, where the quality of the work can vary enormously depending on who does it, the choice of evaluation method has real stakes.

### What We Measured

We focused on three outcomes that taxpayers and policymakers care about:

**1. Cost growth.** Did the contract end up costing more than originally planned? We measured this as the percentage difference between the contract's initial value and its current total value after modifications. A contract that started at $1 million and grew to $1.2 million would have 20% cost growth.

**2. Contract modifications.** How many times was the contract formally changed after it was signed? Modifications can include scope changes, price adjustments, timeline extensions, and other alterations. More modifications generally mean more administrative work, more uncertainty, and potentially more cost.

**3. Competition.** Did the contract attract only a single bid, or did multiple companies compete? Single-bid contracts -- sometimes called "competitions of one" -- are a persistent concern because the government has no leverage when only one company shows up. We measured this as a simple yes/no: did the contract receive one bid, or more than one?

### Making Fair Comparisons: Why This Is Harder Than It Sounds

Here is the central challenge of this type of research: **agencies do not randomly assign evaluation methods to contracts.** They choose the method based on the nature of the work. More complex projects tend to get the tradeoff approach; simpler projects tend to get LPTA.

This means a simple comparison of outcomes between the two methods would be misleading. If tradeoff contracts show higher cost growth, is that because the evaluation method caused higher costs, or because tradeoff contracts tend to be more complex and complex projects naturally have more cost growth? The correlation does not tell us which way the cause-and-effect arrow points.

To address this problem, we used a statistical technique called **propensity score matching**. Here is how it works, in plain terms:

> Imagine you have a large group of contracts. Some used the cheapest-bid method and some used the quality-weighing method. For each quality-weighing contract, you find a cheapest-bid contract that looks as similar as possible in every other way: similar size, similar type of work, similar agency, similar contract structure. You then compare outcomes between these matched pairs. Because the pairs are similar in everything except the evaluation method, any difference in outcomes can more plausibly be attributed to the evaluation method itself.

Think of it like a medical study. You cannot randomly assign patients to smoke or not smoke. But you can match smokers with non-smokers who are similar in age, weight, exercise habits, and family history, and then compare their health outcomes. That is the logic of propensity score matching.

In our study, we matched contracts on:
- The size of the award
- The industry sector (type of work)
- The awarding agency
- The contract pricing structure
- Whether it was a standalone contract or part of a larger vehicle
- Small business set-aside status

Of the 1,379 contracts that used the quality-weighing approach, we successfully matched 1,093 (about 79%) to comparable cheapest-bid contracts. We then compared outcomes between these matched pairs.

We also ran traditional regression models with controls for agency, industry, and other factors to make sure our results held up under different analytical approaches.

### What This Approach Can and Cannot Do

Propensity score matching is one of the best tools available when you cannot run a randomized experiment. It controls for the observable differences between the two groups -- the factors you can see and measure in the data.

But it cannot control for factors you cannot see. If contracting officers choose the tradeoff method for contracts that are more complex in ways that our data does not capture -- for example, because the requirements are unusually vague or the project carries unusual political risk -- then our matching cannot fully account for that. We address this by running multiple types of statistical checks and testing the sensitivity of our results to potential hidden factors. But it is important to be transparent: this study provides strong evidence of patterns and associations, but it cannot prove cause and effect with the certainty of a randomized experiment. No observational study can.

## 5. What the Data Tells Us

The study produced four main findings. Some confirmed what many practitioners expected. Some challenged conventional wisdom. And one was genuinely surprising to everyone involved.

### Finding 1: The Evaluation Method Alone Did Not Drive Cost Overruns

This was the study's most surprising and most robust result.

Across the full sample of 15,477 contracts, **there was no statistically significant difference in cost growth between contracts awarded using the cheapest-bid method and those awarded using the quality-weighing method.**

> **By the numbers:** The statistical test produced a p-value of 0.377. In plain English, this means there is roughly a 38% chance you would see a difference this large (or larger) even if the evaluation method had absolutely no effect on cost growth. By scientific convention, a result needs a p-value below 0.05 -- a less than 5% chance of occurring by accident -- to be considered statistically significant. Our result was nowhere close to that threshold.

In the matched sample -- where we compared only contracts that were similar in size, type, and other characteristics -- the quality-weighing approach was actually associated with *slightly higher* cost growth, though this result was only marginally significant (p = 0.061) and could reflect residual differences between the groups rather than a true effect of the evaluation method.

This finding contradicts a widely held assumption. Many procurement professionals, industry advocates, and congressional staffers have long argued that going with the cheapest bid leads to higher downstream costs. The intuition is compelling: "you get what you pay for." But the data did not support this as a general rule.

**Importantly, this finding was rock-solid.** It held up in:
- The full sample of all 15,477 contracts
- The propensity-score-matched sample
- Subsamples limited to specific agency types
- Subsamples limited to specific industry sectors
- Analyses that excluded statistical outliers
- Every alternative statistical specification we tested

When a finding is this consistent across every test, it warrants serious attention -- even when it challenges conventional wisdom.

### Finding 2: No Difference in Contract Modifications

We also examined whether contracts awarded using the cheapest-bid method required more modifications -- formal changes to the contract terms, scope, price, or schedule.

**The answer: no meaningful difference.**

> **By the numbers:** The statistical test produced a p-value of 0.449. The direction of the estimate was consistent with the theory (slightly fewer modifications for quality-weighing contracts), but the difference was trivially small and statistically indistinguishable from zero.

This finding matters because contract modifications are often cited as the hidden cost of the cheapest-bid approach. The argument goes like this: a contractor that won by bidding unrealistically low will seek to recover its costs through change orders and contract modifications after the work begins. Our data did not support this claim as a general pattern.

This does not mean modifications are irrelevant -- they are a significant administrative burden regardless of evaluation method. But the evaluation method does not appear to be what drives modification frequency.

### Finding 3: Weighing Quality May Attract More Bidders

Here we found an intriguing signal. Contracts that used the quality-weighing approach showed approximately **25% lower odds of receiving only a single bid** compared to cheapest-bid contracts.

> **By the numbers:** The odds ratio was 0.752, with a p-value of 0.085. This means the result fell short of the conventional 0.05 threshold for statistical significance but was close enough to warrant serious attention, particularly given its practical importance.

Why might this happen? The logic is intuitive. When the government announces it will pick the cheapest bidder, some companies look at the solicitation and think: *We can't win a price war against competitors who cut corners on staffing and quality. It's not worth the cost of preparing a proposal.* But when the government says it will consider quality, experience, and technical approach alongside price, more firms see a viable path to winning. They are willing to invest the time and money to compete because they believe their strengths will be recognized.

Single-bid contracts are a persistent problem in federal procurement. When only one company bids, the government has no competitive leverage -- no alternative proposals to compare, no market pressure to keep prices reasonable. If the quality-weighing approach genuinely reduces single-bid rates, even by a modest amount, the implications for taxpayer value are significant.

We categorize this finding as *suggestive but not conclusive*. It deserves further study with larger datasets and longer time horizons.

### Finding 4: Contract Size Is the Key -- Best Value Helps Most on Smaller Contracts

This is the study's strongest and most policy-relevant finding.

The relationship between evaluation method and cost growth **depends on the size of the contract.** We found a statistically significant interaction effect (p = 0.003) between evaluation method and contract size.

Here is what that means in practical terms:

> **For smaller contracts** -- in the range of $250,000 to about $5 million -- the quality-weighing approach was associated with **meaningfully lower cost growth** compared to the cheapest-bid approach.

> **For larger contracts** -- above approximately $10 million -- the advantage disappeared. In fact, at the largest contract sizes, quality-weighing contracts showed slightly *higher* cost growth, though the difference was not large enough to draw firm conclusions.

To visualize this pattern, imagine a graph where the horizontal axis shows contract size and the vertical axis shows the cost-growth advantage of the quality-weighing approach over the cheapest-bid approach. At smaller contract sizes, the line is below zero -- meaning quality-weighing contracts had lower cost growth. As contract size increases, the line slopes upward, crossing zero at some point in the mid-range and eventually moving slightly above zero for the largest contracts.

**Why is this finding so important?** Because it tells policymakers that the evaluation method question is not a one-size-fits-all issue. The right answer depends on the situation -- and the data gives us specific guidance about when each approach works best.

Smaller contracts represent a huge share of federal contracting activity. There are far more contracts in the $250,000 to $5 million range than in the $50 million-plus range. If the quality-weighing approach can reduce cost growth for these smaller contracts, the cumulative impact across the federal government could be substantial.

This finding was consistent across every statistical test we ran and was the one clearly supported hypothesis in the study.

## 6. Why These Results Make Sense

At first glance, the results might seem contradictory. If the evaluation method does not matter overall, how can it matter for smaller contracts? And why would the cheapest-bid approach not lead to worse outcomes, given everything practitioners and theorists have said about it?

A closer look reveals that the results make considerable sense when you consider how the federal procurement system actually works.

### The Federal System Has Many Quality Safeguards

The evaluation method is just one step in a much longer process. Even before a company's proposal is evaluated, the federal system imposes multiple quality screens:

**Responsibility determinations.** Before any contract is awarded, the contracting officer must determine that the company is "responsible" -- meaning it has the financial resources, technical capability, past performance, and organizational integrity to perform the work. This determination filters out the most clearly unqualified bidders regardless of evaluation method.

**Past performance information.** Federal agencies maintain records of contractor performance (in a system called CPARS) and are required to consider past performance in their responsibility determinations and, for tradeoff evaluations, as a formal evaluation factor. Even under LPTA, a contractor with a truly terrible track record may be found non-responsible.

**Small business programs.** Many federal contracts are set aside for small businesses, which limits the competitive field to companies that meet size standards. This does not eliminate quality variation, but it does create a different competitive dynamic than an unrestricted competition.

**Security clearances and certifications.** For many federal contracts, particularly in defense and IT, companies must hold specific security clearances, certifications, or accreditations. These requirements serve as additional quality screens.

**Contract type.** The contract pricing structure (firm fixed price, cost-plus, time and materials) allocates risk between the government and the contractor. A firm fixed-price contract, for example, places cost risk on the contractor, creating incentives for careful pricing regardless of the evaluation method.

The cumulative effect of these safeguards is that the federal procurement system provides a quality floor that does not depend on the evaluation method. The evaluation method sits on top of these other mechanisms. If the system already screens out the worst performers through other means, the incremental benefit of adding quality evaluation to the source selection process may be smaller than theory predicts.

### Contract Management Matters More Than Contractor Selection

A contract's ultimate success or failure depends on what happens *after* the award, not just on how the contractor was chosen. The post-award management environment includes:

- **Active oversight by the contracting officer and the contracting officer's representative** -- the government officials who monitor contractor performance, approve invoices, and manage modifications
- **Regular performance reviews and progress reporting** that can identify problems before they escalate
- **Modification and change order processes** that structure how the contract evolves over time
- **Audit and compliance functions** that check contractor costs and performance

A well-managed LPTA contract can outperform a poorly managed tradeoff contract. The evaluation method matters, but it is not the most important variable. The people managing the contract and the quality of their oversight may be far more consequential.

### Requirements Quality Is Often the Real Problem

Ask any experienced procurement professional what causes contracts to go wrong, and you will hear the same answer: **bad requirements.**

If the government does not clearly define what it wants -- if the statement of work is vague, the performance standards are ambiguous, or the deliverables are poorly specified -- the contract is set up for failure regardless of the evaluation method. Ambiguous requirements lead to misunderstandings, scope disputes, change orders, and cost growth.

A well-written requirement can produce a successful outcome under either LPTA or tradeoff. A poorly written requirement will produce problems under both. The evaluation method cannot compensate for fundamental deficiencies in what the government is asking for.

### The Skilled Workforce Gap

Conducting a rigorous tradeoff evaluation requires significant skill: the ability to develop meaningful evaluation criteria, evaluate complex technical proposals, make defensible tradeoff judgments, and document the rationale. When the acquisition workforce lacks these skills -- due to inexperience, high turnover, or excessive workload -- the tradeoff evaluation may not deliver its potential benefits.

Conversely, an experienced contracting officer using LPTA may write such clear requirements and set such well-calibrated minimum standards that the evaluation method's limitations are minimized. The person matters as much as the process.

The federal acquisition workforce has faced years of staffing challenges. According to multiple government reports, many procurement offices are understaffed, with contracting officers managing far more active contracts than recommended workload standards suggest. In this environment, the choice between LPTA and tradeoff may be less consequential than the question of whether the people running the process have the time, training, and support to do it well.

### Why Bigger Contracts Are Different

The study's strongest finding -- that the quality-weighing approach helps most for smaller contracts -- makes intuitive sense when you consider the differences between small and large procurements:

**Smaller contracts often lack dedicated oversight infrastructure.** A $500,000 consulting contract may not have a full-time program manager, detailed reporting requirements, or multiple layers of review. For these contracts, the evaluation method may be one of the few quality screens available. Getting the right contractor through a tradeoff evaluation can make a real difference in outcomes.

**Larger contracts receive more attention regardless.** A $50 million systems integration contract will typically have a dedicated program manager, regular status reviews, multiple oversight bodies, and senior leadership attention. These management mechanisms can catch problems early and hold contractors accountable whether or not the initial evaluation considered quality.

**Larger contracts are more complex.** Their cost growth may be driven by factors that no evaluation method can address: changing government requirements, technology evolution, shifting political priorities, and the sheer unpredictability of long-duration, high-stakes projects. The evaluation method is one input among many, and its influence may be swamped by these other forces.

**The winner's curse effect.** For very large contracts, the competitive dynamics are different. Fewer companies can compete, the proposals are more complex, and the stakes are higher. At this scale, the tradeoff evaluation process itself adds cost and time, and the marginal benefit of quality screening may not justify the additional investment.

In essence, the evaluation method appears to have the most leverage where it is the primary quality screen -- and that tends to be smaller contracts where other oversight mechanisms are thinner.

## 7. Recommendations for Policymakers

Based on the evidence from this study, we offer seven recommendations for lawmakers, agency leaders, and procurement policy officials.

### Recommendation 1: Do Not Mandate a One-Size-Fits-All Approach

The data does not support requiring agencies to use either the cheapest-bid or the quality-weighing approach for all contracts. A blanket mandate in either direction would be inconsistent with the evidence.

Some advocates have pushed for eliminating LPTA entirely. Others have argued that LPTA should be the default for most procurements. Neither position is supported by the data. The study's key finding -- that the evaluation method matters for some contracts but not others -- argues for a context-dependent approach that gives agencies the flexibility to choose the right method for each situation.

> **For lawmakers:** Instead of legislating which evaluation method agencies must use, focus on ensuring that contracting officers have the training, resources, and authority to make informed decisions. Mandate that agencies document their evaluation method choice and the rationale behind it, but do not dictate the choice itself.

The FAR's existing framework -- which provides guidance on when each method is appropriate while preserving contracting officer discretion -- is fundamentally sound. The problem is not with the rules; it is with how they are implemented in practice.

### Recommendation 2: Target Quality-Weighing Requirements for Smaller and Mid-Size Contracts

The study's strongest finding -- that the quality-weighing approach produces lower cost growth for smaller contracts -- provides actionable guidance for policy.

For contracts in the **$250,000 to $5 million range** in services and IT, the data supports a **presumption in favor of quality-weighing evaluation** unless there is a documented reason to use the cheapest-bid approach. This could be implemented through:

- **Agency procurement guidance** that establishes the quality-weighing approach as the default for service and IT contracts under $5 million, with a written justification required for choosing the cheapest-bid method instead
- **FAR revisions** that supplement the existing guidance with a more explicit statement about the conditions under which quality evaluation adds the most value, citing the empirical evidence on contract size
- **Training programs** that equip contracting officers to conduct efficient tradeoff evaluations for smaller contracts, recognizing that these evaluations need not be as elaborate as those for major programs

The irony of current practice is that the institutional incentives push in the opposite direction: large, high-visibility contracts tend to get the most rigorous evaluations, while smaller contracts -- where the evaluation method appears to matter most -- are more likely to default to LPTA for administrative convenience.

### Recommendation 3: Invest in the Acquisition Workforce

If the evaluation method itself explains only a modest share of the variation in contract outcomes, then something else matters more. The evidence points strongly toward **the people running the process.**

A skilled contracting officer who writes clear requirements, sets appropriate evaluation criteria, assesses proposals thoughtfully, and actively manages contract performance will likely achieve good outcomes regardless of the evaluation method. An undertrained or overworked contracting officer may struggle under either approach.

The federal acquisition workforce faces well-documented challenges:

- **Staffing shortages.** Many agencies report difficulty recruiting and retaining experienced procurement professionals, particularly when the private sector offers higher salaries for the same skill set.
- **Growing workloads.** The number of contracts managed per contracting officer has increased even as workforce sizes have remained flat or declined.
- **Retirement wave.** The most experienced professionals are retiring faster than they can be replaced, taking institutional knowledge with them.
- **Training gaps.** While the Federal Acquisition Institute and the Defense Acquisition University provide training, many contracting officers report that their workloads do not allow adequate time for professional development.

Concrete investments could include:

- **Expanding the Acquisition Workforce Development Fund** to support recruitment, retention bonuses, and training programs
- **Creating fellowship and rotation programs** that expose junior contracting officers to a variety of procurement environments
- **Reducing administrative burden** so contracting officers can spend more time on judgment-intensive tasks like evaluation and oversight, and less time on paperwork and compliance checklists
- **Establishing career pathways** that make procurement a more attractive long-term career in government

The return on investment from workforce improvements may exceed the return from any regulatory change to evaluation procedures.

### Recommendation 4: Improve Post-Award Contract Management

The study found no significant difference in modification rates between the two evaluation methods. This suggests that what happens after the contract is signed may matter more than how the contractor was chosen.

Post-award contract management is the Cinderella of the procurement world -- unglamorous, underfunded, and often overlooked in policy discussions that focus on pre-award reforms. But the evidence suggests that this is where the real leverage lies.

Investments in post-award management could include:

- **Stronger contract oversight staffing.** Ensure that every significant contract has a qualified contracting officer's representative (COR) with adequate time and training to monitor performance.
- **Early warning systems.** Develop tools and processes that flag contracts showing signs of trouble -- unusual modification patterns, spending anomalies, missed milestones -- before problems become crises.
- **Performance management training.** Equip government staff with the skills to hold contractors accountable, manage disputes constructively, and make effective use of contract incentive provisions.
- **Simplified modification processes.** Reduce the administrative friction of routine modifications (like funding increments or administrative changes) so that government staff can focus their time on substantive issues.

> **The bottom line:** The best source selection in the world cannot save a poorly managed contract. And a good contract management team can salvage a contract that started with a less-than-ideal contractor selection.

### Recommendation 5: Make CPARS Data Available for Research

One of the most frustrating limitations of this study -- and of procurement research generally -- is the inability to measure what matters most: **Did the contractor actually do a good job?**

The federal government maintains a **Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS)** in which agencies rate contractors on quality, schedule, cost control, management, and other dimensions. This is exactly the kind of data that could answer the fundamental question: does the quality-weighing approach select contractors that perform better?

But CPARS data is not available to researchers or the public. Access is restricted to government personnel with a need-to-know authorization.

This means the most important question in the evaluation method debate remains empirically unanswered. We can measure cost growth and modifications, but we cannot directly measure whether the work itself was done well.

> **For lawmakers and agency leaders:** Making anonymized, aggregated contractor performance data available for research -- with appropriate privacy protections -- would dramatically improve our ability to answer the questions that matter most. Cost growth is an imperfect proxy for quality. We need to measure quality directly.

Several approaches could work:

- **A formal research data use agreement** modeled on those used for other sensitive government datasets (like IRS data or Census microdata), allowing qualified researchers to access anonymized CPARS data in a secure environment
- **Congressional authorization** for a research initiative that mandates the release of aggregated performance data for procurement research purposes
- **A government-academic partnership** in which agencies collaborate with university researchers under a memorandum of understanding that protects data sensitivity while enabling empirical analysis

The inability to study contractor performance quality is the single biggest blind spot in procurement policy research. Fixing it would benefit not just academic researchers but everyone who makes or influences procurement policy.

### Recommendation 6: Strengthen Requirements Development Processes

Many procurement problems trace back not to the evaluation method or to contractor failure, but to **poorly written requirements.** When the government does not clearly define what it wants, the contract is set up for problems regardless of how the contractor was chosen.

Requirements development is the foundation on which everything else is built. If the requirements are clear, complete, and realistic, the evaluation method can work as intended. If they are vague, contradictory, or unrealistic, no evaluation method can compensate.

Policy improvements could include:

- **Requirements review boards** that provide independent quality checks on statements of work before solicitations are released
- **Templates and best practices** for requirements development in commonly procured service categories, reducing the need for each contracting officer to start from scratch
- **Market research requirements** that ensure agencies understand what the market can deliver and at what cost before they write their requirements
- **Feedback loops** that systematically capture lessons learned from contract performance and feed them back into requirements development for future procurements

### Recommendation 7: Consider Competition Effects, Not Just Cost

The suggestive finding that quality-weighing evaluation may attract more bidders deserves policy attention, even though the result fell just short of conventional statistical significance.

Competition is the engine of value in public procurement. More bidders generally mean better prices, more innovative approaches, and greater accountability. When only one company bids, the government has no leverage.

If the evaluation method influences how many companies compete, that is a policy-relevant consideration that goes beyond the direct cost-growth question. A procurement approach that saves 2% on initial price but drives away half the potential bidders may not serve the taxpayer well in the long run.

> **For policy analysts and oversight bodies:** When evaluating procurement practices, look beyond the price of individual contracts. Examine trends in competition -- how many companies are bidding, how that has changed over time, and whether the evaluation method influences participation. A procurement system that is hemorrhaging competition is a system that is losing value, regardless of what individual contract prices look like.

The Government Accountability Office already tracks single-bid rates as a metric of procurement system health. The marginal finding in this study suggests that this metric may be linked to evaluation method choices in ways that deserve further investigation.

## 8. What We Still Don't Know

Honest research requires honest acknowledgment of its limits. Here is what this study cannot tell us:

### We Could Not Measure Quality Directly

As discussed in the recommendations, the most important outcome -- whether the contractor actually delivered quality work -- was not measurable with available data. CPARS performance ratings, which are the government's own assessment of contractor quality, are not publicly accessible. It is entirely possible that quality-weighing evaluation produces significantly better contractor performance on dimensions we could not measure, even though it did not produce lower cost growth. If so, the full value of the quality-weighing approach would be understated by our analysis.

### We Studied One Quarter of One Fiscal Year

The study analyzed data from a single quarter of FY2024. Patterns may differ across years, economic cycles, or policy regimes. A contract that appears to have low cost growth in early 2024 might accumulate significant overruns by 2027. A multi-year study that tracks contracts from award through completion would provide a more complete picture.

### We Cannot Prove Cause and Effect With Certainty

Propensity score matching and regression analysis are powerful tools, but they are not substitutes for a randomized experiment. There may be factors we could not observe or measure -- the quality of the original requirements, the experience of the contracting officer, the political sensitivity of the procurement -- that influence both the evaluation method choice and the outcomes. Our matching procedure controls for observable differences, but not for these hidden variables.

The moderation finding (that contract size changes the relationship) is robust and unlikely to be explained by hidden bias alone, but strictly speaking, the causal interpretation should be viewed as strongly suggestive rather than proven.

### We Need to Hear From Practitioners

The data can tell us *what* happened but not *why.* To understand the mechanisms -- why contracting officers choose one method over another, how the evaluation process actually affects the proposals they receive, how the initial evaluation influences post-award management -- we need to talk to the people who do this work every day. Surveys of contracting officers, interviews with agency procurement leaders, and case studies of specific contracts would provide the mechanistic understanding that our quantitative analysis cannot.

This study was designed as the first phase of a two-phase research program. The second phase will involve exactly this kind of practitioner engagement. But that work remains to be done.

### The Sample May Not Represent All Federal Procurement

Our sample was limited to competitive service and IT contracts above $250,000. The findings may not apply to commodity purchases, construction contracts, weapons systems, or procurements below the $250,000 threshold. They also may not apply to state, local, or international government procurement, which operates under different legal frameworks and institutional contexts.

---

## 9. Conclusion

This study set out to answer a question that has been debated for decades: Does how the government picks its contractors actually matter?

The answer, based on the analysis of more than 15,000 federal contracts, is nuanced -- and that nuance is itself important.

**The evaluation method is not a silver bullet.** Changing from the cheapest-bid approach to the quality-weighing approach does not, by itself, produce systematically better cost outcomes across the board. The theoretical prediction that cheapest-bid awards lead to higher cost overruns was not supported as a general rule. This is a finding that should give pause to advocates who argue that simply mandating the quality-weighing approach will solve the government's procurement problems.

**But the evaluation method is not irrelevant.** For smaller contracts -- the $250,000 to $5 million range that represents a huge volume of federal contracting activity -- weighing quality alongside price was associated with meaningfully lower cost growth. This is a finding that should give pause to those who argue for expanding the cheapest-bid approach into more areas of procurement.

**Context matters.** The right evaluation method depends on the specific procurement. For smaller service and IT contracts, the quality-weighing approach appears to add genuine value. For larger, more complex contracts, other factors -- the quality of requirements, the skill of the acquisition workforce, the rigor of post-award management -- may be more consequential than the formal evaluation method.

**The system needs investment, not just reform.** The evidence points toward the people and processes surrounding the evaluation method as being at least as important as the method itself. Investing in the acquisition workforce, strengthening requirements development, improving post-award oversight, and making performance data available for research are all interventions that the evidence suggests would yield substantial returns for taxpayers.

The federal government's $700+ billion annual contracting spend is one of the most consequential policy domains in American governance. Decisions about how to award these contracts affect the quality of services that citizens receive, the effectiveness of national defense, the competitiveness of the economy, and the trust that the public places in its government.

For too long, these decisions have been shaped more by institutional inertia, professional intuition, and ideological preference than by rigorous evidence. This study represents a step toward evidence-based procurement policy. The findings are not simple, and they will not satisfy those looking for easy answers. But they point toward a set of reforms -- targeted rather than sweeping, systemic rather than procedural, and grounded in data rather than assumption -- that can help the government get better value for every dollar it spends.

The path to better procurement outcomes does not run through a single policy lever. It runs through the entire system -- from the initial identification of needs, through the development of requirements, the design of the solicitation, the evaluation of proposals, the structuring of the contract, the management of performance, and the assessment of outcomes. Improving this system requires attention to all of these stages, not just the moment when the winning bid is selected.

The taxpayers who fund this system deserve nothing less.

## 10. Glossary

**Acquisition Workforce.** The federal employees -- contracting officers, contracting officer's representatives, program managers, and other procurement professionals -- who plan, manage, and oversee the government's contracts with the private sector.

**Best Value (Tradeoff).** A method of evaluating contractor proposals that considers technical quality, past performance, management capability, and other factors alongside price. The government can choose a higher-priced proposal if it offers meaningfully better quality. Formally authorized under FAR 15.101-1.

**Bid Protest.** A formal challenge by a losing company (or an interested party) to a government contract award decision. Protests are most commonly filed with the Government Accountability Office (GAO), which reviews the agency's process and can sustain the protest if it finds the agency violated procurement rules.

**Change Order.** A formal modification to a contract that changes the scope, price, or terms of the work. Change orders can be initiated by either the government or the contractor and are a common source of cost growth.

**Competition in Contracting Act (CICA).** A 1984 federal law that established full and open competition as the foundational principle of federal procurement. CICA requires agencies to justify any departure from competitive acquisition.

**Contracting Officer (CO).** The government official with the legal authority to enter into, administer, and terminate federal contracts. The CO is the only person authorized to obligate the government on a contract.

**Contracting Officer's Representative (COR).** A government employee designated by the contracting officer to monitor contractor performance, review deliverables, and provide technical oversight. The COR does not have the authority to make contract changes but serves as the day-to-day point of contact between the government and the contractor.

**Cost Growth.** The percentage increase in a contract's total value from the original award amount to the final amount after all modifications. Cost growth can result from scope changes, change orders, price adjustments, or other post-award actions.

**CPARS (Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System).** A federal database where agencies rate contractor performance on dimensions including quality, schedule, cost control, management, and regulatory compliance. Currently restricted to government users and not available for independent research.

**FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation).** The primary body of rules governing how the federal government buys goods and services. The FAR is codified in Title 48 of the Code of Federal Regulations and covers everything from competition requirements to contract types to source selection procedures. Part 15 of the FAR addresses competitive proposals and source selection.

**FPDS (Federal Procurement Data System).** The government-wide database that captures detailed information on every federal contract action above the micro-purchase threshold. FPDS data are made publicly available through USAspending.gov.

**GAO (Government Accountability Office).** An independent, nonpartisan agency that works for Congress to audit and evaluate federal programs and spending. GAO also adjudicates bid protests filed by companies that believe a contract was awarded improperly.

**Indefinite Delivery/Indefinite Quantity (IDIQ) Contract.** A contract vehicle that establishes an overall framework and pricing but does not specify exact quantities or delivery schedules. Work is ordered through individual task orders or delivery orders as needs arise. IDIQ contracts are commonly used for services and IT.

**LPTA (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable).** A method of evaluating contractor proposals where the government sets minimum technical requirements and awards the contract to the lowest-priced bidder that meets them. No credit is given for exceeding the minimum standards. Formally authorized under FAR 15.101-2.

**Modification.** A formal change to a contract after award. Modifications can change the scope, price, schedule, terms, or other aspects of the contract. Types include bilateral modifications (agreed to by both parties) and unilateral modifications (issued by the government under specific contract clauses).

**NAICS Code (North American Industry Classification System).** A standardized numerical system used to classify business establishments by the type of economic activity they perform. In procurement data, NAICS codes identify the industry sector of the work being contracted (e.g., 541511 for Custom Computer Programming Services).

**NDAA (National Defense Authorization Act).** Annual legislation that authorizes funding and sets policy for the Department of Defense and other national security programs. Recent NDAAs have included provisions restricting the use of LPTA for certain types of Defense Department contracts.

**p-value.** A statistical measure indicating how likely it is that an observed result occurred by chance alone. A lower p-value means the result is less likely to be a fluke. By scientific convention, a p-value below 0.05 (a less than 5% chance of occurring by accident) is considered "statistically significant." A p-value above 0.05 means the result could plausibly have occurred by chance and should be interpreted cautiously.

**Propensity Score Matching.** A statistical technique used to make fairer comparisons in observational studies. It works by pairing observations that are similar in all measured characteristics except the variable of interest (in this case, the evaluation method). This mimics some of the benefits of a randomized experiment, controlling for known differences between groups.

**Simplified Acquisition Threshold (SAT).** The dollar value ($250,000 as of 2024) below which agencies may use simplified acquisition procedures rather than the full competitive proposal process. Contracts above the SAT are subject to more rigorous competition, documentation, and oversight requirements.

**Single-Bid Contract.** A contract that received only one proposal in response to a competitive solicitation. Also called a "competition of one." Single-bid outcomes are a concern because the government lacks competitive leverage when only one company offers to do the work.

**Source Selection.** The process by which the government evaluates competing proposals and chooses a contractor for award. The two principal source selection methods are LPTA and best-value tradeoff.

**Statement of Work (SOW) / Performance Work Statement (PWS).** The section of a solicitation that describes what the government wants the contractor to do. The quality and clarity of this document is widely considered one of the most important determinants of contract success or failure.

**USAspending.gov.** The federal government's official public website for tracking government spending, including detailed information on contracts, grants, loans, and other awards. Data from USAspending.gov was the primary data source for this study.
