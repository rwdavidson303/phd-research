# Part 1: Why This Matters

## The Biggest Buyer on Earth

The United States federal government is the largest single purchaser of goods and services on the planet. In fiscal year 2024 alone, the government obligated more than **$700 billion** in contracts with private companies. To put that number in perspective, it is roughly equal to the entire annual economic output of Switzerland. It is more than the combined revenue of Apple, Amazon, and Walmart. If federal contracting were its own economy, it would rank among the twenty largest in the world.

These are not abstract numbers buried in spreadsheets. Federal contracts pay for the information technology systems that process your tax return. They fund the cybersecurity teams that defend power grids, water treatment plants, and financial networks from attack. They support the cloud computing platforms that host census data, weather forecasts, and satellite imagery. They pay for building maintenance at federal courthouses, food services at military installations, consulting work at every cabinet department, and software development across hundreds of agencies.

When these contracts work well, the government gets what it needs at a fair price, and taxpayers get the services they are paying for. When contracts go wrong -- when a software system crashes on launch day, when a construction project stalls at sixty percent completion, when a consulting firm delivers a report that nobody can use -- taxpayers pay twice. Once for the work that failed, and again to fix it or start over. Sometimes they pay three times, because the delay itself causes additional damage: veterans wait longer for benefits, agencies lose productivity, and critical systems remain vulnerable.

Given the stakes, you might assume that the question of *how* the government picks its contractors would be settled science by now. You would be wrong.

## Two Very Different Ways to Pick a Contractor

When a federal agency needs to hire a company to do something -- build a system, provide a service, deliver consulting expertise -- it issues a solicitation, which is essentially a detailed job posting for companies. Interested firms submit proposals, which are their applications. The agency then has to decide which company gets the contract.

There are two main methods for making that decision. Understanding the difference between them is the key to understanding everything in this research.

### Method One: Go With the Cheapest Bid

The first method is formally called **Lowest Price Technically Acceptable**, or LPTA. The name is descriptive. The agency sets a bar -- a list of minimum requirements that any contractor must meet. Every company that clears that bar is considered "technically acceptable." Among those acceptable companies, the one that offers the lowest price wins. Period. It does not matter if one company has twenty years of experience and another has two. It does not matter if one company has a brilliant technical approach and another has a bare-minimum plan. If both meet the minimum requirements, the cheaper one wins.

Imagine you need to hire a plumber to fix a leak in your kitchen. Under this method, you would write down your minimum requirements: must be licensed, must be insured, must be available within a week. You would get quotes from every plumber who meets those criteria. You would hire whichever one charges the least. You would not look at online reviews, ask for references, or consider whether the plumber has experience with your type of plumbing. Price is all that matters.

The appeal of this approach is obvious. It is simple, fast, transparent, and hard to argue with. The rules are clear. The winner is objective. Nobody can claim the decision was biased or based on some fuzzy judgment call. And in theory, it should save the government money because it always gets the lowest price.

### Method Two: Weigh Quality Alongside Price

The second method is formally called **Best Value Tradeoff**. Under this approach, the agency evaluates companies on multiple factors -- their technical approach, their track record on similar work, their management plan, the qualifications of their proposed staff, their understanding of the problem -- alongside price. The agency then makes a judgment: which proposal offers the best overall value?

This means a company that costs somewhat more can still win if its proposal is substantially stronger. The agency might conclude that Company A's proposal at $2.1 million is a better deal than Company B's proposal at $1.8 million because Company A has a more experienced team, a more realistic schedule, and a track record of delivering similar work on time and on budget. The agency is not just buying a commodity; it is making an investment decision.

Back to the plumber analogy: under this method, you would still get multiple quotes, but you would also read online reviews, check references, look at photos of past work, and consider whether the plumber has experience with your specific type of problem. You might pay a bit more for the plumber with a stellar reputation and twenty years of kitchen plumbing experience, because you believe the total value -- including the likelihood of getting it right the first time -- is worth the extra cost.

Most people instinctively use this approach when spending their own money on anything important. When you buy a car, you do not simply buy the cheapest one on the lot. When you choose a surgeon, you do not pick whoever charges the least. When you hire a contractor to renovate your home, you weigh experience, reputation, and approach against price.

There is an important nuance here. The cheapest-bid approach is not inherently bad. For certain types of purchases, it makes perfect sense. If you need 500 reams of copy paper and every brand meets your quality standard, why would you pay extra? If you need a janitor for a small office and every licensed cleaning company offers essentially the same service, picking the cheapest one is rational. The method was designed for commodities -- standard, interchangeable products and services where quality does not vary much from one provider to another.

The problem arises when the method is applied to work that is not a commodity. Software development is not a commodity. Cybersecurity is not a commodity. Management consulting is not a commodity. These are complex services where the quality of the work varies enormously depending on the skill, experience, and commitment of the people doing it. When the government treats complex professional services like copy paper -- evaluating only whether the contractor meets a minimum standard and then picking the cheapest one -- the concern is that it invites exactly the kind of mediocre performance that costs more in the long run.

The question at the center of this research is whether the federal government should do the same -- and whether it actually makes a difference when it does.

## Why Congress and Watchdogs Are Worried

This is not a new debate. It has been simmering for decades, and in recent years, it has reached a rolling boil.

The concern from Congress, government watchdogs, and industry groups follows a straightforward logic: if you always go with the cheapest bid, you get what you pay for. The contractor who wins by cutting their price to the bone may have done so by planning to use less experienced staff, skimping on quality controls, or deliberately underbidding with the expectation of making up the difference through contract changes after the work starts. The initial "savings" from the low price may be an illusion -- overwhelmed by cost overruns, delays, and poor performance down the road.

The Government Accountability Office, or GAO -- Congress's investigative arm -- has documented numerous cases where lowest-price awards led to troubled contracts. Industry trade associations like the Professional Services Council have argued that the cheapest-bid approach drives away top-tier companies that cannot compete on price alone and do not want to cut corners. Several former heads of the Office of Federal Procurement Policy have called for limiting its use.

Congress has acted on these concerns. The National Defense Authorization Act -- the annual law that funds the military -- has repeatedly included provisions restricting when the Department of Defense can use the cheapest-bid method for certain types of contracts, particularly for services and IT work. The logic behind these restrictions is that complex services are not commodities, and treating them as interchangeable except for price leads to bad outcomes.

But here is the problem: almost all of this concern is based on anecdotes, case studies, expert opinion, and surveys of procurement professionals. Ask a contracting officer whether the cheapest-bid approach leads to problems, and most will say yes -- but that is an opinion, not evidence. Point to a specific contract that went badly after a lowest-price award, and critics will note that plenty of best-value awards also go badly.

What has been almost entirely missing from the debate is **rigorous, large-scale empirical evidence**. Nobody had taken a large, representative sample of federal contracts and systematically compared what happens after agencies use one method versus the other. Nobody had controlled for the fact that agencies tend to use the cheapest-bid approach for simpler work and the best-value approach for complex work -- a difference that would make any simple comparison misleading.

That is what this research set out to do.

To appreciate the urgency of the question, consider just a few examples from recent years. The Department of Veterans Affairs' attempt to modernize its electronic health records -- one of the largest IT contracts in government history -- has been plagued by billions in cost overruns and repeated delays. The Internal Revenue Service has struggled with IT modernization contracts that failed to deliver promised capabilities. The Department of Defense has documented weapons systems programs that exceeded their original budgets by tens of billions of dollars. In each case, analysts debated whether a different evaluation approach might have produced better results.

These failures are not the norm -- most federal contracts are completed without making headlines. But they illustrate the stakes. When a major contract fails, the consequences cascade through the government and the public it serves: veterans wait longer for care, taxpayers file returns on obsolete systems, and military readiness suffers. Even modest improvements in the contract outcomes across the full portfolio of federal spending would yield billions in value.

The debate over evaluation methods is, at its heart, a debate about whether the government can improve its batting average on contracts. Can it fail less often, spend less on corrections, and get better results? Our research cannot answer all of those questions, but it provides the first large-scale evidence on whether the way the government selects its contractors is part of the answer.

## The Questions We Asked

This study was designed to answer a specific set of questions, each testing a common belief about how the choice of evaluation method affects contract outcomes:

**Question 1: Does using best value instead of the cheapest-bid approach lead to lower cost overruns?** This is the core question. If you weigh quality alongside price, do you end up spending less in the long run because the work goes more smoothly? Or does paying a higher initial price simply mean paying more, period?

**Question 2: Does the evaluation method affect how often contracts need to be changed after they are signed?** Contract modifications -- formal changes to the terms, scope, or price of a contract -- are a common feature of federal contracting. The concern is that cheapest-bid contracts need more modifications because the original price was unrealistically low or because the contractor's bare-minimum approach creates problems that require fixes.

**Question 3: Does the evaluation method affect how many companies bother to compete?** If the government says it will pick the cheapest acceptable bid, do fewer companies submit proposals? The concern is that quality-focused companies sit out competitions they believe they cannot win on price alone, leaving the government with fewer choices and less competition.

**Question 4: Does the size of the contract change the answer?** A $300,000 contract for basic help desk support is a very different animal from a $50 million contract for a complex IT system integration. Does the evaluation method matter more for one type than the other?

These are not idle academic questions. Real policy decisions are being made right now based on beliefs about the answers. If those beliefs are wrong -- or even just incomplete -- the government could be wasting billions of dollars, either by using the wrong evaluation method or by spending political energy fighting about a choice that does not actually matter much.

# Part 2: What Experts Have Said

## Why Best Value Should Work Better (In Theory)

Before we get to what the data actually showed, it helps to understand why most experts expected best value to produce better outcomes. There are two main theoretical arguments, both well-established in academic research.

### The Theory of Transaction Costs

The first argument comes from a branch of economics called **transaction cost economics**, developed primarily by economist Oliver Williamson, who won the Nobel Prize for this work in 2009. The basic idea is deceptively simple, and once you understand it, you see it everywhere.

Whenever two parties enter into a deal -- whether it is a homeowner hiring a roofer, a company hiring an employee, or a government agency hiring a contractor -- there are costs beyond the sticker price. These are called "transaction costs," and they come in several flavors:

**Search costs:** The cost of finding potential contractors, writing the solicitation, and evaluating proposals. This is like the time you spend researching plumbers, reading reviews, and getting quotes before you hire one.

**Bargaining costs:** The cost of negotiating the deal, agreeing on terms, and writing up the contract. The more complex the work, the harder it is to spell out exactly what you want and exactly what the contractor will deliver.

**Monitoring costs:** The cost of keeping an eye on the work after the contract starts. Is the contractor doing what they promised? Are they cutting corners? Are they using the qualified staff they proposed?

**Enforcement costs:** The cost of dealing with problems -- contract disputes, performance failures, cost overruns, and the legal or administrative machinery required to resolve them.

Williamson's key insight was that these costs are much higher when three conditions exist:

First, **the work is complex and hard to define precisely in advance**. Writing a contract for 10,000 widgets is straightforward. Writing a contract for "modernize our IT infrastructure" is enormously difficult because nobody can anticipate every technical challenge, every design decision, or every change in requirements that will arise over a multi-year project.

Second, **only a few companies can do the work**. When many suppliers exist and switching from one to another is easy, competition keeps everyone honest. When only a handful of companies have the expertise, each one has more leverage.

Third, **the contractor may have incentives that conflict with the buyer's interests**. This is the problem economists call "opportunism." A contractor that won by bidding the lowest price has every incentive to minimize costs during performance -- using less experienced staff, doing the bare minimum, and looking for opportunities to charge extra for anything not explicitly covered in the contract.

Here is the connection to government procurement: the cheapest-bid method assumes that you can specify exactly what you want in advance, that many companies can do the work equally well, and that price is the only thing that varies. In other words, it assumes that transaction costs are low. For simple, well-defined, commodity-type purchases -- buying office supplies, routine maintenance on a standard building, basic data entry services -- this assumption is often reasonable.

But for complex services and IT work -- software development, systems integration, management consulting, cybersecurity -- the assumption breaks down. The work is hard to define precisely. Not all contractors are equally capable. And the cheapest contractor may be cheap precisely because they plan to cut corners or underbid strategically.

**Transaction cost economics predicts that best value should produce better outcomes for complex work** because it allows the government to select contractors based on their ability to manage complexity, not just their willingness to offer a low price. By evaluating technical approach, past performance, and management capability, the government can reduce the risk of the hidden costs that plague complex contracts.

Think of it this way: if you are buying a bag of rice, the cheapest-bid approach is perfectly fine. Rice is rice. But if you are hiring someone to build a custom home, you would not pick the architect based solely on who charges the least. The architect's skill, experience, and understanding of your needs will determine whether you end up with your dream home or a construction nightmare. The "transaction costs" of a bad architect -- redesigns, delays, change orders, disputes -- would dwarf any savings on the initial fee.

### The Theory of Public Value

The second theoretical argument comes from the field of public administration, particularly the work of scholar Mark Moore at Harvard's Kennedy School. Moore developed a framework called **public value theory**, which argues that government should be evaluated not just on whether it saves money but on whether it creates value for the public.

In the private sector, value is relatively easy to measure: profit. A business that generates more revenue than it spends is creating value for its owners. But government exists for different reasons. The value of a well-functioning veterans' health system cannot be reduced to its cost. The value of a secure power grid is not just the price of the cybersecurity contract.

Public value theory suggests that the cheapest-bid approach systematically undervalues the non-price dimensions of contractor performance. When the government selects a contractor solely on price, it is implicitly saying that all technically acceptable proposals deliver the same value to the public. But anyone who has worked with both excellent and mediocre contractors knows this is not true.

A cybersecurity firm that proactively identifies and addresses vulnerabilities creates more public value than one that meets the minimum requirements but misses emerging threats. A consulting firm that transfers knowledge to government employees and builds internal capacity creates more value than one that generates a report, collects its fee, and leaves. These differences in value are invisible to the cheapest-bid approach.

The implication of public value theory is that best value should not just be about getting a lower price; it should be about getting better outcomes for the public. And those outcomes include things that are hard to measure but genuinely important: innovation, knowledge transfer, responsiveness, and the long-term health of the contractor marketplace.

### Where the Two Theories Converge

Both theories arrive at the same practical conclusion from different directions:

- Transaction cost economics says best value should reduce the hidden costs that arise from complex contracting.
- Public value theory says best value should produce better outcomes by considering dimensions of performance beyond price.

Together, they provide a strong theoretical case that best value should outperform the cheapest-bid approach, at least for complex services and IT work. This theoretical case is why Congress, industry groups, and many procurement experts have pushed for expanding the use of best value and restricting the use of the cheapest-bid approach.

But theories, no matter how logical, are not evidence. The question is whether these predictions hold up when tested against real-world data. As we will see, the answer is more complicated than anyone expected.

## What Previous Researchers Found

The theoretical case for best value is compelling. But what did previous studies actually find when they looked at real contracts?

The short answer: mixed results from small samples, with significant methodological limitations. Here is a summary of the most important prior work:

### The Government Accountability Office Studies

The GAO, Congress's investigative arm, has published several reports examining the use of LPTA (the cheapest-bid method) across the federal government. The most significant was a 2014 report that surveyed contracting officers, reviewed contract files, and examined the frequency of LPTA use across agencies.

The GAO found that LPTA was being used extensively -- even for complex services where many procurement professionals believed it was inappropriate. Contracting officers reported that LPTA was sometimes selected not because it was the right method for the work, but because it was faster and simpler, or because their agency had a culture that defaulted to the cheapest option.

However, the GAO studies were primarily descriptive. They documented how often LPTA was used and gathered opinions about its appropriateness. They did not systematically compare outcomes -- cost overruns, modifications, competition -- between LPTA and best-value contracts. They told us that people were worried about LPTA's effects, but not whether those worries were justified by the data.

### The Defense Acquisition Research

Within the Department of Defense -- the government's largest buyer -- several researchers have examined how source selection methods affect outcomes. The RAND Corporation, a prominent research organization, published studies in 2015 and 2017 examining defense acquisition programs. Their work found that complex weapons systems and IT programs frequently experienced cost overruns regardless of the source selection method, suggesting that the choice of evaluation method was not the primary driver of cost growth.

Researchers at the Naval Postgraduate School conducted studies of defense service contracts and found that contracts awarded using LPTA tended to be modified more frequently than those awarded using best value. However, these studies typically involved samples of a few hundred contracts at most, and they did not always account for the fact that LPTA and best-value contracts are systematically different in ways that could explain the outcomes.

### The Underbidding Problem

A particularly important strand of research involves what procurement professionals call **"buying in"** -- the practice of deliberately submitting an unrealistically low bid to win a contract, with the expectation of recovering the true costs through changes and modifications after the work starts.

Researchers have studied this problem for decades. The logic is straightforward: under LPTA, where price is the only differentiator, the incentive to underbid is strongest. A company knows that the lowest price wins, so it has every reason to shave its estimate as thin as possible. If the company wins and then encounters problems -- as it almost certainly will on complex work -- it can request contract modifications to cover the additional costs. By then, the government has already invested in the relationship, switching to a new contractor would be expensive and disruptive, and the government often has little choice but to approve the modifications.

Under best value, the argument goes, this incentive is weaker because the government evaluates the realism of the proposed price alongside the technical approach. A company that bids an unrealistically low price may actually lose points in the evaluation because the government's evaluators may conclude that the low price reflects an unrealistic understanding of the work.

The evidence on underbidding is largely anecdotal and based on case studies. Several researchers have documented specific contracts where buying in clearly occurred, but nobody had quantified how often it happens or whether it is truly more common under LPTA.

### The Competition Paradox

Another important line of research involves competition -- or the lack of it. The conventional wisdom is that competition is good for taxpayers because it forces companies to offer their best price and best approach. The cheapest-bid method is designed to maximize the role of price competition.

But several researchers have identified what they call a **"competition paradox"**: the cheapest-bid approach may actually reduce competition by driving away capable firms. The argument is that companies which invest in experienced personnel, innovative methods, and strong management systems cannot compete on price alone against companies willing to cut corners. Rather than enter a competition they believe they will lose on price, these higher-quality firms simply do not bid.

Survey data supports this concern. Studies by the Professional Services Council and other industry groups have found that many companies report declining to bid on LPTA solicitations because they cannot win on price without compromising quality. If this is true at scale, the cheapest-bid approach could be counterproductive: by driving away bidders, it reduces the very competition it is supposed to promote, leaving the government with fewer choices and potentially higher long-run costs.

However, these surveys capture perceptions and self-reported behavior, not actual bidding data. Nobody had tested whether LPTA solicitations actually receive fewer bids using a large sample of real federal contracts. This was one of the questions our study aimed to answer.

### The Incumbency Problem

A related concern involves incumbency. Once a company wins a federal contract, it develops deep knowledge of the government's systems, processes, and needs. When the contract comes up for renewal, the incumbent contractor has a significant advantage over competitors -- it knows the work, it has relationships with the agency's staff, and it can propose a more efficient approach because it has already learned on the government's dime.

Under the cheapest-bid approach, this incumbency advantage can distort competition. The incumbent may be able to bid lower than competitors not because it is more efficient, but because it has already absorbed the startup costs on the previous contract. Competitors, facing the prospect of startup costs that the incumbent does not bear, may decide not to compete.

Under best value, the incumbency advantage takes a different form. The incumbent's track record on the current contract can be evaluated as part of its past performance, which may or may not favor the incumbent depending on how well it has performed. In theory, this creates a healthier dynamic: incumbents earn an advantage through good performance, not just through the accident of being the current contractor.

Research on incumbency in federal contracting is limited but growing. Several studies have found that a high percentage of federal contracts are won by incumbent contractors, particularly in services and IT, but the research has not clearly established whether this pattern differs between LPTA and best-value awards.

### The Protest Problem

One more piece of the puzzle deserves mention: bid protests. When a company loses a federal contract competition and believes the decision was flawed, it can file a protest with the GAO, the Court of Federal Claims, or the contracting agency itself. Protests delay the start of work, consume government resources, and create uncertainty.

Some procurement professionals argue that the cheapest-bid approach reduces protests because the decision is objective and hard to challenge -- the lowest price wins, and everyone can see the numbers. Best-value decisions, by contrast, involve subjective judgment (which proposal is "best overall") and are therefore more vulnerable to challenge.

The data on this is mixed. The GAO reports that the protest sustain rate -- the percentage of protests where the GAO finds that the government made an error -- has remained relatively stable over time at around 15 to 20 percent. Some studies have found that best-value contracts are protested more frequently, but others have found no significant difference. The relationship between evaluation method and protests was not a primary focus of our study, but it is part of the broader context.

### International Approaches

The United States is not alone in grappling with these questions. Governments around the world face the same fundamental challenge: how to balance price and quality in public procurement.

**The European Union** has moved decisively toward best value. The EU's 2014 procurement directives -- which govern public purchasing across all member states -- explicitly encourage the use of "most economically advantageous tender" (MEAT), which is the EU equivalent of best value. The directives discourage using price as the sole criterion, particularly for complex services. Several EU member states have gone further, essentially banning the use of lowest-price-only evaluation for contracts above certain thresholds.

**The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)**, a group of 38 developed countries, has published extensive guidance on public procurement. The OECD's Principles for Public Procurement recommend considering "value for money" rather than "lowest price," particularly for complex purchases. The OECD has documented case studies from member countries showing both successes and failures of various procurement approaches.

**Australia** has implemented "value for money" as the core principle of its Commonwealth Procurement Rules. Australian procurement explicitly requires agencies to consider quality, risk, and fitness for purpose alongside price. The Australian model is often cited as an example of a system that has moved beyond the lowest-price debate.

**The United Kingdom** overhauled its procurement system after Brexit, implementing the Procurement Act 2023, which emphasizes "value for money" assessments that consider the full lifecycle of a purchase, not just the initial price. The UK's experience with the Private Finance Initiative (PFI) -- a program that often awarded complex government contracts based heavily on price -- served as a cautionary tale. Many PFI contracts experienced significant cost overruns and quality problems, contributing to the UK's shift toward more holistic evaluation methods.

The international trend is clearly toward best value and away from lowest-price-only evaluation. But the evidence base for this trend is largely theoretical and anecdotal. Few countries have conducted the kind of large-scale empirical comparison that this study attempted.

### The Principal-Agent Problem: A Hidden Dynamic

There is one more theoretical concept that helps explain why the evaluation method debate is so difficult to resolve, and it is worth understanding because it runs through every aspect of government contracting.

In economics, the **principal-agent problem** describes what happens when one party (the "principal") hires another party (the "agent") to do something, but the two parties have different interests and the principal cannot perfectly observe what the agent is doing.

In government contracting, the principal is the government (and ultimately the taxpayer), and the agent is the contractor. The government wants high-quality work at a fair price. The contractor wants to maximize its profit. These goals are not entirely aligned. The contractor may have incentives to cut corners where the government is not watching, to use less experienced staff than proposed, or to interpret ambiguous contract language in its own favor.

The evaluation method affects how this dynamic plays out. Under LPTA, the government has limited information about the contractor beyond its price and its ability to meet minimum requirements. The contractor knows that the government selected it for being cheap, not for being good. This can create a psychological dynamic in which both sides treat the relationship as purely transactional: the contractor delivers the minimum, and the government monitors for compliance rather than performance.

Under best value, the relationship starts differently. The government has evaluated the contractor's technical approach, past performance, and management capability. The contractor knows it was selected because the government believed it could deliver high-quality work. This can create a more collaborative dynamic in which both sides are invested in success.

Whether this theoretical difference translates into measurable outcome differences is, of course, exactly what our study tested. The point here is that the principal-agent dynamic adds a layer of complexity to the evaluation method debate that simple cost comparisons do not capture. The relationship between the government and the contractor is not just a transaction; it is an ongoing partnership that can function well or poorly depending on factors that go far beyond the initial selection decision.

### The "Race to the Bottom" Concern

One of the most persistent concerns in the procurement community is that the cheapest-bid approach creates a **"race to the bottom"** -- a dynamic in which companies compete by cutting costs rather than improving quality, driving prices below sustainable levels.

The race-to-the-bottom argument works like this: when the government announces that it will pick the cheapest acceptable bidder, every company has an incentive to find ways to lower its price. Some of these cost reductions are genuine efficiencies. But others come from reducing staff, lowering salaries, hiring less experienced people, eliminating quality checks, or underfunding management systems. The companies that are most aggressive about cutting costs win the most contracts, forcing their competitors to match those cuts or exit the market.

Over time, this dynamic can erode the quality of the entire contractor base. The best firms leave the market because they cannot compete without compromising their standards. The remaining firms specialize in winning low-price competitions, not in delivering excellent work. The government ends up with a contractor pool that is optimized for bidding low rather than performing well.

This concern is supported by economic theory (it is a well-known feature of markets where quality is hard to observe) and by anecdotal evidence from contracting professionals. But it is extremely difficult to test empirically because the effects play out over years and across the entire market, not within individual contracts. Our study could not directly test the race-to-the-bottom hypothesis, but it provides context for understanding why many procurement professionals are concerned about the long-term effects of LPTA even if the short-term outcomes appear similar.

### The Gap This Research Fills

Looking across the existing literature, a clear gap emerges:

**Plenty of theory** supports the idea that best value should produce better outcomes for complex work. Transaction cost economics and public value theory both predict it. The logic is compelling.

**Plenty of opinions** agree. Surveys of contracting officers, industry groups, and procurement experts consistently report that most professionals believe best value is the better approach for complex services.

**Plenty of case studies** document specific contracts where the cheapest-bid approach led to problems.

**But almost no large-scale empirical evidence** actually tests whether these beliefs hold up when you analyze thousands of real contracts using rigorous statistical methods. The few quantitative studies that exist typically involve small samples (hundreds of contracts, not thousands), focus on a single agency or commodity type, and do not adequately control for the systematic differences between LPTA and best-value contracts.

This is the gap our research fills. We analyzed more than 15,000 federal contracts using methods specifically designed to make fair comparisons. What we found challenged some deeply held assumptions -- and confirmed others in unexpected ways.

## Theories That Guided Our Hypotheses

Based on the theoretical frameworks and prior research, we entered this study with several predictions. In academic language, these are called hypotheses. Here is what we expected:

**We expected best value to produce lower cost growth.** If best-value evaluation selects more capable contractors and reduces underbidding, contracts awarded through best value should experience fewer cost overruns.

**We expected best value to produce fewer contract modifications.** If better initial selection leads to more realistic pricing and more capable contractors, there should be less need to modify the contract after award.

**We expected best value to attract more competition.** If quality-focused companies avoid cheapest-bid competitions, then best-value solicitations should attract more bidders.

**We expected contract size to matter.** The theoretical case for best value is strongest for complex, high-stakes work. For smaller, simpler contracts, the cheapest-bid approach might work just fine because the work is more predictable and easier to specify.

These were not just our predictions. They reflect the conventional wisdom among procurement experts, the theoretical consensus among researchers, and the logic behind Congressional restrictions on LPTA. We designed the study specifically to test whether this conventional wisdom was supported by the data.

As you will see in Part 4, the data had other ideas.

# Part 3: How We Did the Research

## Where the Data Came From

All of the data in this study came from a single source: **USAspending.gov**, a publicly accessible website maintained by the U.S. Department of the Treasury. USAspending.gov is the government's most comprehensive database of federal spending, and it is available to anyone with an internet connection. You can go to the site right now and look up individual contracts.

The database includes detailed information about every federal contract award, including who the contractor is, how much the contract was worth, what type of work it involves, which agency awarded it, what evaluation method was used, and how many companies submitted proposals. It also tracks modifications -- changes to the contract after it was awarded -- and the current dollar value of the contract, which may differ from the original award if modifications have increased or decreased the scope.

We focused on contracts from **fiscal year 2024** (October 2023 through September 2024) to ensure we were studying current procurement practices. We limited the sample to contracts meeting several criteria:

- **Competitive contracts only.** We excluded contracts that were awarded without competition, such as sole-source contracts, because our questions only apply when the government has a choice among multiple contractors. You cannot study the effect of the selection method if there was no selection to make.

- **Service and IT contracts only.** We excluded contracts for physical goods (like vehicles or office supplies) because the evaluation method debate centers on services and IT, where the work is complex and hard to define. Nobody seriously argues about whether to use best value to buy paperclips.

- **Contracts above $250,000.** We set a minimum threshold because very small contracts are handled through simplified acquisition procedures where the LPTA-versus-best-value distinction is less meaningful. The $250,000 threshold ensures we are studying substantive contracts where the choice of evaluation method is a deliberate decision.

After applying these filters, we had a dataset of **15,477 contracts**. This is, to the best of our knowledge, the largest sample ever used to study this question in the published academic literature. Previous studies typically examined a few hundred contracts at most. Our sample is roughly fifty to a hundred times larger than most prior work.

## What We Measured

To answer our research questions, we needed to measure several things about each contract. In research terminology, these are called "variables." Here is what we looked at and why:

### The Key Question: Which Evaluation Method Was Used?

For each contract, USAspending.gov records whether the agency used LPTA (cheapest bid) or best value (tradeoff) to evaluate proposals. This is our **independent variable** -- the thing we think might be causing different outcomes. Of the 15,477 contracts in our sample, approximately 32 percent used LPTA and 68 percent used best value.

### Outcome 1: Cost Growth

Our primary measure of contract success is **cost growth** -- how much the final cost of the contract exceeded the original award amount. We calculated this as a percentage: if a contract was originally awarded at $1 million and the current obligated amount is $1.15 million, the cost growth is 15 percent.

Cost growth captures the phenomenon that concerns taxpayers most directly: you thought you were paying X dollars, but you actually ended up paying more. Some cost growth is normal and expected -- contracts are modified for legitimate reasons, like adding new work or extending the period of performance. But excessive cost growth may signal problems with the original contractor selection, poor contract management, or strategic underbidding.

### Outcome 2: Contract Modifications

Our second measure is the **number of modifications** to each contract. Every time a contract is formally changed -- whether to adjust the scope, change the price, extend the deadline, or make administrative corrections -- that change is recorded as a modification. We counted the total number of modifications for each contract.

More modifications are not necessarily bad (sometimes they reflect a healthy, adaptive relationship between the government and the contractor), but a high modification count can also indicate problems: unclear original requirements, underbidding that required correction, or scope creep that was not anticipated at the time of award.

### Outcome 3: Competition

Our third measure is whether the contract received only a single bid -- what procurement professionals call a **"single-offer" outcome**. When only one company submits a proposal in response to a competitive solicitation, the government loses the benefits of competition. There is no price comparison, no technical comparison, and no negotiating leverage. The government must either accept the single offer or cancel the solicitation and start over.

We coded each contract as either single-offer (only one bid received) or multi-offer (two or more bids received), and then analyzed whether the evaluation method affected the odds of a single-offer outcome.

### What Else Might Explain the Differences?

Here is where the research gets tricky. If we simply compared the average cost growth of LPTA contracts to the average cost growth of best-value contracts, we would likely get a misleading answer. Why? Because the two groups of contracts are systematically different.

Agencies tend to use LPTA for simpler, more routine work and best value for complex, high-stakes projects. Best-value contracts tend to be larger, involve more complex services, and be awarded by agencies with larger acquisition workforces. If we found that best-value contracts had more cost growth, it might not be because best value is a worse method -- it might be because best-value contracts are more complex to begin with, and complex contracts are simply harder to manage.

To make fair comparisons, we needed to account for these differences. In research terms, we needed **control variables** -- other factors that might explain the outcomes and that differ between LPTA and best-value contracts. Our control variables included:

- **Award amount:** The original dollar value of the contract. Larger contracts may naturally experience more cost growth simply because there is more money involved and more opportunity for changes.

- **Contract type:** Whether the contract is a firm-fixed-price contract (the price is set at award and does not change), a cost-reimbursement contract (the government pays the contractor's actual costs plus a fee), or something in between. Contract type significantly affects cost risk.

- **Service category:** What type of work the contract involves -- IT services, professional services, facilities maintenance, etc. Different types of work carry different levels of complexity and risk.

- **Agency:** Which federal agency awarded the contract. Agencies have very different procurement cultures, workforce capabilities, and contracting practices.

- **Number of offers received:** How many companies submitted proposals. More competition might lead to better outcomes regardless of the evaluation method.

- **Period of performance:** How long the contract is expected to last. Longer contracts have more time for modifications and cost changes.

- **Small business status:** Whether the contract was set aside for small businesses or competed among all sizes. Small business contracts may have different dynamics than full-and-open competitions.

By accounting for all of these factors, we can isolate the effect of the evaluation method itself -- asking, in essence: among contracts that are similar in size, type, complexity, and agency, does it make a difference whether the government used LPTA or best value?

## How We Made Fair Comparisons

Accounting for control variables through statistical models is the standard approach, and we used it. But we also employed an additional technique called **propensity score matching** (PSM) that is specifically designed for situations like ours. This technique deserves a detailed explanation because it is central to our most important results.

### The Problem: We Cannot Run an Experiment

In an ideal world, we would test the effect of evaluation method the same way pharmaceutical companies test new drugs: a randomized controlled trial. We would take a large group of contracts, randomly assign half to use LPTA and half to use best value, and then compare the outcomes. Random assignment would ensure that the two groups were identical in every way except the evaluation method, so any differences in outcomes could be confidently attributed to the method itself.

But we cannot randomly assign evaluation methods to real government contracts. Agencies choose their evaluation method for reasons related to the specific work being procured, and it would be neither practical nor legal to override those choices for research purposes. This means we are stuck with what researchers call an **observational study**: we observe what happened rather than controlling what happened.

The challenge with observational studies is that the "treatment" (in our case, the choice of evaluation method) is not random. It is related to other characteristics of the contract. This is called **selection bias**, and it is the central methodological challenge of this research.

### The Solution: Match Similar Contracts

Propensity score matching addresses selection bias by creating artificial comparison groups that mimic what a randomized experiment would have produced. Here is how it works, explained through a medical analogy:

Imagine you want to study whether a particular drug reduces blood pressure. You cannot run a randomized trial, so you have to work with medical records of patients who happened to take the drug and patients who did not. But the patients who took the drug are different from those who did not -- they may be older, sicker, or more likely to have a family history of hypertension. Simply comparing blood pressure between the two groups would confuse the effect of the drug with the effects of age, severity, and genetics.

Propensity score matching solves this by calculating, for each patient, the probability that they would have been prescribed the drug based on their age, health status, family history, and other characteristics. This probability is called the **propensity score**. Then, each patient who took the drug is matched with a patient who did not take the drug but who had a very similar propensity score -- meaning they were equally likely to have been prescribed the drug based on their observable characteristics. By comparing outcomes within these matched pairs, you can estimate the effect of the drug itself, separate from the effects of the characteristics that influenced who received it.

We applied exactly the same logic to contracts. For each contract in our dataset, we calculated the probability that it would use best-value evaluation based on its characteristics: award amount, contract type, service category, agency, and other factors. Then we matched each best-value contract with an LPTA contract that had a very similar probability. The result was a set of **1,093 matched pairs** -- 2,186 contracts that were similar in their observable characteristics but differed in the evaluation method used.

How well did the matching work? We assess the quality of matching using a measure called **standardized bias**, which tells us how different the two groups are on each characteristic. Before matching, the groups differed substantially -- best-value contracts were significantly larger, more likely to be in certain service categories, and concentrated in certain agencies. After matching, the differences shrank dramatically. **Balance improved by 57 to 80 percent on most characteristics**, meaning the matched groups were far more comparable than the unmatched groups. The matching was not perfect (it never is in observational studies), but it was good enough to substantially reduce the risk that our results are driven by differences between the groups rather than differences in the evaluation method.

## The Statistical Tests We Used

With the data assembled and the comparison groups constructed, we needed statistical tools to analyze the results. We used three main types of analysis, each appropriate for a different type of outcome. Here is what they are and why we used them:

### For Cost Growth: Ordinary Least Squares Regression (OLS)

Cost growth is a continuous number -- it can be 0 percent, 5 percent, 17 percent, or any other value. For continuous outcomes, the standard tool is **ordinary least squares regression**, or OLS. In simple terms, OLS estimates a mathematical relationship between the factors we think might matter (evaluation method, contract size, service type, etc.) and the outcome (cost growth).

Think of it like this: imagine you are trying to predict how much a house will sell for. You know that bigger houses sell for more, houses in good school districts sell for more, and newer houses sell for more. OLS is a way to estimate how much each of these factors contributes to the price, while holding the others constant. So you can say, "a house with one additional bedroom sells for about $30,000 more, all else being equal."

In our study, OLS estimates how much the evaluation method (LPTA vs. best value) contributes to cost growth, after accounting for contract size, type, service category, agency, and all the other factors. The result is a **coefficient** -- a number that tells us how much cost growth changes, on average, when we switch from LPTA to best value, holding everything else constant. If the coefficient is negative, it means best value is associated with lower cost growth. If it is positive, it means best value is associated with higher cost growth. If the coefficient is close to zero and not statistically significant, it means the evaluation method does not appear to have a meaningful effect.

### For Contract Modifications: Negative Binomial Regression

The number of modifications is a count -- it can be 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on, but it cannot be negative or fractional. Counts have special statistical properties (they tend to be right-skewed, with many contracts having few modifications and a few contracts having many), so OLS is not the best tool. Instead, we used a method called **negative binomial regression**, which is specifically designed for count data.

Negative binomial regression works conceptually like OLS but is adapted for the mathematical properties of counts. It estimates how the expected number of modifications changes when we switch from LPTA to best value, accounting for other factors. The result is again a coefficient that tells us the direction and magnitude of the effect.

### For Competition: Logistic Regression

Whether a contract received a single bid is a yes-or-no outcome -- it either did or it did not. For binary outcomes, the appropriate tool is **logistic regression**. Instead of estimating how much an outcome changes (like OLS), logistic regression estimates how the probability of the outcome changes.

The result of a logistic regression is typically expressed as an **odds ratio**. An odds ratio tells you how the odds of something happening change when one factor changes, holding everything else constant. An odds ratio of 1.0 means no change. An odds ratio below 1.0 means lower odds. An odds ratio above 1.0 means higher odds.

For example, if we find that the odds ratio for single-bid outcomes comparing best value to LPTA is 0.75, it means that best-value contracts have about 25 percent lower odds of receiving only a single bid, compared to LPTA contracts that are otherwise similar.

### The Significance Question

For every statistical result, we report a **p-value**, which answers a simple but crucial question: how likely is it that we would see a result this large purely by chance, even if the evaluation method has no real effect?

By convention, researchers consider a result "statistically significant" if the p-value is below 0.05, which means there is less than a 5 percent chance of seeing a result this large by chance alone. This is not a perfect standard (it is somewhat arbitrary, and it does not tell you whether the result is practically important), but it is the most widely used benchmark in social science research.

A p-value of 0.377, for example, means there is a 37.7 percent chance of seeing a result this large by chance. That is far too high to conclude that the result reflects a real effect. A p-value of 0.003, on the other hand, means there is only a 0.3 percent chance of seeing a result this large by chance -- strong evidence that something real is going on.

Throughout Part 4, we report p-values for every major finding and explain what they mean in plain language.

## What the Data Can and Cannot Tell Us

Before we present the results, it is important to be honest about the limitations of this research. Every study has limitations, and being upfront about them is a sign of good science, not weakness.

**We can observe what happened, but we cannot prove what caused it.** Even with propensity score matching and careful statistical controls, this is an observational study, not a randomized experiment. We can say that the evaluation method is or is not associated with different outcomes, but we cannot definitively say it caused those outcomes. There may be factors we could not measure -- the skill of the contracting officer, the quality of the requirements document, the management attention the contract received -- that explain the results.

**We can measure cost growth and modifications, but we cannot measure quality.** USAspending.gov does not include data on how well the contractor performed the work. A contract with zero cost growth and zero modifications might still have delivered mediocre results. A contract with substantial cost growth might have delivered outstanding results that were worth every extra penny. Our measures capture financial and administrative outcomes, not performance quality.

**We can see what happened in fiscal year 2024, but we cannot be sure the results apply to other years or other contexts.** Our dataset provides a snapshot of one year. Procurement practices, market conditions, and policy environments change over time.

**We can analyze contracts above $250,000 in services and IT, but we cannot generalize to all federal contracting.** Our results may not apply to construction contracts, supply contracts, or very small purchases.

**We analyzed a single year of data.** Procurement practices evolve over time. The mix of LPTA and best-value contracts, the characteristics of the contractor base, and the policy environment all change from year to year. Our fiscal year 2024 snapshot may not reflect the dynamics of earlier or later periods.

**We cannot observe the quality of the evaluation process itself.** Two contracts both coded as "best value" in the database may have had very different evaluation processes. One may have involved a rigorous, multi-stage evaluation with expert reviewers, while another may have been a perfunctory process that gave lip service to quality factors. The database does not capture how well the evaluation was conducted, only which method was formally used.

**Survivorship bias may affect our results.** We can only study contracts that were awarded. Solicitations that were cancelled before award -- perhaps because LPTA failed to attract any acceptable bidders, or because the best-value process revealed that no proposal met the government's needs -- are not in our dataset. If the evaluation method affects whether a solicitation succeeds in producing an award, our analysis of awarded contracts may not capture the full picture.

Despite these limitations, this study represents a significant advance over previous work. Our sample is far larger than any prior study. Our methods -- particularly propensity score matching -- address the most important sources of bias. Our results are robust across multiple analytical approaches. And our findings have clear, actionable implications for policy and practice.

These limitations do not invalidate the research. They simply define its boundaries. Within those boundaries, we believe the evidence is strong and the conclusions are well-supported. But intellectual honesty requires acknowledging what we do not know alongside what we do.

With that foundation laid, let us look at what we found.

## Finding 1: Evaluation Method Did Not Drive Cost Overruns

This was our most surprising result and the one that most challenges the conventional wisdom.

After accounting for contract size, contract type, service category, agency, competition, and every other factor in our model, **the choice between LPTA and best value had no statistically significant effect on cost growth**.

Let us unpack the key number: the coefficient for the evaluation method is 0.413, with a p-value of 0.377.

**What the coefficient means:** On average, after controlling for other factors, best-value contracts experienced about 0.4 percentage points more cost growth than LPTA contracts. But this is a tiny difference and could easily be due to chance.

**What the p-value means:** There is a 37.7 percent probability of seeing a difference this large (or larger) purely by chance, even if the evaluation method has absolutely no effect on cost growth. In research, we require at least 95 percent confidence (a p-value below 0.05) before concluding that a relationship is real. At 37.7 percent, we are nowhere close.

> **Key Takeaway:** The data does not support the claim that best-value evaluation produces lower cost overruns compared to the cheapest-bid approach. Across more than 15,000 contracts, after controlling for a wide range of factors, the evaluation method alone did not drive cost growth in either direction. This finding held up across every subgroup and every statistical specification we tested.

**What does this mean practically?** It means that the common belief -- "if you always go with the cheapest bid, you will pay more in the long run" -- is not supported by the large-scale evidence, at least not as a general rule. It also means the opposite claim -- "the cheapest bid saves money" -- is equally unsupported. The evaluation method, by itself, does not appear to be the key driver of cost outcomes.

Notice, however, what did significantly predict cost growth: contract size, contract type, competition, and contract duration all mattered. Larger contracts, longer contracts, and contracts with less competition experienced more cost growth. Fixed-price contracts and small business set-asides experienced less. These factors -- not the evaluation method -- were the main drivers of cost outcomes.

It is worth pausing on this point because it is easy to gloss over. The debate in Congress, in industry trade groups, and in the procurement community has focused intensely on the evaluation method as a driver of cost outcomes. Our data says: look elsewhere. Contract structure (particularly whether the contract is fixed-price or cost-reimbursement) was a far more powerful predictor of cost growth than the evaluation method. Competition mattered more than evaluation method. Even contract duration was a stronger predictor.

This does not mean the evaluation method is irrelevant to costs -- as we will see, it matters for smaller contracts. But the overall evidence suggests that the evaluation method debate has consumed policy attention that would have been better directed at contract structure, competition, and management.

To put the numbers in context: switching from a cost-reimbursement contract to a firm-fixed-price contract was associated with roughly 4.7 percentage points less cost growth. Adding one additional competing bidder was associated with about 0.3 percentage points less cost growth. Switching from LPTA to best value? About 0.4 percentage points -- in the wrong direction -- and not statistically distinguishable from zero. The factors the government can control through contract structure and competition policy have far more leverage than the evaluation method.

Our third analysis produced the most interesting result from a policy standpoint, though it fell just short of the conventional threshold for statistical significance.

The odds ratio for the evaluation method is 0.752. Here is what that means:

Best-value contracts had approximately **25 percent lower odds** of receiving only a single bid compared to otherwise similar LPTA contracts. In other words, when the government said it would evaluate quality alongside price, more companies were willing to compete.

**However**, the p-value was 0.085. This means there is an 8.5 percent chance of seeing a result this large by chance. By the conventional 5 percent cutoff, this falls short of statistical significance. It is what researchers call "marginally significant" or "suggestive."

> **Key Takeaway:** There is suggestive but not conclusive evidence that best-value evaluation attracts more competition. The pattern is consistent with what industry surveys have reported -- that quality-focused companies are more willing to compete when the government evaluates quality, not just price. But we cannot rule out that the difference is due to chance. This finding deserves further study.

**Why this matters for taxpayers:** Competition is the taxpayer's best friend in procurement. When multiple companies compete for a contract, the government gets better prices, better ideas, and more leverage to negotiate. When only one company bids, the government has no alternatives and no negotiating power. If the way the government structures its solicitations affects how many companies bother to compete, that has real implications for the prices taxpayers pay and the quality of services they receive.

Consider the arithmetic of competition. Suppose the government issues a solicitation for a $2 million IT support contract. Under LPTA, three companies bid: $1.8 million, $2.0 million, and $2.1 million. The government takes the $1.8 million bid. Under best value, five companies bid (including two quality-focused firms that sat out the LPTA version), and the price range might be $1.9 million to $2.3 million. Even though the best-value winner might cost $2.0 million instead of $1.8 million, the taxpayer might get substantially better performance -- and the additional competition may itself exert downward pressure on prices because more firms are vying for the work.

This is a simplified example, but it illustrates why competition effects matter. The single-bid problem is particularly concerning. When only one company bids on a competitive solicitation, the "competition" is fiction. The government must either accept the sole offer or cancel the solicitation, restart the process, and hope for better luck next time -- wasting months and significant staff time. Our data showed that 26.3 percent of LPTA contracts and 22.9 percent of best-value contracts received only one bid. That difference -- about 3.4 percentage points -- may not sound large, but across thousands of contracts it translates to hundreds of additional competitive outcomes under best value.

The economic logic is straightforward. Under LPTA, companies know the cheapest acceptable bid wins. Firms that invest in experienced personnel, innovative approaches, and robust quality systems may conclude they cannot compete on price against firms willing to cut costs aggressively. Rather than waste time and money on a proposal they believe they will lose, they sit out the competition. Under best value, these higher-quality firms see a path to winning based on the strength of their overall proposal, not just their price. This potentially brings more firms to the table.

Our data is consistent with this story, but we want to be careful not to overstate the evidence. The result was suggestive, not definitive. We consider it a pattern worth watching and studying further.

## Finding 4: Contract Size Changes Everything

This is the strongest and most important finding in the entire study. It is also the most nuanced, so let us walk through it carefully.

Up to this point, we have been looking at the overall effect of evaluation method across all contracts in our sample. But what if the effect is not the same for all contracts? What if the evaluation method matters more for some contracts than others?

To test this, we added what statisticians call an **interaction term** to our cost growth model. An interaction term tests whether the effect of one factor (evaluation method) depends on the level of another factor (contract size). Think of it this way: aspirin might be effective for mild headaches but not for migraines. The effect of aspirin *depends on* the severity of the headache. Similarly, the effect of evaluation method might depend on the size of the contract.

### Table 6: The Size Interaction -- Where Best Value Helps Most

| Award Size | Estimated Effect of Best Value on Cost Growth | Statistical Significance |
|:---|:---|:---|
| $250K - $500K | Approximately 2-3 percentage points lower cost growth | Significant |
| $500K - $1M | Approximately 1.5-2 percentage points lower cost growth | Significant |
| $1M - $5M | Approximately 0.5-1.5 percentage points lower cost growth | Marginally significant |
| $5M - $25M | Near zero; no meaningful difference | Not significant |
| $25M - $100M | Approximately 0.5-1 percentage points higher cost growth | Not significant |
| Over $100M | Approximately 1-2 percentage points higher cost growth | Not significant |

**The interaction effect was statistically significant at p = 0.003**, making it the strongest finding in the study.

Here is what this means, step by step:

**For smaller contracts ($250,000 to about $5 million), best-value evaluation was associated with meaningfully lower cost growth.** On contracts in this size range, using best value instead of LPTA was associated with roughly 1 to 3 percentage points less cost growth, controlling for other factors. Given the number of contracts in this range, the cumulative savings would be substantial.

**For mid-sized contracts ($5 million to $25 million), the difference shrank to essentially zero.** The evaluation method did not appear to matter much for contracts in this range.

**For larger contracts (above $25 million), the advantage of best value disappeared and may have reversed.** There were hints that very large best-value contracts experienced somewhat more cost growth than similar LPTA contracts, though this reversal was not statistically significant.

> **Key Takeaway:** The effect of evaluation method on cost overruns depends on contract size, and this is the study's most robust finding. Best value helps most on smaller contracts and helps least (or not at all) on larger contracts. The interaction was statistically significant at p = 0.003, meaning there is only a 0.3% chance of seeing this pattern by chance.

**Why might this be?** We can offer several plausible explanations:

**On smaller contracts, the evaluation method is the primary quality gate.** For a $400,000 help desk support contract, the source selection decision may be the most consequential quality decision the government makes. If the government picks the cheapest acceptable bidder and that bidder turns out to be mediocre, there may be limited resources and management attention available to course-correct. Best-value evaluation helps the government avoid mediocre contractors on these smaller contracts where the government's oversight capacity is thinnest.

**On larger contracts, other factors dominate.** A $50 million systems integration contract involves extensive oversight, dedicated program management staff, regular performance reviews, and detailed quality assurance processes. The government's ability to manage and monitor the contractor's performance may matter more than how the contractor was initially selected. Whether the contractor was chosen through LPTA or best value becomes less important when there is a team of government employees monitoring every deliverable.

**Larger contracts also attract more capable contractors regardless of method.** Very large contracts are prestigious and profitable enough to attract top-tier firms regardless of the evaluation method. The competition dynamics are different: even under LPTA, the firms competing for a $75 million contract are likely to be large, established companies with significant experience. The risk of getting a fly-by-night low-bidder is much lower on large contracts than on small ones.

**Requirements for larger contracts tend to be more thoroughly developed.** Agencies invest more time and effort in writing requirements for major contracts. When the requirements are clear and comprehensive, the evaluation method matters less because both LPTA and best value will identify capable contractors. When requirements are thin or ambiguous -- as they often are for smaller contracts -- best-value evaluation compensates by allowing the government to assess the contractor's understanding and approach.

**The possible reversal on very large contracts is intriguing but not conclusive.** Our data hinted that best value might actually be associated with slightly higher cost growth on contracts above $25 million, but this effect was not statistically significant. If it is real, several explanations are possible. Very large best-value evaluations take longer, and the market may shift between the time the solicitation is issued and the time the contract is awarded, leading to pricing that is already outdated. The evaluation process for very large contracts also tends to be more complex and politically sensitive, which can lead to suboptimal selection decisions driven by factors other than technical merit. And the larger the contract, the more modifications are likely to be legitimately needed, which can obscure any initial advantage from better contractor selection.

We want to be cautious about overinterpreting the reversal on large contracts because the sample of very large contracts in our dataset was small (only 323 contracts above $100 million) and the result was not statistically significant. But it is a pattern worth investigating in future research.

**The practical significance of the size interaction is substantial.** Contracts in the $250,000 to $5 million range represent roughly 74 percent of all competitive service and IT contracts -- the vast majority of the government's contracting workload. If best-value evaluation reduces cost growth by even 1 to 2 percentage points in this range, the aggregate savings across tens of thousands of contracts could be measured in hundreds of millions of dollars annually. This is not a minor finding; it suggests a specific, evidence-based policy change that could have real financial impact.

This finding has significant policy implications, which we will explore in Part 5.

## Propensity Score Matching: The Apples-to-Apples Test

As described in Part 3, we also used propensity score matching to create the most direct comparison possible between LPTA and best-value contracts. Out of the full sample of 15,477 contracts, the matching process identified **1,093 matched pairs** -- 2,186 contracts total that were similar in their observable characteristics but differed in the evaluation method used.

The matching substantially improved the comparability of the two groups:

**Before matching**, best-value contracts were on average more than twice as large as LPTA contracts ($8.2 million vs. $3.8 million), much more likely to be cost-reimbursement contracts, and concentrated in different service categories.

**After matching**, the differences shrank dramatically. The average award amount was within 10 percent between the two groups. The distribution of contract types, service categories, and agency sizes was closely balanced. The standardized bias -- a measure of how different the groups are -- decreased by **57 to 80 percent** on most key characteristics. This means the matching successfully created a much more apples-to-apples comparison.

**What the matched analysis found:**

The results from the matched sample were consistent with the full-sample results. In the matched pairs, there was no significant difference in cost growth between LPTA and best-value contracts (confirming our main finding). There was no significant difference in modification frequency (confirming our second finding). And the pattern of best value helping more on smaller contracts was preserved, though the smaller sample size made precise estimation more difficult.

The matched analysis is important because it provides the most direct response to the most common criticism of observational procurement research: "you are comparing apples to oranges." By matching LPTA and best-value contracts on their observable characteristics, we created the closest possible approximation to an apples-to-apples comparison. The fact that the matched results confirmed the full-sample results strengthens our confidence that the findings reflect genuine patterns rather than artifacts of comparing different types of contracts.

It is worth noting what the matching process revealed about the differences between LPTA and best-value contracts. Before matching, the two groups were strikingly different -- different sizes, different contract types, different service categories, different agencies. This confirms what procurement professionals have long observed: the choice of evaluation method is not random. It is systematically related to the characteristics of the procurement. Any study that ignores these systematic differences -- as most prior studies did -- risks drawing misleading conclusions.

After matching, many of the unmatched contracts were "discarded" because they had no suitable match in the other group. This is a natural consequence of the matching process and reflects the fact that some types of contracts almost exclusively use one evaluation method or the other. The matched sample represents the subset of the contracting universe where both evaluation methods are plausible choices -- which is exactly the subset where the policy debate is most relevant.

> **Key Takeaway:** The propensity score matching analysis, which creates the most direct possible comparison between similar LPTA and best-value contracts, confirmed all of the main findings from the full-sample analysis. The null results on cost growth and modifications are not an artifact of comparing different types of contracts. They hold up even when we restrict the comparison to contracts that are similar in every observable way except the evaluation method.

## Robustness Checks: We Tested This Every Way We Could

In empirical research, a finding is only as good as its robustness -- its ability to hold up under different analytical approaches. If a result changes when you tweak the method, it might be an artifact of the particular approach you used rather than a genuine feature of the data. We subjected our findings to an extensive battery of robustness checks.

**We tried different model specifications.** We added and removed control variables, used different functional forms (linear vs. log-transformed outcomes), and estimated the models with and without certain variable categories. The null result on the main effect of evaluation method held across all specifications.

**We tested different subsamples.** We analyzed the results separately for IT contracts, professional services contracts, Department of Defense contracts, civilian agency contracts, firm-fixed-price contracts, and cost-reimbursement contracts. In no subgroup did we find a significant main effect of evaluation method on cost growth.

**We used different matching techniques.** In addition to nearest-neighbor propensity score matching, we tried caliper matching (requiring matches to be within a certain distance), kernel matching (weighting all potential matches rather than picking the single closest), and Mahalanobis distance matching. The results were consistent across methods.

**We tested for nonlinear effects.** We allowed the relationship between evaluation method and cost growth to be nonlinear -- perhaps best value helps, but only up to a point. We found no evidence of threshold effects or nonlinear patterns beyond the size interaction we already identified.

**We tested for sensitivity to outliers.** We removed the top and bottom 1 percent of cost growth values (the most extreme cases) to check whether our results were driven by a handful of unusual contracts. They were not. The null result on the main effect and the significant size interaction both survived.

**We conducted a Rosenbaum bounds test** on the propensity score matching results. This is a technical test that asks: how large would a hidden bias (an unmeasured factor that differs between the groups) have to be to overturn our null result? The answer: even moderate hidden bias would not change our conclusions about the main effect. The null result is not fragile.

### Table 7: Summary of All Hypotheses Tested

| Hypothesis | What We Predicted | What We Found | Statistical Significance | Verdict |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| H1: Best value reduces cost growth | Best value contracts will have lower cost overruns | No significant difference (coef = 0.413) | p = 0.377 | **Not supported** |
| H2: Best value reduces modifications | Best value contracts will have fewer contract changes | No significant difference (coef = -0.034) | p = 0.449 | **Not supported** |

> **Key Takeaway:** The overall pattern is clear. The evaluation method -- LPTA versus best value -- does not appear to be a significant driver of contract cost outcomes when considered in isolation across all contracts. However, contract size moderates the relationship: best value does appear to help on smaller contracts. And there are suggestive signs that best value may attract more competition. These findings are robust across multiple analytical approaches, subsamples, and model specifications.

# Part 5: What It All Means

## Why "No Difference" Is Actually an Important Finding

Let us start with what may seem counterintuitive: our most important finding is that we found no significant difference.

In the world of research, null results -- findings of "no effect" -- are often seen as disappointing. They are harder to publish, harder to publicize, and harder to build careers on. But in the world of policy, null results can be among the most valuable findings of all.

Consider the context. For years, Congress has debated whether to restrict the government's use of LPTA, based largely on the assumption that LPTA leads to worse outcomes. Industry groups have lobbied against LPTA, arguing that it produces cost overruns, quality problems, and reduced competition. Government reports have questioned its use. The assumption underlying all of this activity is that the evaluation method is a primary driver of contract outcomes -- that switching from LPTA to best value would meaningfully improve results.

Our data does not support this assumption, at least not as a general rule.

This does not mean the debate has been pointless. It does not mean that individual contracts have never gone wrong because of a poorly chosen evaluation method. And it does not mean that the evaluation method never matters (as we showed, it does matter for smaller contracts). But it does mean that the evaluation method is not the silver bullet that some advocates have presented it as.

> **Key Takeaway:** Mandating best value across the board would not, by itself, significantly reduce cost overruns or contract modifications in the federal government. The data suggests that other factors -- contract management, workforce capability, requirements quality, and competition -- are more important drivers of outcomes than the method used to select the contractor.

This has profound implications for where the government invests its reform energy. If the evaluation method is not the primary driver of outcomes, then spending political capital on mandating one method over the other is a poor investment. That energy would be better spent on the factors that do drive outcomes -- which is where we turn next.

## Why the Source Selection Method Alone Does Not Drive Outcomes

To understand why the evaluation method had less effect than expected, consider what happens over the life of a federal contract. The evaluation and selection process -- the part where the government decides which contractor to hire -- is typically completed within a few months. But the contract itself may last three, five, or even ten years.

During those years, hundreds of decisions are made by government program managers, contracting officers, and technical monitors. Each of those decisions can affect cost, schedule, and quality. Did the government clearly define what it wanted before writing the contract? Did it provide timely decisions when the contractor had questions? Did it monitor the contractor's work and catch problems early? Did it manage scope changes effectively? Did it hold the contractor accountable for performance?

The evaluation method determines who shows up at the starting line. Everything else determines who crosses the finish line.

Imagine two scenarios:

**Scenario A:** A government agency uses best value to select an outstanding contractor for a five-year IT support contract. The contractor starts strong. But over the next five years, the government's program manager changes three times, requirements are poorly documented, change requests pile up without proper analysis, and nobody monitors the contractor's performance metrics. By year five, the contract is over budget and the work quality has declined.

**Scenario B:** A different agency uses LPTA to select a merely adequate contractor for a similar contract. But this agency has experienced program managers, clear requirements, a rigorous modification review process, and monthly performance meetings. The program managers catch small problems before they become big ones. By year five, the contract is on budget and the work is satisfactory.

Which contract produced a better outcome? Scenario B, despite using LPTA and selecting a less impressive contractor. The quality of contract management overwhelmed the quality of contractor selection.

This is not a hypothetical. Researchers in public administration and contract management have documented repeatedly that post-award management is the dominant factor in contract outcomes. The Defense Acquisition University, which trains military acquisition professionals, emphasizes that "you cannot evaluate your way to good performance" -- meaning that even the best evaluation process cannot substitute for good management after the award.

Our data is consistent with this view. The evaluation method had little effect on outcomes, but factors related to contract structure and management (contract type, period of performance, competition level) had significant effects. This suggests that the action is in the management, not the selection.

## The Contract Management Argument

If the evaluation method is not the key driver of outcomes, what is? The evidence points strongly toward **contract management** -- the day-to-day work of overseeing the contractor after the award.

Contract management includes:

**Requirements development.** Before a contract can succeed, the government must clearly define what it wants. Ambiguous, incomplete, or unrealistic requirements create problems regardless of which evaluation method is used and regardless of which contractor wins. A brilliant contractor cannot deliver what the government cannot articulate, and a mediocre contractor will struggle even more with unclear direction.

**Post-award oversight.** Once the contract starts, government program managers must monitor the contractor's work, review deliverables, conduct quality assurance, and ensure the contractor is meeting its commitments. When oversight is thin -- because the program manager is responsible for too many contracts, lacks expertise, or has other priorities -- problems go undetected until they become expensive to fix.

**Modification management.** When a contract needs to be changed -- and almost all contracts need some changes over time -- the government must analyze the proposed change, negotiate a fair price, and ensure the modification aligns with the contract's objectives. Poorly managed modifications are a major source of cost growth, regardless of how the contractor was selected.

**Performance accountability.** The government must hold contractors accountable for performance. This means setting clear metrics, evaluating performance regularly, documenting problems, and imposing consequences (or providing incentives) based on results. When the government fails to hold contractors accountable, there is no mechanism to ensure quality regardless of how the contractor was chosen.

The common thread is that all of these functions depend on the government having enough skilled people to do the work. Which brings us to the next argument.

## The Workforce Argument

The federal acquisition workforce -- the government employees who write contracts, evaluate proposals, manage programs, and oversee contractor performance -- is arguably the most important factor in determining whether federal contracts succeed or fail. And this workforce has been under stress for decades.

Consider the numbers: the volume and complexity of federal contracting have grown enormously over the past thirty years, driven by the outsourcing of functions once performed by government employees, the proliferation of IT contracts, and the increasing complexity of the services the government buys. But the size of the acquisition workforce has not kept pace. The result is that individual contracting officers and program managers are responsible for more contracts, more dollars, and more complexity than ever before.

In this environment, the choice between LPTA and best value may be less consequential than the skill, experience, and workload of the person making the choice and managing the resulting contract. A skilled contracting officer using LPTA on an appropriate contract will likely produce better outcomes than an overwhelmed, inexperienced contracting officer using best value on a complex contract.

There is a deeper point here as well. Best-value evaluation is more complex and time-consuming than LPTA. It requires the government to assemble evaluation teams, develop detailed evaluation criteria, review and score technical proposals, conduct comparative analyses, and document the rationale for the selection decision. All of this takes time, expertise, and management attention.

When the acquisition workforce is stretched thin, agencies may default to LPTA not because it is the right method but because it is the faster, simpler one. Critics point to this as a problem -- agencies are choosing LPTA when they should be using best value, leading to worse outcomes. But our data suggests a different interpretation: even when agencies use best value, the outcomes are not significantly different, because the underlying challenge is not the evaluation method but the capacity of the workforce to manage the contract effectively after the award.

This does not mean best value is pointless. It means that mandating best value without investing in the workforce's ability to execute it is like buying a high-performance car and then never changing the oil. The evaluation method is only as good as the people implementing it.

The workforce issue is not abstract. Consider what it takes to execute a best-value evaluation properly. The contracting officer must develop a detailed source selection plan, defining evaluation criteria, rating scales, and the relative importance of each factor. The agency must assemble an evaluation team -- typically three to seven people with relevant technical expertise -- who will review proposals, score them, and write evaluation narratives. The source selection authority must then review the evaluation results, conduct a comparative analysis, and make a selection decision that can withstand scrutiny from protesters, auditors, and courts.

All of this requires training, experience, and time. When the workforce is stretched thin, some of these steps are compressed, abbreviated, or done poorly. The evaluation team may lack expertise. The rating criteria may be generic rather than tailored. The comparative analysis may be superficial. In these cases, best-value evaluation delivers less value not because the method is flawed but because the execution is compromised.

The government accountability community has flagged this concern repeatedly. The GAO has found that contracting officers often receive insufficient training in source selection methods and that evaluation teams are sometimes assembled hastily with personnel who have competing responsibilities. The Defense Department's own reviews have identified workforce shortfalls as a contributing factor in acquisition failures.

If Congress mandates best value without investing in the workforce capacity to execute it, the likely result is not better outcomes but more paperwork, longer procurement timelines, and evaluation processes that look rigorous on paper but are not rigorous in practice. The form of best value without the substance of best value.

## The Requirements Quality Argument

Before a government agency can select a contractor, it must define what it wants. This process -- called "requirements development" -- is the foundation on which everything else rests. If the requirements are clear, complete, and realistic, both LPTA and best value will tend to produce reasonable outcomes because both methods will attract capable contractors who understand the work. If the requirements are vague, incomplete, or unrealistic, both methods will struggle because neither can compensate for the government's failure to articulate its needs.

Requirements quality is widely acknowledged within the acquisition community as one of the most critical factors in contract success. The GAO has identified poor requirements as a contributing factor in numerous troubled contracts. The Defense Science Board has called requirements instability -- the tendency for the government to change what it wants after the contract starts -- one of the most significant drivers of cost growth in defense programs.

Think about it in everyday terms: if you hire a contractor to renovate your kitchen but you have not decided on the layout, the appliances, or the finishes, no amount of careful contractor selection will prevent problems. You will end up with change orders, delays, and cost overruns -- not because you picked the wrong contractor, but because you did not know what you wanted.

The same logic applies to federal contracts. If an agency issues a solicitation with vague requirements, the evaluation method may be irrelevant because the root problem is upstream. The contractor -- whether selected by LPTA or best value -- will face ambiguity, leading to different interpretations, disputes, and modifications.

Our finding that the evaluation method has little overall effect on outcomes is consistent with this argument. If requirements quality is the dominant factor, then improving requirements development would do more to reduce cost overruns and modifications than changing the evaluation method.

Consider the cascade effect of poor requirements. When requirements are unclear:

- **Contractors interpret ambiguities differently**, leading to proposals that are not truly comparable. Under LPTA, the lowest bidder may have interpreted the requirements in the narrowest possible way (costing less but doing less). Under best value, different contractors may propose very different solutions, making comparison difficult.

- **After award, the work itself becomes contentious.** The government and the contractor may disagree about what was included in the original scope, leading to disputes, modifications, and cost growth. These problems occur regardless of how the contractor was selected.

- **The evaluation process itself is compromised.** If the government cannot clearly define what it wants, it cannot clearly evaluate proposals against its needs. The evaluation becomes an exercise in comparing apples to oranges, regardless of the method used.

The acquisition community has a saying: "garbage in, garbage out." This applies with particular force to requirements. The most sophisticated evaluation method in the world cannot compensate for a government that does not know what it wants. Conversely, even a simple LPTA evaluation can produce good outcomes when the requirements are clear and comprehensive, because every contractor is working toward the same well-defined objective.

The data from our study cannot directly test the requirements quality hypothesis because USAspending.gov does not include information about requirements quality. But the null effect of evaluation method on cost growth and modifications is consistent with a world in which requirements quality -- not evaluation method -- is the more important variable.

## When Best Value Does Help: The Small Contract Story

While the overall null result is important, we should not lose sight of the one place where best value did appear to make a meaningful difference: smaller contracts.

For contracts between $250,000 and approximately $5 million, best-value evaluation was associated with lower cost growth. This is an important finding because smaller contracts represent the majority of federal contracting actions.

Why might best value be particularly beneficial for smaller contracts? We believe several factors are at work:

**Smaller contracts receive less management attention.** A $400,000 contract for IT help desk support is unlikely to have a dedicated program manager, regular performance reviews, or detailed quality assurance processes. The contracting officer who awarded it probably has dozens or hundreds of other contracts to manage. In this environment, the initial selection decision is the primary quality gate. If the government picks the wrong contractor, there may be nobody watching closely enough to catch problems early.

**The contractor pool for smaller contracts is more variable.** Large federal contracts attract large, established companies with track records, management systems, and reputations to protect. Small contracts attract a wider range of firms, including newer companies with limited experience and firms that compete primarily on price. The risk of getting a low-quality contractor is higher in the small contract space, and best-value evaluation helps screen out the weakest firms.

**Requirements for smaller contracts tend to be less thoroughly developed.** Agencies invest less time and effort in requirements for smaller purchases. When requirements are less precise, the evaluation method compensates: under best value, the government can assess whether a contractor truly understands the work, even if the requirements document is not perfect. Under LPTA, the government can only assess whether the contractor meets the minimum threshold, regardless of how well the contractor understands the nuances.

**The stakes of a bad choice are proportionally larger for smaller contracts.** A 10 percent cost overrun on a $500,000 contract is $50,000 -- not catastrophic, but real. More importantly, a poorly performing contractor on a smaller contract may cause operational disruptions that affect government employees' ability to do their jobs. Unlike a large contract where problems can sometimes be contained or managed through additional oversight, problems on smaller contracts often fester.

> **Key Takeaway:** If the government were to mandate or strongly encourage best-value evaluation for one category of contracts, the evidence suggests that smaller service and IT contracts (roughly $250,000 to $5 million) would benefit the most. For larger contracts, other factors -- management quality, requirements clarity, and workforce capability -- appear to dominate.

## Policy Recommendations

Based on the full body of evidence from this study, we offer the following recommendations for policymakers, agency leaders, and the acquisition workforce:

### Recommendation 1: Do Not Mandate a Single Evaluation Method for All Contracts

The evidence does not support a one-size-fits-all approach. Mandating best value for all contracts would impose additional costs and complexity without improving outcomes for larger contracts. Mandating LPTA for all contracts would eliminate a tool that appears to genuinely help on smaller contracts. The government should preserve flexibility for contracting officers to choose the method that best fits the specific procurement, while providing clear guidance on when each method is most appropriate.

### Recommendation 2: Strongly Encourage Best Value for Smaller Service and IT Contracts

The data shows that best value is associated with lower cost growth for contracts in the $250,000 to $5 million range. Agencies should default to best-value evaluation for service and IT contracts in this range unless there is a documented justification for using LPTA. This could be implemented through policy guidance, training, or regulatory changes to the Federal Acquisition Regulation.

### Recommendation 3: Invest in the Acquisition Workforce

The skill and capacity of the people managing federal contracts may be more important than the rules they follow. Congress should increase funding for acquisition workforce training, hiring, and retention. Agencies should reduce the ratio of contracts per contracting officer, invest in professional development, and create career paths that attract and retain talented acquisition professionals.

### Recommendation 4: Improve Post-Award Contract Management

What happens after the contract is signed matters at least as much as how the contractor was selected. Agencies should invest in contract administration -- dedicated program managers, performance monitoring systems, regular contractor reviews, and robust modification management processes. The government should track and report on contract management quality metrics, not just procurement process metrics.

### Recommendation 5: Strengthen Requirements Development

The government should invest in helping program offices develop clearer, more complete, and more realistic requirements before contracts are solicited. This includes training for program officials (who often write requirements without procurement expertise), structured requirements review processes, and lessons-learned databases that capture common requirements pitfalls.

### Recommendation 6: Make Contractor Performance Data Available for Research

One of the most significant limitations of this study -- and of the procurement evidence base generally -- is the lack of publicly available data on contractor performance quality. The government collects performance data through the Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS), but this data is not publicly accessible and is difficult to use even for government researchers. Making de-identified CPARS data available for academic and policy research would enable studies that measure what really matters: not just cost and schedule outcomes, but the quality of the work.

### Recommendation 7: Address the Single-Bid Problem

Nearly one in four contracts in our sample received only a single bid. This is a crisis of competition that receives far too little attention. The government should investigate why so many solicitations attract only one bidder and take steps to increase competition, including simplifying the proposal process, providing better market research, engaging with industry earlier in the procurement process, and ensuring that solicitation requirements do not unnecessarily exclude capable firms. Our data suggests that using best-value evaluation may help attract more bidders, and agencies should consider this when deciding how to structure their solicitations.

### Recommendation 8: Fund Rigorous Procurement Research

The fact that a question this important -- affecting hundreds of billions of dollars in annual spending -- has been debated for decades largely on the basis of anecdotes and opinions is itself a policy failure. The government should fund rigorous empirical research on procurement outcomes, including randomized controlled trials where feasible, large-scale observational studies, and program evaluations. The cost of this research would be trivial compared to the potential savings from evidence-based procurement policy.

To put this in perspective: the federal government spends more than $700 billion per year on contracts. If better evidence led to even a one percent improvement in outcomes, that would be $7 billion in value -- enough to fund thousands of research studies. Yet the total amount spent on rigorous procurement research is a rounding error in the federal budget. The National Science Foundation funds research on topics far less consequential to the public interest. Congress should establish a dedicated research program on procurement effectiveness, housed at an independent research institution, with access to government data and a mandate to produce actionable findings.

### Recommendation 9: Use the Data the Government Already Collects

The federal government collects an enormous amount of data about its contracts -- through USAspending.gov, FPDS, CPARS, the Electronic Subcontracting Reporting System, and other databases. But these data systems are fragmented, difficult to integrate, and underused for analytical purposes. The government should invest in making its procurement data more accessible, more complete, and more useful for evidence-based decision-making.

Specifically, the government should link contract outcome data (cost growth, modifications, performance ratings) with procurement process data (evaluation method, competition level, workforce characteristics) to enable the kind of comprehensive analysis that this study attempted. Currently, answering even basic questions about procurement effectiveness requires researchers to manually download, clean, and merge data from multiple systems -- a process that takes months and that few researchers have the resources to undertake.

## What We Still Need to Learn

Every good study raises more questions than it answers. Here are the most important questions that our research could not resolve and that future studies should address:

**Does the evaluation method affect the quality of contractor performance?** Our study measured cost growth, modifications, and competition -- all important outcomes -- but could not measure whether the work itself was any good. A contract that comes in on budget but delivers mediocre results is not a success. Future research using performance data (such as CPARS ratings) is essential to answering the quality question.

**Does the evaluation method affect long-term outcomes?** Our study used fiscal year 2024 data. Many of these contracts were still in their early stages. Cost growth and performance problems may emerge later in the contract's life. Longitudinal studies that track contracts from award through completion would provide a more complete picture.

**Does the evaluation method affect innovation and knowledge transfer?** Best-value evaluation allows the government to consider innovative approaches and value-added features that LPTA ignores. Even if cost outcomes are similar, best value might produce more innovative solutions or better knowledge transfer to government employees. These outcomes are important but difficult to measure.

**Why do so many contracts receive only one bid?** Our finding that nearly one in four competitive contracts had only one bidder deserves its own research program. Is it because of the evaluation method? The complexity of the proposal process? The requirements themselves? Market concentration? Understanding the drivers of single-bid outcomes is critical to improving competition.

**Does the evaluation method affect bid protest rates and outcomes?** Some procurement professionals argue that LPTA reduces protests because decisions are more objective. Others argue that best value reduces protests because the government can make more thoughtful, defensible decisions. The data on this question is sparse and inconclusive.

**Do these findings apply to construction, goods, and other contract types?** Our study focused on services and IT. The dynamics may be very different for construction (where lowest-price awards have a long history and established practices), manufactured goods (where technical specifications can be very precise), or research and development (where innovation is the primary objective).

**Would randomized experiments confirm our findings?** The gold standard of causal evidence is the randomized controlled trial. In theory, agencies could randomly assign evaluation methods to similar contracts and compare the outcomes. This would eliminate the selection bias concerns that plague observational studies like ours. While there are practical and legal challenges to implementing such experiments, pilot programs within willing agencies could provide invaluable evidence.

**How does the acquisition workforce's experience level affect outcomes?** Our data did not include information about the experience, training, or workload of the contracting officers and program managers responsible for each contract. If workforce quality is indeed more important than evaluation method, then studies that can measure workforce characteristics would provide crucial insights. Some agencies track workforce certifications and experience levels, which could be linked to contract outcomes.

**Does the evaluation method affect contractor innovation and long-term market health?** The race-to-the-bottom concern -- that LPTA drives quality-focused firms out of the market over time -- is one of the most important unanswered questions. Answering it requires tracking the composition of the contractor base over many years, measuring firm entry and exit, and assessing whether the evaluation method mix in a market affects the quality of firms competing. This is a difficult but critically important research question.

**How do multiple evaluation reforms interact?** The evaluation method does not operate in isolation. It interacts with contract type (fixed-price vs. cost-reimbursement), small business policies, organizational conflict of interest rules, past performance evaluation systems, and dozens of other procurement policies. Future research should examine these interactions rather than studying individual policies in isolation.

## The Broader Context: A Government That Learns

Our findings fit into a larger story about government performance and reform. For decades, procurement reform has followed a predictable cycle that is worth describing because it explains why the evaluation method debate has been so persistent and so unproductive.

**The cycle works like this.** A high-profile contract failure makes the news -- a software system that costs three times its original estimate, a defense program that is years behind schedule, a consulting engagement that delivers nothing useful. Congress holds hearings. Witnesses testify that the problem was caused by some specific feature of the procurement process -- in recent years, the overuse of LPTA has been a frequent target. Congress mandates new rules. Agencies implement the rules, which create additional paperwork, longer procurement timelines, and new costs. But the underlying problems -- workforce shortages, poor requirements, weak contract management -- remain unaddressed because they are harder and more expensive to fix than changing a procurement rule.

Eventually, a new failure occurs, and the cycle begins again. Each iteration adds a new layer of rules and procedures without addressing the root causes of contract failure. The procurement process becomes more complex, the workforce becomes more burdened, and the outcomes do not improve. This pattern has been documented by scholars of government reform going back to the 1990s, including the landmark National Performance Review led by Vice President Al Gore, which identified excessive regulation and insufficient workforce capacity as the central challenges of government procurement.

Our research suggests that the current iteration of this cycle -- the push to mandate best value and restrict LPTA -- may be another case of treating a symptom rather than the disease. The evaluation method is a visible, controllable policy lever, and it is natural to reach for it when something goes wrong. But the data suggests that pulling this lever has less effect than expected because the more important factors are less visible and harder to change.

This does not mean procurement reform is hopeless. It means reform needs to be based on evidence rather than anecdote, and it needs to target the factors that actually drive outcomes. Our study provides some of that evidence and points toward the factors that matter most.

This study represents a different approach: using large-scale data and rigorous methods to test commonly held beliefs before turning them into mandates. The findings are humbling -- the conventional wisdom was not entirely wrong, but it was incomplete in important ways. Best value is not a cure-all. LPTA is not a catastrophe. The factors that most affect contract outcomes are the ones that receive the least policy attention: workforce capacity, contract management quality, and requirements development.

This does not mean the evaluation method is irrelevant. It means it is one tool among many, and its effectiveness depends on the context in which it is used. For smaller contracts with limited oversight, best value appears to provide genuine value by helping the government select more capable contractors. For larger contracts with robust management structures, the initial selection method matters less because other quality gates and management processes compensate.

The path to better government contracting runs not through mandating one evaluation method over another, but through building a government that has the capacity to manage its contracts effectively, define its requirements clearly, attract sufficient competition, and hold its contractors accountable for results. These are harder problems than changing an evaluation rule, but they are the problems that actually need solving.

## The Bottom Line for Taxpayers

If you have read this far, you deserve a clear, simple answer to the question posed in the title: does it matter how the government picks its contractors?

The answer is: **it matters, but not in the way most people think, and not as much as other things matter**.

Choosing between the cheapest bid and the best overall value is a real decision with real consequences. For smaller contracts, the evidence suggests that weighing quality alongside price produces better results. For this category of contracts -- which represents the majority of federal contracting actions -- the investment in best-value evaluation appears to pay off in lower cost overruns.

But for the federal contracting system as a whole, the evaluation method is not the primary driver of outcomes. Whether the government gets good value for taxpayer money depends much more on:

- **Whether the government clearly defines what it wants** before it writes the contract.
- **Whether the government has enough skilled people** to manage the contract after it is awarded.
- **Whether multiple companies compete** for the work, giving the government choices and leverage.
- **Whether the government holds contractors accountable** for performance throughout the life of the contract.
- **Whether the contract is structured** in a way that aligns the contractor's incentives with the government's objectives.

These factors operate regardless of the evaluation method. A poorly managed best-value contract will fail just as surely as a poorly managed LPTA contract. A well-managed LPTA contract can succeed just as reliably as a well-managed best-value contract.

The implication for taxpayers is this: if you want the government to spend your money more wisely, do not focus solely on the rules for picking contractors. Focus on the capacity of the government to manage the work after the contractor is hired. Invest in the people who oversee the contracts. Demand clear definitions of what the government is buying. Insist on meaningful competition. And hold the government accountable for results, not just compliance with procedural rules.

The evaluation method debate has consumed enormous amounts of political energy, regulatory attention, and industry advocacy for decades. Our data suggests that much of this energy would have been better spent on the less glamorous but more consequential work of building a government that can manage its contracts effectively.

The evaluation method matters for smaller contracts, and the government should use that insight. But for the overall system, the most productive reforms would address the fundamentals: better people, better requirements, better management, and better data. These are not glamorous reforms. They do not make for good sound bites or compelling campaign slogans. They require sustained investment rather than one-time rule changes. But they are what the evidence says will actually make a difference.

There is one more thing the evidence says, and it is perhaps the most important of all: we need to keep studying this. The fact that a question affecting $700 billion in annual spending has gone largely untested by rigorous research is a failure of policy analysis that should not continue. The government should invest in understanding what works and what does not, not just in procurement but across all of its operations. Evidence-based government is not a luxury; it is a responsibility to the taxpayers who fund it.

That is what 15,000 federal contracts tell us about saving taxpayer money: the way you pick your contractors matters, but what you do after you pick them matters more.

# Part 6: References and Resources

## Key Sources

The following are the most important sources that informed this research, organized by topic. Full academic citations are available in the original dissertation.

### Government Reports and Data

**Government Accountability Office (GAO).** The GAO has published numerous reports on federal procurement practices, including several that directly address the use of LPTA versus best value. Their 2014 report "Federal Contracting: Observations on the Use of LPTA Criteria" was particularly influential in documenting the extent of LPTA use and the concerns surrounding it. The GAO also publishes annual reports on bid protest statistics and contract management challenges.

**Office of Federal Procurement Policy (OFPP).** OFPP, part of the Office of Management and Budget, issues government-wide procurement policy guidance. Their memoranda on source selection methods and acquisition workforce development informed our understanding of the policy context.

**Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR).** The FAR is the primary set of rules governing federal procurement. Part 15 of the FAR, which addresses "Contracting by Negotiation," contains the specific rules for LPTA and best-value evaluation. The FAR is available online through the Acquisition.gov website.

**USAspending.gov.** The source of all contract data used in this study. USAspending.gov is maintained by the Department of the Treasury and provides detailed, downloadable data on all federal spending, including contracts, grants, and direct payments. The data is free, publicly accessible, and updated regularly.

**Federal Procurement Data System (FPDS).** FPDS is the government's contract database that feeds into USAspending.gov. Researchers who wish to replicate or extend our analyses can access contract-level data through both USAspending.gov and FPDS.

### Academic Research on Transaction Cost Economics

**Oliver E. Williamson** developed the foundational theory of transaction cost economics that underpins much of the theoretical argument for best value. His 1985 book "The Economic Institutions of Capitalism" and his 1996 book "The Mechanisms of Governance" are the essential references. Williamson won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2009 for this work.

**Ronald Coase** laid the groundwork for transaction cost economics with his 1937 article "The Nature of the Firm," which asked why firms exist when markets could theoretically handle all economic transactions. His insight -- that transactions have costs beyond the price of goods -- is the foundation of the entire field.

### Public Value Theory

**Mark H. Moore** developed the concept of public value in his 1995 book "Creating Public Value: Strategic Management in Government." Moore's framework provides the theoretical basis for evaluating government procurement in terms of public outcomes rather than just cost efficiency.

**Barry Bozeman** extended public value theory to the context of government contracting and public management in his work on public values and public failure. His framework helps explain why cost is not the only relevant measure of procurement success.

### Procurement Research

**Steven Kelman** of Harvard's Kennedy School has written extensively about procurement reform, including the tension between cost efficiency and innovation in government contracting. His book "Procurement and Public Management: The Fear of Discretion and the Quality of Government Performance" (1990) remains influential.

**W. Noel Keyes** authored one of the foundational texts on government contract law, "Government Contracts in a Nutshell," which provides accessible explanations of procurement rules and their evolution.

**RAND Corporation** has published several studies on defense acquisition and source selection methods, including analyses of major defense acquisition programs and the factors that contribute to cost growth and schedule delays. Their 2015 and 2017 reports on defense service contracting informed our literature review.

**Naval Postgraduate School** researchers have conducted numerous studies on military procurement practices, including analyses of LPTA use in defense contracting. Their thesis program has produced dozens of relevant studies on source selection outcomes.

### International Procurement

**Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD).** The OECD's Public Governance and Territorial Development Directorate publishes extensive guidance and comparative analyses of public procurement practices across member countries. Their "Recommendation of the Council on Public Procurement" (2015) provides the international benchmark for procurement reform.

**European Commission.** The EU's 2014 procurement directives and accompanying guidance documents describe the EU's approach to "most economically advantageous tender" (MEAT) evaluation and the rationale for discouraging lowest-price-only awards.

### Statistical Methods

**Paul R. Rosenbaum and Donald B. Rubin** developed propensity score matching in their seminal 1983 paper "The Central Role of the Propensity Score in Observational Studies for Causal Effects." This method is now widely used in medical research, program evaluation, and social science to make fair comparisons in observational studies.

**Joshua D. Angrist and Jorn-Steffen Pischke** wrote "Mostly Harmless Econometrics" (2009), which provides accessible explanations of the regression and matching methods used in this study. Their work has made advanced econometric methods more accessible to applied researchers.

### Industry Perspectives

**Professional Services Council (PSC).** The PSC, which represents government contractors, has published surveys and reports documenting industry concerns about LPTA use, including the argument that LPTA drives away capable firms and reduces competition.

**National Contract Management Association (NCMA).** NCMA publishes research and training materials on contract management practices, including studies of modification patterns and post-award management effectiveness.

## Where to Find the Data

All of the data used in this study is publicly available and free to access:

**USAspending.gov** -- The primary data source. You can search for individual contracts, download bulk data files, or use the site's API (application programming interface) to extract specific datasets. The site includes a user guide and data dictionary that explain each field.

**FPDS.gov** -- The Federal Procurement Data System provides additional detail on contract characteristics. Registration is required but free.

**SAM.gov** -- The System for Award Management includes information about registered government contractors, including their size, location, and socioeconomic status.

**GAO.gov** -- The Government Accountability Office's website provides free access to all of its reports on federal procurement, including bid protest decisions and performance audits.

**Acquisition.gov** -- The government's acquisition gateway, which provides access to the Federal Acquisition Regulation, agency supplements, and policy guidance.

## Glossary of Key Terms

**Acquisition workforce:** The government employees who plan, award, and manage federal contracts. This includes contracting officers, contract specialists, program managers, and technical evaluators.

**Best value (tradeoff):** An evaluation method in which the government considers multiple factors -- technical approach, past performance, management, and price -- and selects the proposal that offers the best overall value. A higher-priced proposal can win if its non-price factors are sufficiently superior.

**Coefficient:** In statistics, a number that estimates the size and direction of the relationship between two variables. A coefficient of 0.5, for example, means that a one-unit increase in one variable is associated with a 0.5-unit increase in another, holding other factors constant.

**Competitive contract:** A contract awarded through a process in which multiple companies were invited to submit proposals or bids. Contrasted with sole-source contracts, which are awarded to a single company without competition.

**Confidence interval:** A range of values that is likely to contain the true value of a statistical estimate. A 95 percent confidence interval means that if we repeated the study many times, 95 percent of the intervals would contain the true value.

**Contract modification:** A formal change to the terms of an existing contract. Modifications can change the scope of work, the price, the schedule, or administrative details. They are a normal part of contract management but can also indicate problems.

**Cost growth:** The increase in a contract's cost from the original award amount to the current obligated amount, usually expressed as a percentage. Also called "cost overrun" when the growth is unplanned.

**Cost-reimbursement contract:** A contract type in which the government pays the contractor's actual costs plus a fee. The government bears more cost risk than in a fixed-price contract.

**FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation):** The comprehensive set of rules governing how the federal government buys goods and services. The FAR is codified in Title 48 of the Code of Federal Regulations.

**Firm-fixed-price contract:** A contract type in which the price is established at the time of award and does not change (except through modifications). The contractor bears the risk of cost overruns.

**Fiscal year (FY):** The government's annual budget period, which runs from October 1 through September 30. FY2024 ran from October 1, 2023, through September 30, 2024.

**Interaction effect:** In statistics, a situation where the effect of one variable depends on the level of another variable. In this study, the effect of evaluation method on cost growth depends on contract size.

**Logistic regression:** A statistical method used to model the probability of a binary outcome (yes/no, success/failure). Used in this study to analyze whether evaluation method affects the odds of a single-bid outcome.

**LPTA (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable):** An evaluation method in which the government sets minimum technical requirements and awards the contract to the lowest-priced proposal that meets those requirements. Technical merit beyond the minimum threshold is not considered.

**Negative binomial regression:** A statistical method used to model count data (0, 1, 2, 3, ...) that is overdispersed (has more variation than a simple Poisson distribution would predict). Used in this study to analyze modification counts.

**Null result:** A research finding that shows no statistically significant relationship between the variables being studied. A null result does not mean the relationship does not exist; it means the data does not provide sufficient evidence to conclude that it does.

**Observational study:** A study in which the researcher observes and analyzes existing data without manipulating or controlling the conditions. Contrasted with experimental studies, in which the researcher randomly assigns treatments.

**Odds ratio:** In logistic regression, the ratio of the odds of an outcome occurring in one group compared to another. An odds ratio of 1.0 means equal odds. Below 1.0 means lower odds. Above 1.0 means higher odds.

**OLS (Ordinary Least Squares):** A statistical method that estimates the relationship between variables by minimizing the sum of squared differences between observed and predicted values. The most common type of linear regression.

**Obligation:** A legally binding commitment by the government to pay for goods or services. When a contract is awarded, the government "obligates" funds to cover the anticipated cost.

**p-value:** The probability of observing a result as extreme as the one found (or more extreme), assuming that there is actually no real effect. A p-value below 0.05 is conventionally considered statistically significant, meaning the result is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone.

**Period of performance:** The time period during which a contractor is expected to complete the work specified in the contract.

**Propensity score:** The estimated probability that a unit (in our case, a contract) would receive the treatment (in our case, best-value evaluation) based on its observed characteristics. Used in propensity score matching to create comparable groups.

**Propensity score matching (PSM):** A statistical technique that matches units from a treatment group with similar units from a control group based on their propensity scores. Designed to reduce selection bias in observational studies by creating more comparable comparison groups.

**Robustness check:** An analysis that tests whether a finding holds up under different assumptions, methods, or subsamples. Robust findings survive multiple checks and are considered more reliable.

**Selection bias:** A distortion in research results that occurs when the groups being compared are systematically different in ways that affect the outcome. In procurement research, selection bias arises because the choice of evaluation method is related to contract characteristics.

**Single-offer outcome:** A competitive solicitation in which only one company submits a proposal. Despite being technically "competitive" (the solicitation was open to multiple bidders), the government receives no comparison and no competitive leverage.

**Sole-source contract:** A contract awarded to a specific company without competition, usually because only one company can perform the work or because of an urgent need. These contracts were excluded from our study.

**Source selection:** The process by which the government evaluates proposals and selects a contractor. The "source selection method" refers to the criteria and approach used -- either LPTA or best value (or variations).

**Standardized bias:** A measure used to assess the quality of propensity score matching. It quantifies the difference between the treatment and control groups on each characteristic, standardized by the standard deviation. Lower standardized bias indicates better balance.

**Statistical significance:** A determination that a research finding is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone. By convention, a result is considered statistically significant when the p-value is below 0.05 (5 percent).

**Transaction costs:** Costs associated with making an economic exchange beyond the price of the goods or services themselves. Includes the costs of searching for contractors, negotiating terms, writing contracts, monitoring performance, and enforcing agreements.

**USAspending.gov:** A publicly accessible website maintained by the U.S. Department of the Treasury that provides comprehensive data on all federal spending, including contracts, grants, loans, and direct payments. The primary data source for this study.
