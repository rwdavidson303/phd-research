# The Vendor Journey: Cost and Complexity of Federal Contracting

## Overview

This document maps the full journey a business must navigate to compete for and win a federal contract. It covers three phases: qualification (becoming eligible to bid), bidding (responding to a single RFP), and the end-to-end lifecycle from market entry through contract closeout.

The data here supports a central argument of the dissertation: when barriers to entry are this high, competition shrinks, incumbency advantage grows, and lowest-price procurement methods may not maximize public value.

---

## Phase 1: The Qualification Gauntlet (15 Steps)

Everything a business must do before it can submit its first bid.

| Step | Action | Estimated Cost | Notes |
|------|--------|---------------|-------|
| 1 | Form legal business entity (LLC, S-Corp, C-Corp) | $100–$800 | State registration, operating agreements, governance |
| 2 | Get EIN from IRS | Free | Employer Identification Number — federal tax ID |
| 3 | Open business bank account | Free–$25/mo | Required for ACH payments from government |
| 4 | Identify NAICS codes | Free | 6,000+ codes; determines contract eligibility and size standard |
| 5 | Obtain UEI number | Free | Unique Entity Identifier — replaced DUNS in 2022 |
| 6 | Register on SAM.gov | Free (30–60 min) | Mandatory gateway; must be renewed annually |
| 7 | Determine SBA size standard | Free | Revenue/employee thresholds per NAICS code |
| 8 | DCAA-compliant accounting system | $4,200–$200,000+/yr | Specialized cost-accounting; most commercial software doesn't qualify |
| 9 | Socioeconomic certifications (8(a), HUBZone, WOSB, SDVOSB) | Free to apply | Heavy documentation; months to process; ongoing compliance |
| 10 | CMMC certification (DoD contracts) | $5,000–$300,000 | Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification; Level 2+ requires third-party assessment |
| 11 | Surety bonding (construction) | 1–5% of contract value | Bid, performance, and payment bonds; required over $150K |
| 12 | Required insurance (GL, E&O, workers' comp, cyber) | $2,000–$10,000+/yr | Coverage requirements vary by contract type and agency |
| 13 | Capability statement | $500–$5,000 | 1–2 page marketing document for government buyers |
| 14 | Build past performance record | Opportunity cost: years | The Catch-22 — need contracts to build record, need record to win contracts |
| 15 | Claim SBS profile on SAM.gov | Free | Small Business Search — where COs and primes find small businesses |

**Estimated Qualification Cost: $6,800 – $516,000+**

**Timeline: 3–12 months**

### Key Observations

- Steps 1–7 are administrative but collectively take weeks of effort
- Step 8 (DCAA accounting) is the single biggest recurring cost for small firms
- Step 10 (CMMC) is a growing barrier — many small IT firms cannot afford compliance
- Step 14 (past performance) is the structural barrier that most entrenches incumbents
- Many of these costs are sunk whether or not the firm ever wins a contract

## Phase 2: The Bidding Maze (13 Steps)

What it takes to respond to a single Request for Proposal.

| Step | Action | Estimated Cost | Notes |
|------|--------|---------------|-------|
| 1 | Monitor SAM.gov daily | Free (time-intensive) | Thousands of opportunities posted weekly |
| 2 | Review the RFP (50–200+ pages) | Free | Multi-day effort; most opportunities aren't worth pursuing |
| 3 | Attend industry day | $500–$5,000 | Pre-solicitation conference; often requires travel |
| 4 | Submit questions during Q&A period | Free | Formal process; answers published to all bidders |
| 5 | Prepare technical proposal | $15,000–$150,000+ | Technical approach, management plan, staffing, transition; weeks of work |
| 6 | Prepare cost/price proposal | $5,000–$50,000 | Labor rates, indirect rates, fee structures; must withstand audit |
| 7 | Assemble past performance volume | $2,000–$10,000 | Project descriptions, CPARS ratings, contactable references |
| 8 | Submit by deadline | Free | Late = disqualified, no exceptions; systems sometimes crash on deadline day |
| 9 | Government evaluation | Waiting (weeks–months) | Evaluation boards score against stated criteria |
| 10 | Discussions/negotiations (best-value only) | Additional weeks | LPTA procurements typically skip this phase |
| 11 | Award decision | — | Losers receive brief (often vague) debriefing |
| 12 | Potential bid protest (GAO or COFC) | $50,000–$250,000 legal | Delays program 100+ days; outcomes uncertain |
| 13 | Post-award conference and kickoff | 1–4 weeks | Transition, security clearances, badging, IT access |

**Estimated Cost Per Bid: $15,000 – $250,000+**

### Key Observations

- Steps 5–7 represent 90%+ of the direct cost — proposal writing is the core expense
- Win rates for new entrants are often below 20%
- At a 20% win rate, a firm may invest $75K–$1.25M across 5+ bids before winning once
- Step 10 (discussions) is the key differentiator between best-value and LPTA procurements
- Step 12 (bid protest) adds systemic cost and delay regardless of whether it's filed
- The entire investment is at risk from a single late submission (Step 8)

## Phase 3: The Full Lifecycle (9 Phases)

End-to-end pipeline from market entry through contract closeout.

| Phase | Description | Cumulative Cost Contribution |
|-------|-------------|------------------------------|
| 1. Market Entry | Form entity, obtain EIN, open accounts | $100–$800 |
| 2. Qualification | SAM.gov, DCAA, CMMC, certifications, insurance | $6K–$515K |
| 3. Opportunity Discovery | Daily SAM.gov monitoring, market research | Ongoing labor cost |
| 4. Capture | Industry days, relationship building, teaming | $500–$5K per opportunity |
| 5. Proposal | Technical, cost, and past performance volumes | $22K–$210K per bid |
| 6. Evaluation | Government review period | Waiting (no revenue) |
| 7. Award | Decision, debriefing, potential protest | $0–$250K if protested |
| 8. Performance | Contract execution | Revenue finally begins |
| 9. Closeout | Final deliverables, audits, CPARS evaluation | Administrative cost |

**Cumulative Investment Before Revenue: $30K – $1.1M+**

## Connection to Dissertation Research

### The Competition Problem

When the barrier to entry is this high:
- **Fewer firms compete** — reducing innovation and price pressure
- **Incumbents are entrenched** — past performance requirements favor firms already in the system
- **New entrants are priced out** — particularly small and disadvantaged businesses
- **The competitive pool narrows** — undermining the premise that competition drives value

### The LPTA vs. Best-Value Question

This cost structure directly informs the dissertation's central research question:

> When the process is this expensive and complex, does choosing the lowest-price bidder actually maximize public value? Or does it simply reward the firms most willing to absorb sunk costs — while shutting out potentially superior competitors who can't afford to play?

**LPTA (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable)** compresses all evaluation into a single dimension: who bid lowest? In a system with $30K–$1.1M+ entry costs, this approach:
- Rewards firms that can absorb losses on bids they don't win
- Incentivizes cutting corners to minimize price
- Ignores technical innovation, management quality, and past performance nuance
- Disproportionately benefits large incumbents with deep pockets

**Best-value tradeoff procurement** provides a mechanism to weigh technical merit, past performance, and innovation alongside price. This approach:
- Gives evaluators a way to see past the price tag
- Rewards quality and innovation, not just cost-cutting
- Can justify paying more when the expected outcome is better
- Creates incentives for firms to invest in capability, not just low bids

### Implications for Policy

The vendor journey data supports several policy-relevant conclusions:

1. **Barrier reduction** — Simplifying qualification requirements could expand the competitive base
2. **Evaluation method matters** — The choice between LPTA and best-value has downstream effects on who can afford to compete
3. **Past performance requirements create structural incumbency** — Alternative evaluation approaches may be needed for new market entrants
4. **Protest costs are systemic** — Even the possibility of protest adds cost to every procurement, not just protested ones
5. **Small business programs are necessary but insufficient** — Set-asides help access, but don't address the fundamental cost of competing
