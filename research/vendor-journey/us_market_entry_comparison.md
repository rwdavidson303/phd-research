# US Vendor Market Entry Comparison

## Barriers and Access for US Companies Across 12 International Procurement Markets

**Purpose:** This document compares what a US company faces when trying to win government contracts in each of the 12 international procurement systems studied. It identifies which markets are most accessible, which pose the highest barriers, and what factors drive the difference. This analysis supports the dissertation's examination of how procurement system design affects competition and value outcomes.

### Key Finding

Several international markets are **cheaper to enter from the US than the US domestic market itself.** Georgia ($29,500), Brazil ($17,760–$57,940), and Chile ($26,500–$149,500) all have lower maximum entry costs than the US domestic system. Singapore and the UK are comparable. This finding suggests that the US federal procurement system's barriers are not just high by international standards — they may be the highest in the world.

## Connection to Dissertation Research

### Five Key Findings for the Dissertation

1. **The US domestic market is among the most expensive to compete in — from any direction.** A US company faces $30K–$1.1M+ to compete domestically. Several international markets are cheaper to enter from abroad than the US market is for domestic firms. This is a structural indictment of US procurement system complexity.

2. **Language is the dominant non-regulatory barrier.** Across all 12 markets, the single biggest cost differentiator for US vendors is whether bids can be submitted in English. The five English-speaking markets (UK, Australia, Canada, NZ, Singapore) have 1.5–4x foreign-entry premiums; non-English markets have 3–15x premiums. This variable should be controlled for in cross-country procurement research.

3. **Treaty access matters but isn't sufficient.** WTO GPA and bilateral FTAs provide formal non-discrimination above thresholds, but they don't address language, local presence, or relationship-based evaluation preferences. Chile (FTA, observer only on GPA) is more accessible in practice than the EU (full GPA member) because of lower systemic barriers.

4. **Evaluation method interacts with foreign entry barriers.** In LPTA/lowest-price systems (Brazil's pregao, Georgia's traditional approach), foreign vendors compete purely on price — and often lose to lower-cost local firms. In best-value systems (UK, NZ, Australia), foreign vendors can differentiate on quality, innovation, and capability — but must also demonstrate local value contribution. Neither method is inherently better for foreign access; the barrier structure determines which is more hospitable.

5. **Domestic preference is universal but varies in mechanism.** Every country studied has some form of domestic preference — whether explicit (Brazil's 10–20% margin), structural (Australia's IPP, NZ's Rule 8), or informal (relationship-based evaluation). The US uses set-asides for small/disadvantaged businesses. The mechanism matters: explicit margins are transparent and predictable; structural preferences embedded in evaluation criteria are harder to quantify and challenge.

### The Overarching Implication

> The cross-country evidence shows that procurement system design — digital infrastructure, registration complexity, evaluation methodology, and barrier structure — collectively determines who competes and what "value" means. The US is not unique in having barriers, but it is unique in the magnitude of those barriers relative to its own stated goals of open competition and best value. This research provides an empirical foundation for arguing that meaningful procurement reform must address system design, not just evaluation method.
