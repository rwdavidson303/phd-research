# The Vendor Journey: US Company Entering Georgian Government Procurement

## Overview

This document maps the full journey a US-based company must navigate to compete for and win a Georgian government contract through the Georgian Electronic Government Procurement (Ge-GP) system. Georgia presents a paradox for US vendors: it is one of the world's most business-friendly countries (ranked 7th globally by World Bank's Doing Business), with extremely low registration costs, a flat 15% corporate tax rate, and a fully electronic procurement system designed for remote participation from anywhere -- yet the small market size, Georgian language barrier, and limited contract values make it a niche opportunity rather than a major revenue target.

The good news: Georgia's Ge-GP system was explicitly designed for remote participation. A US company can theoretically register and bid without ever visiting Tbilisi. The bidding documents are free, the participation fee is GEL 50 (about USD 19), and the entire process is electronic. The US and Georgia have a Bilateral Investment Treaty (signed 1994), and Georgia is negotiating accession to the WTO Government Procurement Agreement (GPA), which would formalize reciprocal procurement access. Georgia has also signed a Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Area (DCFTA) with the EU and maintains one of the most liberal foreign investment regimes in the world.

The bad news: Georgia's total annual public procurement is approximately GEL 2.7 billion (~USD 1 billion) -- smaller than many single US federal contracts. The working language is Georgian (a unique script and language unrelated to any major language family). The evaluation system has historically been lowest-price dominant through reverse auction, compressing margins. And effective March 2026, Georgia is introducing new work permit requirements for foreign nationals, ending its historically open labor policy.

**Exchange rate used throughout:** 1 GEL = approximately 0.37 USD; 1 USD = approximately 2.70 GEL (February 2026).

**Estimated Qualification Cost: GEL 40,000 - 200,000 (USD 14,800 - 74,000)**

**Timeline: 2-6 weeks for basic registration; 2-4 months for full operational readiness**

### Key Observations

- **Registration is remarkably cheap and fast** -- Georgia's NAPR registration (GEL 100-400) is the cheapest and fastest in this research series. Compare to ASIC in Australia ($1,500-$3,000 AUD) or Companies House in the UK (GBP 12-30 but weeks of compliance setup)
- **The Georgian language barrier is the single biggest non-financial obstacle** -- Georgian is a Kartvelian language with a unique script, spoken by only ~4 million people. Machine translation is improving but unreliable for legal/technical documents. This barrier is more severe than Korean or even Estonian
- **The Estonian tax model is highly attractive** -- Profits reinvested in Georgia are tax-free. Only distributions to the US parent are taxed at 15% (reduced further by treaty). This is among the most favorable corporate tax regimes globally
- **No DCAA, no CMMC, no ITAR** -- Georgian procurement has none of the specialized compliance frameworks that add $50,000-$500,000+ to US federal contracting entry costs
- **The work permit change (March 2026) is new and significant** -- Georgia's historically open immigration policy was a major attraction. The new requirements add cost and bureaucracy, though the regime remains liberal by global standards
- **US citizens enter visa-free for 1 year** -- Extraordinary by global standards. Even after the work permit changes, entry itself remains trivial

## Phase 2: The Bidding Process

What it takes for a US company to respond to a single tender through Ge-GP.

### WTO GPA and Treaty Access

Georgia is currently negotiating accession to the WTO Government Procurement Agreement (GPA). The US is a GPA signatory. Once Georgia completes accession, US vendors will have formal treaty-based rights to bid on Georgian government contracts above GPA thresholds. Currently, access is based on Georgia's domestic procurement law, which does not discriminate against foreign bidders for tenders above the international notification threshold (GEL 2 million for goods/services, GEL 4 million for works).

The US-Georgia Bilateral Investment Treaty (1994) provides investment protection (national treatment, protection against expropriation, free transfer of funds) but does not specifically guarantee procurement market access.

### Bidding Steps

| Step | Action | Estimated Cost (GEL) | Estimated Cost (USD) | Notes |
|------|--------|---------------------|---------------------|-------|
| 1 | Monitor Ge-GP portal for opportunities | Free (time + translation) | Free | Portal at procurement.gov.ge; interface is in Georgian with some English-language features. Open data portal provides structured data. Third-party services (TendersInfo, GlobalTenders) aggregate Georgian tenders in English for a fee |
| 2 | Translate and review tender documentation | 2,000-15,000 per tender | $740-$5,550 | Full translation of tender documents from Georgian. Technical specifications, evaluation criteria, legal terms. Budget $0.08-$0.15/word; typical tender package 5,000-20,000 words |
| 3 | Submit clarification requests | 500-2,000 (translation) | $185-$740 | Questions must be submitted in Georgian through the portal. Responses published to all bidders. Translation costs both ways |
| 4 | Prepare technical proposal (in Georgian) | 5,000-30,000 | $1,850-$11,100 | Technical response must address specifications in Georgian. Requires either bilingual staff or professional translation of US-prepared proposal. Quality of translation matters for evaluation |
| 5 | Prepare pricing/financial proposal | Included in Step 4 | -- | Pricing through reverse auction mechanism for electronic tenders. Must factor in import logistics, exchange rate risk, local delivery costs |
| 6 | Obtain bid security (bank guarantee) | 1,000-50,000 (bank fees) | $370-$18,500 | Issued by Georgian bank. Typically 2-5% of estimated contract value. US bank guarantees may not be accepted; Georgian banking relationship required |
| 7 | Pay tender participation fee | 50 / 5,000 | $19 / $1,850 | GEL 50 for standard/simplified electronic tenders; GEL 5,000 for consolidated tenders |
| 8 | Submit bid electronically | Free | Free | Through Ge-GP portal using Georgian digital signature. System is fully electronic; remote submission from US possible |
| 9 | Participate in electronic reverse auction | Free (time cost) | Free | Real-time price competition. Time zone difference (Tbilisi is UTC+4, 9 hours ahead of US Eastern) must be managed. Auction participation requires real-time attention |
| 10 | Evaluation, award, and contract signing | Free | Free | All conducted electronically through the portal |
| 11 | Challenge/complaint (if needed) | 5,000-20,000 | $1,850-$7,400 | Dispute Resolution Board complaint in Georgian. Legal representation advisable. 10 working day review timeline |

**Estimated Cost Per Bid: GEL 8,550 - 120,000 (USD 3,165 - 44,400)**

**Typical mid-range bid cost for US company: GEL 15,000-40,000 (USD 5,550-14,800)**

**Win rate for foreign bidders: ~10-20% (estimated; foreign participation is low due to small market size)**

### Additional Costs Unique to Foreign Entry

| Cost Category | Estimated Annual Cost (USD) | Notes |
|---------------|---------------------------|-------|
| Translation services (ongoing) | $5,000-$20,000 | Tender documents, correspondence, legal filings, contract amendments |
| Local representative/agent | $2,200-$13,300 | Georgian-speaking professional to manage portal, banking, administration |
| Currency exchange and hedging | $1,000-$5,000 | GEL/USD exchange rate volatility; GEL has been relatively stable (0.36-0.37 USD range in 2025) but hedging instruments are limited |
| International travel (Tbilisi) | $3,000-$8,000 per trip | Direct flights from US East Coast ~12-15 hours with connection. Hotels in Tbilisi $60-$150/night. 1-2 trips per year for relationship building recommended |
| Third-party tender monitoring | $1,000-$5,000 | English-language aggregator services for Georgian government tenders |
| US Commerce Department Advocacy Center | Free | US companies bidding on foreign government tenders can request US government advocacy support |

### Key Observations

- **Translation costs dominate the bid budget** -- For a US company, translation adds $740-$5,550 per tender that a Georgian company does not incur. Over multiple bids, this accumulates significantly
- **Remote bidding is genuinely possible** -- Unlike many countries, Georgia's fully electronic system means a US company can bid from Washington, DC without physical presence. This is a rare advantage
- **The reverse auction favors lowest-cost producers** -- US companies typically have higher cost structures than Georgian or regional competitors. The price-driven auction mechanism disadvantages higher-cost foreign bidders
- **World Bank-financed projects are the sweet spot** -- The World Bank approved Ge-GP for Bank-financed projects (civil works below $10M, goods below $1M). These projects often have English-language documentation and international competitive bidding procedures, reducing the language barrier
- **The US Advocacy Center is an underused resource** -- The Commerce Department's International Trade Administration provides free advocacy for US companies bidding on foreign government contracts
- **Time zone management matters** -- Tbilisi (UTC+4) is 9 hours ahead of Eastern Time. Reverse auctions happening during Georgian business hours require early-morning US attention

### Full Lifecycle Key Observations

- **Entry cost of ~$29,500 is the lowest in this research series for a US company entering a foreign market** -- Compare to Australia (~AUD 90,000-530,000), UK (~GBP 50,000-300,000), South Korea (~$100,000-400,000)
- **But the market is proportionally tiny** -- A $370,000 contract is mid-range for Georgia but would be a micro-purchase in US federal procurement. The return on investment must be evaluated relative to market size
- **Language costs consume 20-30% of the bid investment** -- This is the defining cost penalty for English-speaking companies in Georgia
- **The "when" matters: World Bank and international donor projects** -- The most accessible opportunities for US companies are international donor-financed projects (World Bank, EBRD, ADB, USAID) that use English and international procurement procedures through Ge-GP
- **Georgia as a regional hub** -- US companies already operating in the Caucasus/Black Sea region (Azerbaijan, Armenia, Turkey) may find Georgia a natural extension rather than a standalone market entry

## Connection to Dissertation Research

### LPTA vs. Best-Value: The Georgian Experiment

Georgia's procurement evolution offers unique insights for the dissertation on several dimensions:

**1. Lowest-Price as Anti-Corruption Strategy**

Georgia adopted the reverse auction/lowest-price model specifically to combat endemic corruption. When 97% of the population perceives procurement as corrupt, removing human judgment from evaluation is a rational policy choice. The system treats all technically qualified bidders identically and lets the algorithm select the lowest price -- a pure LPTA approach. The result: corruption perception dropped to 3%. This is the strongest empirical validation of LPTA's anti-corruption properties in the research literature.

**2. The Maturity Transition: When to Move Beyond Price**

The 2023 law's introduction of "price/quality best proportion" evaluation (effective 2025) signals that Georgia has reached the maturity point where pure lowest-price creates more problems than it solves. The quality degradation, race-to-the-bottom pricing, and inability to evaluate innovation or lifecycle costs mirror exactly the critiques of LPTA in the US federal context. Georgia is effectively recapitulating the US evolution from sealed-bid low-price to best-value trade-off -- but compressed into a decade rather than half a century.

**3. Foreign Vendor Access: The Promise vs. Reality**

Despite Georgia's open system and zero discrimination against foreign bidders, actual foreign participation is extremely low. This reveals that formal access rules are insufficient -- language barriers, small contract values, cultural familiarity, and geographic proximity matter more than legal frameworks. This finding has implications for the WTO GPA's effectiveness: even when procurement markets are legally open, practical barriers limit foreign competition.

**4. The Transparency-Competition Paradox**

Georgia has arguably the world's most transparent procurement system (real-time public observation, open data since 2018), yet Transparency International documents that 45% of procurement bypasses the competitive system entirely. This challenges the assumption that transparency alone drives competition and value. The 55% that goes through the system is extremely competitive; the 45% that bypasses it is essentially sole-source.

**5. Small Market, Big Lessons**

Georgia's annual procurement (~$1 billion) is approximately 0.14% of US federal procurement (~$700 billion). Yet the system has attracted more international study and awards than procurement systems of much larger economies. The lessons for the dissertation:
- Radical simplification works: GEL 50 fee, free documents, all-electronic, same-week registration
- Technology can replace institutional trust: the algorithm substitutes for the integrity that the old system lacked
- Lowest-price is a phase, not an endpoint: successful anti-corruption systems eventually need to evolve toward value optimization
- Transparency without comprehensiveness is incomplete: the open data portal is excellent for the 55% on-system, irrelevant for the 45% off-system
