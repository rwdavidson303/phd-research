### Research Interests

The United States federal government awards approximately $755 billion annually in contracts, yet conservative estimates from World Bank and GAO analyses suggest $150 to $200 billion is lost each year to procurement inefficiency. Despite this, a striking vacuum exists in the empirical literature: how should the government strategically evaluate competing proposals to maximize public value?

When should the world's largest buyer choose lowest price over approaches that weigh technical merit, past performance, and long-term value? This is a governance design question rooted in Transaction Cost Economics, and the answer depends on the transaction itself: its complexity, uncertainty, and specificity. Yet remarkably, only one peer-reviewed empirical study has directly compared outcomes across these evaluation methods. Landale, Rendon, and Hawkins (2017) examined just 124 Department of Defense contracts from a single service branch. That represents the entire evidence base behind a three-quarter-trillion-dollar annual decision.

In my pre-research exploration of this space, I have identified a comprehensive federal procurement dataset (Omari et al., 2025, Nature Scientific Data) containing millions of verified contract transactions across all federal agencies from 2017 to 2023. Combined with a natural policy experiment created by Congress's 2017 restriction on lowest-price procurement methods through Section 813 of the National Defense Authorization Act, this dataset presents an unprecedented opportunity for rigorous analysis never attempted at this scale.

My secondary research interest involves international comparison: countries like South Korea and Georgia have built procurement systems that are faster, more accessible to vendors, and dramatically more competitive. South Korea's KONEPS platform cut bidding time by 93 percent and serves over 430,000 suppliers; Georgia's digital system increased average bidders per tender from two to five while reducing corruption from over 90 percent to single digits. These systems demonstrate that lowering barriers, streamlining processes, and digitizing end-to-end can simultaneously reduce waste, increase competition, and attract creative solutions. Understanding how to apply these lessons to the US federal system is central to my research agenda.

Professor Matt Josefy's research on corporate governance, strategic decision-making, and computational approaches to large institutional datasets resonates strongly with my intended methodology. Professor Stephanie Lu Wang's expertise in international institutional complexity offers a natural framework for my comparative analysis, and both align with what this research ultimately demands: replacing ideology-driven procurement policy with empirical evidence. I look forward to faculty mentorship that will refine both the direction and methodology of this effort.

My career has been built on inaugurals: the first manufacturing plant, the first successful health insurance exchange platform, the first nationwide distribution network for a health insurance startup. Joining the inaugural cohort of the Kelley EDBA to conduct the first large-scale empirical study of federal procurement evaluation strategy would be the logical culmination of that pattern, and the next first I intend to build.
