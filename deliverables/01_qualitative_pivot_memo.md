## 1. Purpose

This memorandum proposes a revision to my dissertation research design. Rather than pursuing the originally planned sequential mixed-methods study combining quantitative archival analysis with qualitative practitioner inquiry, I propose to focus the dissertation exclusively on a **qualitative multiple-case study** of source selection decision-making in federal procurement.

This pivot is not a retreat from empirical rigor. It is motivated by a completed quantitative pilot study whose findings -- primarily null main effects and one significant moderation result -- demonstrate that the mechanisms underlying source selection outcomes are more nuanced than outcome-level archival data can capture. A qualitative case study is the appropriate next step to investigate the "why" and "how" questions that the quantitative evidence has surfaced.

---

## 2. Summary of Completed Quantitative Work (Pilot Study)

Prior to entering the program, I conducted a large-scale quasi-experimental archival analysis of federal contract outcomes as a function of source selection method. The study parameters were:

- **Sample:** 15,477 competitive service and IT contract awards above $250,000 from USAspending.gov (FY2024 Q2)
- **Comparison:** Quality-Evaluating/Tradeoff design (n = 1,379) vs. Negotiated Fixed-Price (n = 2,490)
- **Methods:** OLS regression, negative binomial GLM, logistic regression, and propensity score matching (1,093 matched pairs)
- **Theoretical grounding:** Transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985), public value theory (Moore, 1995), auction theory (Che, 1993), and incomplete contract theory (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001)

### Key Findings

| Hypothesis | Outcome Variable | Result | p-value |
|-----------|-----------------|--------|---------|
| H1: Cost Growth | % cost increase from initial award | **Not supported** -- null main effect | .377 |
| H2: Modification Intensity | Count of contract modifications | **Not supported** -- null | .449 |
| H6: Single-Bid Rates | Binary single-bid indicator | **Marginal** -- OR = 0.752 (~25% reduction) | .085 |
| H8: Complexity Moderation | Cost growth x award size interaction | **Supported** -- crossover pattern | .003 |
| H3, H4, H5, H7, H9, H10 | Various | **Not testable** with archival data | -- |

The null main effects were robust across all specifications, subsamples, and sensitivity analyses. The one clearly supported finding -- that award size moderates the source selection effect, with quality-evaluating design reducing cost growth for smaller procurements but not for larger ones -- points to the **context-dependent nature** of procurement design effects.

Critically, six of ten hypotheses were untestable with archival data because they require information that public procurement databases do not contain: practitioner decision rationale, solicitation quality, organizational culture, workforce competence, and contractor quality assessments (CPARS).

## 3. The Case for Qualitative Inquiry: From WHAT to WHY and HOW

The quantitative pilot study established **what** happens at the aggregate level: source selection method alone does not predict contract cost performance. But it cannot explain **why** the null findings occur or **how** contracting officers actually make and implement source selection decisions.

Five specific interpretations from the quantitative analysis demand qualitative investigation:

1. **The quality floor hypothesis:** Does the federal procurement system already screen for contractor quality through mechanisms other than source selection (responsibility determinations, past performance, security clearances), reducing the marginal contribution of evaluation method? Only practitioners can describe how these mechanisms interact in practice.

2. **The unobserved complexity confound:** Do agencies selecting tradeoff evaluation do so precisely because the procurement is inherently riskier, masking a genuine treatment effect? Understanding the decision calculus requires direct access to decision-makers, not outcome data.

3. **The scope evolution vs. contractor failure distinction:** Is cost growth in tradeoff contracts legitimate scope evolution rather than performance failure? Distinguishing between these requires case-level understanding of modification histories and their causes.

4. **The temporal snapshot limitation:** Does the tradeoff advantage emerge over longer time horizons than a single-quarter snapshot can capture? Longitudinal case studies can track contract trajectories from award to completion.

5. **The Kelman hypothesis:** Is the quality of the procurement process -- the skill, judgment, and effort of the acquisition workforce -- more consequential than the formal evaluation method? This is fundamentally a question about human behavior and organizational practice that only qualitative inquiry can address.

These are "how" and "why" questions within a real-world context -- precisely the domain where case study research excels (Yin, 2018).

## 4. Methodological Justification

### Sequential Explanatory Design

The pivot follows the logic of a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design (Creswell & Creswell, 2018), where quantitative findings inform the design of qualitative inquiry. The quantitative pilot revealed patterns (null main effects, significant moderation) that require qualitative explanation. This is not abandoning the quantitative work -- it is building on it.

### Methodological Fit

Edmondson and McManus (2007) argue that the choice of research method should match the maturity of the theory being tested. When quantitative tests of existing theory produce unexpected null results, the appropriate response is to "open the black box" through qualitative investigation that can refine or reconceptualize the theoretical mechanisms. The source selection literature is in precisely this intermediate state: the theory is well-developed but the empirical evidence is inconsistent, suggesting that the causal mechanisms are more complex than the theory specifies.

### Case Study as Method

Yin (2018) identifies the case study as the preferred method when: (a) the research questions are "how" and "why" questions, (b) the investigator has little control over events, and (c) the focus is on a contemporary phenomenon within a real-life context. Federal procurement source selection decisions satisfy all three criteria. A multiple-case design with 4-6 cases provides replication logic (Yin, 2018) and supports pattern matching across different procurement contexts -- enabling direct investigation of the complexity moderation finding (H8).

## 5. How the Quantitative Work Adds Value

The completed quantitative study is not wasted work. It contributes to the dissertation and my scholarly development in four ways:

1. **Empirical foundation for the qualitative study.** The null findings provide the specific empirical motivation for the case study research questions. Without the quantitative evidence, the qualitative study would lack the empirical grounding that distinguishes it from exploratory speculation.

2. **Sampling frame for case selection.** The 15,477-record dataset provides a systematic basis for selecting information-rich cases: procurements at different complexity levels, from different agencies, using different source selection methods, with different outcome profiles.

3. **Standalone publishable paper.** The quantitative manuscript will be submitted for journal publication independent of the dissertation, contributing to my scholarly record and to the empirical literature on source selection. Well-powered null results are increasingly valued in social science (Franco et al., 2014).

4. **Demonstrated methodological competence.** The completed analysis demonstrates proficiency in quasi-experimental design, propensity score matching, and multiple regression frameworks -- skills that strengthen my credibility as a researcher even though my dissertation will be qualitative.
